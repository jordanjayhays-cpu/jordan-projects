01

The 25 facts most likely to be tested

Dark-topped cards are Garland's material. Blue-topped cards are Allard's. Each professor writes 5 MCQs and 2 short answers.
If you only have an hour: read this list twice, then the six model answers in chapter 02, then take one practice exam.
  1. GarlandMalthus: population grows geometrically (2, 4, 8, 16), food arithmetically (2, 4, 6, 8). Two checks restore balance. Preventive checks lower births (late marriage, today's one-child policy). Positive checks raise deaths (war, famine, epidemics). He was right about the past and wrong about the future, because the Industrial Revolution broke the limits.
  2. GarlandDutch disease: a resource boom brings in dollars, so the local currency appreciates (in real terms). Other tradables (manufacturing and agriculture) lose competitiveness, imports get cheaper, unemployment rises and long-run growth falls. Named after Dutch gas. It does not make farm products more competitive.
  3. GarlandOvervalued peg (Garland's "below equilibrium," because the axis is local currency per dollar): cheaper external debt, cheaper imports (which fight inflation) and citizens "richer" abroad. The costs are falling exports, rising imports, a trade deficit and a reserve drain. Argentina 1 peso = $1, Brazil's Real Plan, Mexico in 1994.
  4. GarlandUndervalued peg: cheap exports, a trade surplus and growth. The costs are expensive imports and inflation, expensive external debt and poorer citizens. China at 8.28/$ (1995–2004), Japan at 360, the DM under Bretton Woods.
  5. GarlandDirty (managed) floating: the currency floats, but the central bank goes against the market. Against appreciation it buys dollars and sells local currency, which risks inflation. Against depreciation it sells reserves (Angola sold about $10bn in 2009; Brazil's "Lula effect"). It only lasts while reserves do.
  6. GarlandNominal vs. real: to keep competitiveness, the nominal rate must depreciate by the inflation differential. Example with 100% peso inflation and 0% US inflation, starting at 10/$: 20 means real unchanged; 15 means nominal depreciation but real appreciation (lost competitiveness); 25 means real depreciation.
  7. GarlandMundell: capital moves to higher interest rates at equal risk. Capital inflows appreciate the currency, so a trade deficit can persist, financed by capital inflows (the US; Mexico 1990–94).
  8. GarlandDual exchange rate: the favorable rate goes to manufactured exports and essential imports; the unfavorable rate to resource exporters and luxury imports. Its fatal flaw is corruption: insiders flip official dollars on the parallel market (Angola about 190% profit; Venezuela lost about 15% of GDP).
  9. GarlandResource curse: Dutch disease, volatility, corruption and the political curse (rents mean no need to tax, so no accountability), and an asset-depletion mentality. Angola has all of them. Norway escaped thanks to strong institutions before oil, plus a sovereign fund invested 100% abroad (sterilization) under a 4% Action Rule.
  10. GarlandPPP: values everything at US prices, so countries cheaper than the US look bigger. World GDP is $118T nominal vs. $211T at PPP. China: $19.5T vs. $41T (largest at PPP). India: $4T vs. $17T. To rank countries, use nominal GDP in a common currency.
  11. GarlandGNI vs. GDP vs. GNP: GDP is output inside the borders. GNP is by nationality (hard to measure). GNI is GDP minus income sent abroad plus income received (remittances), and is the World Bank standard. Openness = (X + M) / GDP: Germany about 100%, India before 1991 about 22%.
  12. GarlandMaastricht: deficit ≤ 3% of GDP, debt ≤ 60%, inflation ≤ 1.5 points and long-term rates ≤ 2 points above the best three, 2 years in the EMS band. Inside the euro the ECB runs monetary and exchange-rate policy, the EU runs trade policy, and the country keeps fiscal (within the Stability and Growth Pact) and structural policy.
  13. GarlandMexico 1994: Brady bonds (1990: haircut, longer maturity, lower rate, US Treasury backing) reopened markets. Inflows of hot money pushed up the peso and a trade deficit. Then Chiapas, Colosio and Fed hikes; reserves fell from $30bn to $5bn; tesobonos moved dollar risk onto the government. A December devaluation, a float, overshooting from 3 to 7, a $50bn rescue and the Tequila effect.
  14. AllardSurplus vs. deficit countries: surplus countries (Japan, China, Germany) produce more than they consume, have saving above investment, depend on foreign demand, lend abroad, see their currency tend to rise and are prone to deflation. Deficit countries (US, India, UK) are the mirror image.
  15. AllardJapan's current-account surplus is driven by savings (saving above investment), not a peg or low costs. The yen rose about 50% after the 1985 Plaza Accord, followed by a bubble, the lost decade and deflation (real growth above nominal). Abenomics' three arrows: fiscal, QE (Japan invented QE) and structural reform.
  16. AllardJapan's debt is about 250% of GDP, the world's highest, but it is in yen, about 90% held domestically and cheap to service. The risk is pensions as an ageing nation turns from saving to dissaving. Carry trade: borrow in yen, invest in USD/MXN/BRL; it unwinds as Japanese rates rise.
  17. AllardISI vs. EOI: ISI protects infant industry for domestic demand (Brazil, India, Latin America) and ends up inefficient, unable to export and with little FDI. EOI uses external demand, FDI and a weak currency (Japan, the Tigers, China). Early developers (UK, US) grew about 1% a year per capita on domestic demand.
  18. AllardIndia vs. China: India has a chronic current-account deficit (domestic demand, consumption about 60% of GDP, a depreciating rupee). China has a surplus (consumption about 40%, a yuan undervalued by 20–35%). China's population is shrinking and India's growing. China opened about 1978; India in 1991 after a balance-of-payments crisis (two weeks of import reserves) and IMF conditions.
  19. AllardHDI combines life expectancy, education and GDP per capita (PPP). Angola ranks about 101st on income but about 143rd on HDI; Norway is 1st. Development requires GDP per capita to grow faster than population.
  20. AllardEU vs. US: the EU has more people but lower GDP (about 68% of the US's) and lower per-capita income. It has higher spending, universal welfare and more equality (Gini about 0.30). The US has a means-tested safety net, not "no welfare." Allard's slide says EU debt risk "is similar to the United States." The US is closer to an optimal currency area (labor mobility plus federal transfers).
  21. AllardLevels of integration: free-trade area, then customs union (adds a common external tariff), then common market (adds free movement of people and capital), then economic union (adds a single currency). Europe unified for peace (a "Trojan horse"). Rodrik trilemma: hyperglobalization, sovereignty and democracy; you can have only two.
  22. AllardBrexit options from soft to hard: remain, Norway (single market), Turkey (customs union, about 2.2% GDP loss), Canada FTA (about 6.3%, the one chosen), no deal (about 9.1%). Vote 51.9 to 48.1 in 2016; left in 2021.
  23. AllardCountry risk is the probability that political and economic events hurt a firm's financial performance (Kobrin). Risk is quantifiable; uncertainty is not. Investors want revenue potential, a good operating environment, reasonable costs, reasonable financial conditions and market access.
  24. AllardWeights depend on the investment. Financial investor: interest rate, then exchange rate, then inflation. Plant for the local market: market size first. Export platform: production costs, then exchange rate, then trade agreements. ICRG = 50% political + 25% financial + 25% economic. Best phase to invest: early expansion or near the trough.
  25. AllardAfrica: 54 countries, GDP about $2tn (the size of Canada, probably about 30% understated). Growth often trails population growth (2.7%), so per-capita income stalls. Aid has a negative growth coefficient; FDI and trade work better. AfCFTA removes tariffs on 90% of goods.
02

The six short answers the professors named

From the sample-questions file: Malthus, HDI, resource curse, growth strategies, EU vs. US, investor indicators. Each model answer is about 120 words, the length of a 10-point paragraph.
The 10/10 pattern: define the term, explain the mechanism (A leads to B leads to C), give one course example, then add one limit or "so what." The exam tests concepts, not memorized names or numbers.

Malthus Garland · S1

Thomas Malthus, an 18th-century English clergyman and economist, argued that population grows geometrically (2, 4, 8, 16) while food production grows only arithmetically (2, 4, 6, 8). Population therefore tends to outrun subsistence, which is the Malthusian trap. Balance is restored by preventive checks that reduce births (late marriage, moral restraint, or in modern form China's one-child policy) and positive checks that raise deaths: war, famine and epidemics. He described pre-industrial history well, since per-capita income stayed near $400–600 for most of 2,000 years. He was wrong about the future because he could not foresee technological change, the Industrial Revolution or new farmland, and fertility is now falling in rich countries. Neo-Malthusians extend the idea to any scarce resource.

Human Development Index Allard · S7

The HDI is a UN composite measure of development beyond income. It combines health (life expectancy), education (literacy and enrollment, or schooling) and standard of living (GDP or GNI per capita at PPP). It shows whether income turns into well-being. Angola ranks about 101st on GDP per capita but about 143rd of 182 on HDI, because oil wealth is concentrated (Gini 0.59, 70% under $2 a day). Norway ranked 1st in 9 of 11 years. The US is rich but ranks lower, and falls about 45 places on the planetary-pressures-adjusted HDI. Real development needs GDP per capita to grow faster than population. Limits: it ignores inequality and the environment unless adjusted, and it depends on data quality.

The natural resource curse Garland · S3, S8, S9

The resource curse is the paradox that resource-rich countries often grow more slowly and govern worse than resource-poor ones. Its channels: (1) Dutch disease, where export dollars appreciate the real exchange rate and crowd out agriculture and manufacturing; (2) volatility, as GDP, the budget and the currency follow commodity prices; (3) corruption and the political curse, since rents remove the need to tax citizens, which weakens accountability and makes the state a prize; (4) an asset-depletion mentality. Angola shows every channel: real appreciation over 100%, oil about 60% of GDP but 0.2% of jobs, Sonangol as a slush fund, and the 2009 crash. Norway escaped through strong institutions before the discovery and a sovereign fund invested only abroad. Only volatility remained.

Growth strategies in the 20th century Allard · S7

Early industrializers (UK, US) grew slowly on domestic demand, at about 1% per capita a year. Later developers tried to jump-start growth. Import-substitution industrialization (ISI) protected infant industries with tariffs to supply domestic demand (Brazil from the 1930s, India after 1947, Latin America). It bred protected, inefficient firms that couldn't export, drew little FDI and enlarged the state (India's "Hindu rate" of about 3.5%). Export-oriented industrialization (EOI) used external demand (X − M), welcomed FDI and kept the currency undervalued (Japan, the Asian Tigers, China after Deng). EOI generally won. Debt and balance-of-payments crises (Latin America 1982, India 1991) pushed ISI countries to Washington Consensus liberalization. EOI's limits are dependence on foreign demand and backlash from partners (the China shocks).

EU vs. US Allard · S4, S11

The EU (27 members, about 450m people) is larger in population but has lower GDP and per-capita income (about $63–66k vs. $86k), mainly because productivity growth and R&D lag. Europe has universal welfare states, higher spending and taxes, and more equality (Gini about 0.30; middle class 69–80% vs. about 59% in the US). The US relies on a means-tested safety net (Medicare, Medicaid). EU unemployment is structurally higher (rigid labor markets, generous benefits) and Europe is ageing faster. Debt risk is broadly similar: large, and in their own currency. The key structural gap is that the US is closer to an optimal currency area, with labor mobility and federal transfers that absorb regional shocks. Eurozone members have lost monetary and exchange-rate policy, with little common fiscal policy, so they adjust through internal devaluation.

Investor risk and opportunity indicators Allard · S14

Country risk is the probability that political and economic events will hurt a firm's financial performance (Kobrin). Investors look for revenue potential (growth, market size, middle class), a good operating environment (low and stable inflation, predictable policy, flexible labor, institutions, low corruption, infrastructure), reasonable costs, reasonable financial conditions (rates, deficits and debt, reserves) and market access (a stable currency, trade agreements). Each is an opportunity when present and a risk when absent. Weights depend on the investment. A financial investor ranks interest rates, the exchange rate and inflation first. A plant for the local market ranks market size first. An export platform ranks production costs, the exchange rate and trade agreements first. The same indicator can cut both ways (high unemployment means unrest but available labor). Timing matters: invest in early expansion.

03

Garland's half: history, exchange rates, resources and crises

Sessions 1, 2, 3, 5 (likely), 8, 9 and 12. His decks: Intro, Notes on Exchange Rates, Dutch Disease II, Nominal and Real Exchange Rates. Everything else comes from the cases and class. Exchange rates run through all of it.

Intro: Malthus, divergence, GDP and PPP Session 1

  • Malthus: see chapter 02. Scarcity as a founding concept of economics traces back to him.
  • 2,000 years of history (Maddison Project): per-capita income was about $400–600 until about 1800. Western Europe took 500 years to go from $400 to $800; India recently doubled its income in about 13 years. Charts use a logarithmic scale.
  • Great Divergence: from the Industrial Revolution (about 1820s) came the "rise of the Western world." From the 1950s to the 1990s, 20% of the world's population produced 80% of its GDP. China and India were about 70% of world GDP around 1500, so they are "re-emerging" economies.
  • Great Convergence: the 1990s were the crucial decade (Berlin Wall 1989, USSR 1991, the "End of History"). The Washington Consensus spread (free trade, free capital, education and health), and reform waves hit India, Eastern Europe, Latin America and Africa together. The Asian Tigers (South Korea, Hong Kong, Singapore, Taiwan) copied Japan from the 1960s–70s. The world went from G7 to G20, and 5 of the top 10 economies at PPP are now emerging markets.
  • 2025 GDP (deck): US $30.8T, EU $21.2T, China $19.5T, Germany $5.1T, Japan $4.4T, UK $4.0T, India $4.0T, world $118.4T. At PPP: China $41.3T, US $30.8T, EU $29.6T, India $17.2T, world $211.2T. Population 2026: India 1,477m, China 1,413m, US 349m.
  • PPP and Atlas: PPP values output at US prices (China about 50% of US prices, India about 25%). The Atlas method smooths the exchange rate with a 3-year average. Indonesia is the one to watch.

Spain Session 2 · Garland case

  • 1975Franco dies. Suárez and King Juan Carlos lead the transition; parties legalized; 1978 Constitution.
  • 1981Coup attempt fails when the King refuses to back it. This marks the end of the transition.
  • 1982–96PSOE (González): social rights, decentralization, repeated peseta devaluations, unemployment above 20%.
  • 1986Joins the EEC: the end of isolation.
  • 1992–95EMS currency storms: four peseta devaluations; 1993 the worst recession since 1959.
  • 1996–99PP (Aznar): privatization and austerity. Deficit from 6.8% (1994) to 2.9%. Joins the euro in 1999.
  • 2004–07Budget surplus, but a current-account deficit of about 9% (2nd largest in the world) and a housing boom.
  • 2008–14Bust: unemployment 26%, debt above 100%, an EU bank rescue, internal devaluation.
  • 2020Covid: GDP down about 11% (tourism about 15% of GDP). Stability and Growth Pact suspended; NextGenEU (Spain about €140bn).
  • Competitive devaluation ("rob your neighbor"): cheaper exports and pricier imports help GDP in the short run. The costs are inflation, more expensive international transactions, citizens poorer abroad and no pressure to raise productivity. The euro ended it.
  • Symmetric vs. asymmetric shocks: symmetric shocks (pandemic, Great Recession) suit one ECB response. Asymmetric shocks (Greece; Detroit within the US) can't be targeted by one monetary policy. The US uses labor mobility and federal funds; Europe has almost no common fiscal funds.
  • "Austercide": the ECB cut rates (countercyclical) while the EU demanded austerity (procyclical) from Greece, which deepened the slump. The pandemic reversed this lesson.
  • Mundell's warning: without a common fiscal policy, deficits become the only remaining lever. North vs. south fight over eurobonds.

Brazil Session 3 · HBS case

  • Pax Britannica (1815–1914): free trade and comparative advantage: coffee and sugar out, manufactures in. Demand for both is inelastic, so bumper crops crash revenue, and producers destroyed crops to defend prices.
  • ISI from the 1920s–30s (Vargas): tariffs to protect infant industry. Labor benefits for registered workers fed a large informal sector.
  • "Brazilian miracle" 1968–76 (military regime): growth above 10% on foreign borrowing, ending in the 1982 debt suspension that followed Mexico's default.
  • Hyperinflation: with no borrowing left, the government printed money and indexed contracts. Inflation peaked at about 2,000% (1993).
  • Cardoso's Real Plan: stopped printing, a new currency anchored to the dollar, high rates and debt restructuring (lower rate, longer term, haircut). The overvalued real led to current-account deficits and a forced float in 1999, after which exports boomed and inflation targeting began.
  • Lula's three contributions: (1) macro stability, continuing Cardoso's discipline; (2) trade, riding China's commodity demand ("the hand of God"; China is now Brazil's top partner); (3) inequality, through conditional cash transfers (Bolsa Família, conditional on school and vaccination).
  • Institutions (Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson): an extractive colony vs. inclusive "neo-Europes." The result is a bimodal income distribution (many poor, few rich, a thin middle). The market is 214m people but only about 40m middle-class. Becker: crime is rational if expected gains exceed expected costs. São Paulo is the world capital of private helicopters.

Dutch disease, tradables, nominal vs. real Session 3 · Garland decks

StepWhat happens
1. DiscoveryResource exports surge and the economy floods with dollars
2. CurrencyExcess dollar supply makes the dollar depreciate and the local currency appreciate
3. TradablesNon-resource exports become dearer and imports cheaper, so agriculture and manufacturing shrink
4. JobsThe resource sector isn't labor-intensive, so unemployment rises
5. GrowthGDP growth is typically lower after the disease sets in (the Netherlands after its gas)
  • Tradables: anything importable or exportable (cars, chips, commodities). Non-tradables: real estate, haircuts, local legal and medical services, cement (traditionally). Cemex made cement tradable; US export bans made Nvidia chips non-tradable. A tourism surge can cause Dutch disease too.
  • The cure (Dutch Disease II): the root cause is the appreciation, so control it. Dirty floating means the central bank keeps buying dollars and injecting local currency, which causes inflation. A fixed rate has the same inflation problem when the commodity price rises and drains reserves when it falls. The best option is to sterilize: keep the foreign currency outside the country in a sovereign wealth fund.
  • Nominal vs. real exchange rates: see the 10 → 15/20/25 peso example in fact 06. Real depreciation means a gain in competitiveness.

Germany, and measuring economies Session 5 · HBS case

  • GDP is output inside the borders. GNP is by nationality (IBM's Mexican profits count for the US) and is almost impossible to measure. GNI starts from GDP, subtracts income leaving and adds income arriving (remittances: El Salvador adds about 30%). The World Bank uses GNI per capita.
  • PPP examples: India's GNI per capita is about $2,760 nominal vs. $11,600 at PPP. Only Switzerland stays more expensive than the US.
  • Degree of openness = (X + M) / GDP. It depends on trade policy and on size and resources. Germany has exports about 50% and imports about 49% of GDP, so about 100% open. The US is consumption-driven (about 67–70% of GDP).
  • History: unified in 1871 (Bismarck). Customs union and protection before free trade (List: free trade only works between equals; Britain "kicked away the ladder"). Bismarck's payroll-funded welfare was designed to undercut socialism. Weimar hyperinflation left a lasting obsession with low inflation (the Bundesbank). Then the Marshall Plan.
  • The German model: relationship banking, codetermination (workers sit on supervisory boards), cooperative unions (strikes need 90% approval), the Mittelstand, the dual vocational system, and cutting hours rather than jobs in recessions (Kurzarbeit).
  • Reunification 1990: the 1:1 conversion rate was political; about 5:1 was economic. Eastern firms became uncompetitive overnight and decades of transfers followed.
  • Euro motives: ending competitive devaluations (probably the main one), transparency, "great powers have great currencies," and anchoring Germany. The DM would be stronger than the euro.
  • Today: stagnation, the energy shock (nuclear shutdown, Russian gas), Chinese EVs, the AfD rising.

Exchange-rate theory Session 8 · Notes on Exchange Rates

Floating, trade modelExports supply dollars, imports demand them. Persistent X > M means the local currency appreciates. Persistent M > X means it depreciates.
Mundell, capital modelCapital chases higher rates at equal risk. Inflows mean appreciation and a trade deficit financed by capital. Outflows mean depreciation and a surplus.
Fixed / peggedA price control. The central bank buys or sells reserves. Changes are called revaluation or devaluation.
Dirty / managed floatFloats, but the central bank intervenes "against the market." It buys dollars to stop appreciation and sells reserves to stop depreciation.
Dual rateTwo official rates that steer exports and imports. The invitation to corruption is fatal.
DollarizationNo currency risk, but no monetary policy ("amputating an arm"). Ecuador, El Salvador, Panama.
  • Peg examples: China at 8.28/$ (1995–2004; weak yuan, exports, 9–10% growth; under US pressure it moved to a band, now about 7). Argentina at 1 peso = $1 (about 1990–2001; strong peso, Miami real estate, exports collapsed; dollar mortgages became unpayable when it broke).
  • Full convertibility: US, eurozone, Japan, Switzerland, Australia, Canada. Capital controls: China and India.
  • Twin deficits: China's dollar buying financed the US trade deficit, and recycling those dollars into Treasuries financed the US fiscal deficit. China's "nuclear option" of dumping dollars would hurt China too.
  • Black markets appear when a peg creates excess demand for dollars (Nigeria, Ethiopia).
  • Balance of payments: the current account (goods, services, primary and secondary income), the financial account (FDI, portfolio) and the capital account (small items such as debt forgiveness). Errors and omissions balance it to zero.

Angola Session 8 · HBS case · your midterm

  • Background: a Portuguese extractive colony, once the world's 4th-largest coffee producer. At independence about 300,000 skilled Portuguese left. 27 years of civil war (MPLA vs. UNITA, Cold War proxies) followed, until 2002.
  • Dutch disease: the kwanza went from 10 to about 90/$ (2000–05) nominally, but inflation (325% in 2000) was far higher, so the real rate appreciated more than 100% (2002–09). Agriculture and manufacturing (about 7% of GDP; only cement and soft drinks survived) were crushed.
  • Volatility: in 2009 the oil price collapsed, exports fell from $65bn to $39bn and the budget went from +8.8% to −9.1% of GDP. The central bank sold about $10bn of reserves (dirty floating), then turned back to the IMF.
  • Political curse: Sonangol is concessionaire, collector, borrower and quasi-fiscal spender, a slush fund. Corruption ranks 162/180. The IMF was kept out until it was needed. The dual exchange rate gave insiders about a 190% flip profit.
  • Asset depletion: less clear. OPEC quotas limited extraction and the regime planned to stay in power for a long time.
  • China vs. IMF: a $2bn Eximbank oil-backed credit line with no conditions but Chinese contractors and labor, vs. IMF conditions (transparency, a sovereign wealth fund).

Norway Session 9 · HBS case

  • It was the poorer Scandinavian country (fishing, farming) until oil (Ekofisk, 1969). Now GNI per capita is about $97k (3rd after Bermuda and Switzerland) and its HDI ranks 1st.
  • Why no curse: strong institutions before the discovery, no corruption, no civil war, state control (Statoil), and building domestic know-how. Volatility is the one symptom present (growth from about 3% to −1.4% in 2009).
  • Government Pension Fund Global (originally the Petroleum Fund): the largest sovereign wealth fund in the world, invested 100% outside Norway. It may not buy Norwegian assets, which keeps oil dollars out of the krone market (sterilization). The Action Rule allows withdrawals of up to about 4% (its expected real return), for example in the pandemic. It was renamed "Pension" because of ageing.
  • Dutch disease options compared: dirty float (inflation risk), fixed rate (Saudi Arabia, 3.75 riyals/$ for about 40 years: is it too strong for non-oil industry?), or sterilization (Norway). Norway, Australia and Canada have "commodity currencies."
  • Not in the EU (rejected twice, about 52 to 48): it protects fish and cheese and keeps independent monetary policy for managing oil. It is in Schengen (as are Iceland and Switzerland).
  • Welfare-state downsides: people get comfortable ("Homo Petrolicus"), entrepreneurship is weak, taxes and spending are high (about 55% of GDP), infrastructure is poor (Oslo to Bergen takes about 10 hours by train), there is little diversification, and the fund policy means no investment at home.
  • Flexicurity: wages adjust in flexible markets (US). Europe is rigid (Spain: 45 days of severance per year; French courts reinstate workers). South Africa: union-driven high wages and about 60% youth unemployment (insiders vs. outsiders). The fix is flexible hiring and firing plus social security (Austria's "backpack" fund).
  • Corruption Perception Index (Transparency International): Denmark and Norway about 88, Singapore about 85. It is perception-based, so biased.

Mexico Session 12 · HBS case

  • 1970sPetrodollar recycling: banks lend variable-rate dollars to emerging markets.
  • 1982US and UK raise rates. Mexico defaults first and the dominoes fall across Latin America. Banks are nationalized; money is printed (inflation 159% in 1987). The lost decade begins.
  • 1987The Pacto: heterodox stabilization in which government, business, unions and farmers coordinate wages and prices to break inflation expectations.
  • 1990Brady bonds: debt becomes US-Treasury-backed bonds with oil collateral, a haircut, a longer maturity and a lower rate. Market access returns. (The Baker Plan of 1985 had failed.)
  • 1988–94Salinas: the Washington Consensus, bank privatization, an open capital account, NAFTA (1 Jan 1994). Rates of about 30% vs. 6–7% in the US pull in $8bn, then $25bn, then $33bn of portfolio money. The peso is held strong in its band and the trade balance goes from +$0.4bn to −$16bn+.
  • 1994Chiapas (1 Jan), the Colosio assassination (23 Mar), the PRI secretary-general's assassination (28 Sep). The Fed adds 175bp. Tesobonos (dollar-indexed) fail to stem outflows; reserves fall from about $30bn to $5bn.
  • Dec 1994The "December mistake" (insiders briefed). 20 Dec: band widened 15%. 22 Dec: float; the peso overshoots from about 3 to about 7.
  • 1995$50bn rescue ($20bn US, $18bn IMF, $10bn BIS). GDP −6%. Trade balance −$18bn becomes +$7bn. The Tequila effect spreads to Brazil, Argentina and Peru.
  • Why not raise rates in 1994? The banks were too fragile. Capital controls would have signaled the end of reform. So reserves were spent and tesobonos issued.
  • Lessons on capital flows: "sudden stops" (Asia 1997, Russia 1998, Brazil 1999). The newer IMF view is that hot money itself can cause crises, which is why China and India keep capital controls. Chile welcomes FDI but requires a 20% one-year deposit on short-term capital.
04

Allard's half: big economies, integration and risk

Sessions 4, 6, 7, 10, 11, 13 and 14. All of her decks were uploaded: US, Japan, India-China, EU/Spain/UK, Africa, Country Investment Decision. Her thread is how each economy turns into risk and opportunity for an investor.

The US economy Session 4 · deck + Allard case

  • Why the US grew: every factor in abundance (land and resources, immigrant labor, British capital and technology) plus inclusive institutions and no feudal aristocracy. About 1.5% per-capita growth a year in the 19th century; it overtook the UK by 1913.
  • About 25% of world GDP, stable since 1980. China's share came mainly from Europe's. To rank countries, use nominal GDP.
  • Potential growth = productivity growth + labor-force growth. An inflationary gap means above potential; a recessionary gap means below. Annualized growth projects a quarter forward over four quarters.
  • Sahm rule: recession likely when the 3-month average unemployment rate is at least 0.5 points above its 12-month low. NAIRU: below it, inflation picks up.
  • Headline vs. core: core strips out food and fuel. Phillips curve: no clean downward slope over 1978–2026. Stagflation risk comes from supply shocks.
  • Fiscal: spending about 40% of GDP vs. taxes about 32%. A structural deficit since the Reagan tax cuts (one surplus year since 1961). Debt above $40tn, about 125% of GDP, heading to about 150%. Sustainable while nominal GDP growth > interest rate, and that margin is narrowing (10-year yield about 5%).
  • Twin deficits (fiscal plus current account) from overconsumption, financed because the world wants dollar assets. It borrows in its own currency. Its net foreign-asset position is worsening.
  • Health and welfare: public health spending is about 8% of GDP but covers only the elderly and poor (Germany covers everyone for the same 8%). Prices aren't capped. Means-tested, not universal. Attitudes: Americans think the poor aren't trapped and luck doesn't decide income; Europeans think government should guarantee no one is in need.
  • Labor: wages have lagged productivity since the 1970s; manufacturing jobs were lost mainly to automation; the middle class is about 59%. These feed polarization.

Japan Sessions 6 and 10 · deck

  • Three indicators: (1) growth: post-war 8–12%, stepped down in the 1980s and again in the 1990s; real growth above nominal because of deflation. (2) GDP per capita grows slightly faster than GDP because the population is shrinking. (3) The current account is always in surplus (except the oil shocks of 1974 and 1979–80).
  • Plaza Accord (1985): the US (with Germany) pushed Japan to let the yen float, and it rose about 50% in two years. That was terrible for exporters and led to the bubble, the bust and the lost decade. Keeping a currency down is easy (accumulate reserves); keeping it up is hard.
  • Deflation: first in 1995. People postpone big purchases, a downward spiral that is very hard to escape. It raises debt/GDP because nominal GDP falls. Unemployment sat below NAIRU without inflation (cultural factors: saving, trust).
  • Abenomics (2012): fiscal expansion (the sales tax was raised twice, each time causing a recession), QE (Japan invented it; it was ineffective because banks didn't lend), and structural reform (the "neglected arrow"; its success was more women working). PM Takaichi continues the approach.
  • Debt about 250% of GDP, the highest anywhere, but in yen, about 90% domestic and with tiny interest costs. The Bank of Japan holds over 50% of government bonds. Rates have gone from negative to about 1.25%. The risk is pensions.
  • Savings paradox: households and firms save the most while the government borrows the most. One proposed fix was a tax on savings.
  • Demography: fertility 1.2, average age about 48. The life-cycle model says an ageing Japan will shift from saver to dissaver. Old-age support ratio about 1.2 by 2050 (vs. about 4 in the OECD today). Japan resists immigration.
  • Carry trade: borrow cheap yen or Swiss francs and invest in USD, AUD, MXN, BRL. As Japan raises rates it unwinds, capital flows home and the yen rises.

China Sessions 7 and 10 · deck + FT and NYT

  • History: China and India were up to half of world GDP (1625–1825), when output tracked population. Parallels: unification about 200 BC (Han and Maurya), radical change in the 1940s (Mao 1949, Indian independence 1947).
  • Mao: the Great Leap Forward (forced industrialization; steel from melted gates; 20–38m famine deaths) and the Cultural Revolution.
  • Deng (from about 1978): pragmatism ("black cat or white cat"), a dual price system (farmers sell surplus at market prices), SEZs, FDI, a weak yuan, export-led growth. Also the one-child policy, now seen as a mistake. 60% of people were in poverty at the start; growth averaged about 10%, then slowed from about 2015.
  • Model: consumption about 40% of GDP, saving far above investment, a current-account surplus. After the 2012 property collapse, investment moved into infrastructure and green energy. The state controls banks, capital and lending (it can manage crises in slow motion).
  • Yuan undervalued 20–35% and actively held there. Subsidized lending below the base rate. Overcapacity: about 30% of world manufacturing vs. about 18% of consumption; solar share went from about 6% to about 80%.
  • China shock 1.0 (WTO about 2001): the US lost about 2–2.5m manufacturing jobs. 2.0 (post-2020): subsidized high-tech exports aimed at Europe. Manufacturing jobs are now migrating to Vietnam, India, Mexico and Turkey.
  • FT, unreliable numbers: official growth about 5% vs. an estimate of about 2%. No quarterly expenditure breakdown; IMF grade C; data series being cut; local officials over-report. Use proxies (electricity, freight, credit).
  • NYT, hidden export policies: the renminbi is down about 14% in real terms since 2022, and VAT export rebates cost $318bn (1.5% of GDP). The result is a record $1.19tn surplus, higher import costs for households and harder rebalancing.

India Sessions 7 and 10 · deck

  • Allied with the USSR. ISI, the Permit Raj, the state taking over failing firms (about 50% of output), the "Hindu rate of growth" of about 3.5%. More business-friendly in the 1980s.
  • 1991 balance-of-payments crisis: a current-account deficit of about 3% of GDP the financial account couldn't cover; reserves for about two weeks of imports; gold airlifted as collateral. The IMF imposed Washington Consensus conditions and growth rose to about 6%. English speakers fed the outsourcing boom.
  • Model: a chronic current-account deficit, consumption about 60% of GDP, investment about 30% of GDP, a rupee that keeps depreciating, services-led (about 3% of world manufacturing).
  • Reforms: GST (2017), demonetization, Aadhaar biometric ID (about 40% of food rations used to disappear), and Modi's Doing Business push from 142nd to 63rd.
  • Risks: institutions, the external deficit, infrastructure, premature deindustrialization, the "four horsemen" (globalization backlash, climate, automation, under-investment in people). Opportunities: domestic demand, demographics, democracy.

The EU and Brexit Session 11 · deck

Level 1Free-trade areaNo internal tariffs. NAFTA/USMCA, EFTA.
Level 2Customs unionAdds a common external tariff. Coal and Steel Community 1951; Turkey–EU.
Level 3Common marketAdds free movement of people and capital: live and work anywhere.
Level 4Economic unionAdds a single currency and coordinated policy. The deepest level, with the most sovereignty given up.
  • Why: war. Economic integration was the "Trojan horse" for peace, and there have been no wars among members since.
  • Enlargement: the original 6, then 1973 (UK, Denmark, Ireland), 1981 Greece, 1986 Spain and Portugal (consolidating democracy), 1995 (Norway and Switzerland said no), 2004 (10 eastern countries; the migration fed Brexit), 2007 Bulgaria and Romania, 2013 Croatia, 2021 the UK leaves. The EU has 27 members and 450m people; the eurozone 21 members and 351m.
  • Metrics (2024): GDP per capita US about $86k, eurozone about $66k, EU about $63k. The EU runs a current-account surplus (even without Germany). Unemployment is structurally higher. Support for the EU is 74%, for the euro 83%.
  • Challenges: a shrinking working-age population (Poland −40%, Germany −30% by 2060), falling productivity, no big tech, regulation, and lagging R&D (except Sweden, Belgium, Austria and Germany).
  • Welfare: universal, which builds consensus. Sweden (welfare about 50–60% of GDP, debt 30%, high R&D) shows welfare and competitiveness can coexist. Gini: Sweden 0.27, Denmark 0.29, Germany 0.30. Pre-tax inequality is similar to the US; taxes and transfers make the difference.
  • Brexit: a conflicted history (de Gaulle vetoed UK entry in 1963 and 1967; a budget rebate; out of Schengen and the euro). The leave vote was rural, older, less-educated and in low-immigration areas. Article 50 was triggered in 2017. The result: slower growth, inflation, red tape, finance moving to Frankfurt and Paris. Most Britons now favor rejoining or closer ties.
  • Rodrik trilemma and O'Rourke ("too much market and too little state invites backlash") explain Brexit and Trump.

Africa Session 13 · deck

  • Scale: 54 countries and about 2,000 languages. GDP about $2tn, under 2% of the world (3.6% at PPP). Before the Industrial Revolution it was about 7% of world GDP; the slave trade depleted its population.
  • Understated GDP: statistics are expensive to collect, the informal economy is large and capital flight is common. Nigeria's 2014 rebasing added 89% overnight; estimates put the understatement at about 30%. Under 10% of Ethiopia's trade is invoiced.
  • Growth: some of the fastest growers before 2015 (the commodity supercycle). Covid hit less hard (a young population). Fragile supply chains. Non-resource economies (Ethiopia, Kenya) are steadier than oil exporters. Growth trailing population growth means falling per-capita income.
  • Aid vs. FDI: aid has a negative coefficient on growth and breeds corruption (Mobutu's Swiss accounts). FDI brings jobs, technology and links to buyers.
  • Demography: 13% of world population, doubling by 2050. Nigeria will match the US. Fertility was about 7 in the 1980s, but birth rates are falling faster than the UN projected.
  • Institutions and conflict: the Congo (Leopold, Lumumba, Mobutu), Ethiopia (10% growth until its civil war), Sudan and South Sudan, the Bab-el-Mandeb chokepoint. Landlocked with bad neighbors is the worst position. Rwanda is authoritarian but effective.
  • AfCFTA: signed by everyone except Eritrea; operational since 2019; tariffs off 90% of goods; aims at a common market, a single currency and free movement. Allard sees currency integration as feasible.

  • Definitions: country risk (Kobrin). Risk is quantifiable and known in advance; uncertainty is not (an earthquake). Endogenous shocks (bubbles, banking crises, politics) vs. exogenous shocks (commodities, pandemics, war).
  • What investors want: revenue potential (growth, market, middle class: hence the Gini), an operating environment (low predictable inflation, predictable policy, rational labor markets, low taxes, infrastructure), financial conditions, market access.
  • Business-cycle phases: phase 1, early expansion (recessionary gap, low rates), is the best time to invest. Phase 2 is the peak; phase 3 contraction; phase 4 the trough, good once it seems near.
  • Scorecards: ICRG = 0.5 political + 0.25 financial + 0.25 economic; a higher score is safer; below 50 is very high risk and 80+ very low. The World Bank CPIA has 16 criteria. Doing Business was suspended after manipulation (China) and is being replaced by Business Ready.
  • Hard countries attracting investment: Rwanda (Kagame's reforms), India (Doing Business from 142nd to 63rd), Ghana (an industrial park with its own power, water and legal support; Ford is a tenant). Corruption adds about 10% to costs in Africa. Singapore pays officials well.
  • Corruption measures: the Transparency International CPI (perception surveys) and the World Governance Indicators (−2.5 to +2.5). There is a difference between petty bribery and systemic corruption (a Delhi procurement bucket billed at about $1,500 vs. a real cost under $5).
  • Simulation reveals: Tweedworth was Mauritius (the most popular pick), Trinity Island the Czech Republic, Grand Republic Venezuela (the worst), Somerville Bulgaria, Pine Way Israel, Liberty Kingdom Turkey, Princeton Island Egypt; the high-income outlier was Hong Kong. The lesson is that data alone doesn't tell the story.

Arc. A late response to the 1973 oil shock (energy subsidies, soft wage deals, inflation of 24.5% in 1977), then the Moncloa Pacts. EEC entry in 1986 and EU structural funds. The peseta appreciated inside the EMS, then speculative attacks brought four devaluations (1992–95). Austerity, privatization and an independent Bank of Spain (1998) took it into the euro. Cheap euro credit fed a housing boom; the bust brought internal devaluation; the pandemic brought NextGenEU.

Structural problems. A dual labor market (permanent vs. temporary contracts), low R&D (1.24% of GDP), about 75% of energy imported, fertility of 1.23, and productivity growing +6.9% over 1995–2019 against +43.9% in the US.

Exam hook. A fiscal surplus hid a private credit boom and an external deficit. Without a currency, Spain adjusted through wages and unemployment.

China in the news: two articles

FT Big Read (Hale, Nov 2025) · NYT (Bradsher, Oct 2026) · Session 10
Why they matter: data reliability is itself a country-risk indicator, and export strength can hide domestic weakness.

FT, "The growing problem with China's unreliable numbers": official growth hits its about-5% target every time, while one estimate is about 2%. There are three GDP approaches (expenditure, production, income); China publishes only production data quarterly. IMF grade C. The costs are pricier imports for households, a fiscal drain and protectionist backlash. This works against rebalancing toward consumption.
