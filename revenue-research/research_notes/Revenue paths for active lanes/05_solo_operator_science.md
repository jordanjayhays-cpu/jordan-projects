# Solo operator science: what the evidence says about allocating time across several early ventures to maximise income (6–12 months)

Profile researched for: one person, no salary, one paying commission lane (BD for a recruitment agency), one working-but-unpaid product (local booking marketplace), one pre-client agency (outsourced BD), two parked ideas, ~35 open tasks. Research current to 2026-11-24. Each finding is tagged **[PR]** peer-reviewed, **[LD]** large-sample dataset or official statistics, **[PRAC]** practitioner/vendor, or **[UNSURE]** where the number is widely repeated but could not be verified against the primary source.

---

## Key question 1: Focus and multitasking — does spreading across ventures reduce income, and by how much?

### Takeaway
Lab research shows switching between unfamiliar, rule-heavy tasks carries measurable time and attention costs, and that unfinished tasks leave "attention residue" that degrades the next task; the entrepreneurship literature shows that running a venture alongside a job (hybrid entry) improves survival, and that focused pivoting (one or two pivots, not many) correlates with higher performance. No study quantifies the income penalty of a solo operator running N pre-revenue ventures at once; the "40% productivity loss" figure is a researcher's interview remark, not a published result.

### Cited Findings
- **[PR]** Rubinstein, Meyer & Evans (2001, *J. Experimental Psychology: Human Perception & Performance*): task alternation produced switching-time costs that *increased with rule complexity* and *decreased with task familiarity/cuing*. — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11518143/); [APA full text PDF](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/xhp274763.pdf)
- **[UNSURE / not from the paper]** The widely quoted "multitasking can cost as much as 40 percent of someone's productive time" is attributed by the APA to a statement by David Meyer, not to a measured result in the 2001 paper. — [APA, "Multitasking: Switching costs"](https://www.apa.org/topics/research/multitasking)
- **[PR]** Leroy (2009, *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 109(2):168–181; ~825 citations): people carry "attention residue" from a prior task into the next; residue is larger when the prior task is *unfinished*; finishing a task *under time pressure* before switching lowered residue and raised performance on the next task. Participants' minds "tend to multitask" even when told to focus on one task. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749597809000399); [APA PsycNet record](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2009-10822-007)
- **[PR]** Mark, Gudith & Klocke (2008, CHI; ~1,586 citations), "The Cost of Interrupted Work": interrupted workers completed tasks in less time but with higher stress, frustration, time pressure and effort. — [UC Irvine PDF](https://ics.uci.edu/~gmark/chi08-mark.pdf)
- **[PR/PRAC]** The "23 minutes 15 seconds to return to a task after an interruption" figure originates in Gloria Mark's fieldwork and is repeated in her later Microsoft Research paper ("on average around 23 minutes to resume an interrupted task"). — [Mark et al., Microsoft Research PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/p903-mark.pdf); [Gallup interview with Mark](https://news.gallup.com/businessjournal/23146/too-many-interruptions-work.aspx)
- **[PR]** Gompers, Kovner, Lerner & Scharfstein (2010, *J. Financial Economics*; ~1,054 citations): VC-backed entrepreneurs who previously succeeded (IPO) have a 30% chance of success in the next venture vs 18% for first-timers and 20% for previously failed founders. Overall first-venture success rate 25.3%; serial entrepreneurs' subsequent ventures 29.0%. — [Harvard Law Forum summary](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2010/04/16/performance-persistence-in-entrepreneurship-and-venture-capital/); [NBER working paper w12592](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12592/w12592.pdf); [RePEc record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:96:y:2010:i:1:p:18-32)
- **[PR]** Raffiee & Feng (2014, *Academy of Management Journal* 57(4), "Should I Quit My Day Job? A Hybrid Path to Entrepreneurship"; ~522 citations): hybrid entrepreneurs who later go full-time "have much higher rates of survival relative to individuals who enter full-time self-employment directly from paid employment"; the survival advantage is attributed to a learning effect and is stronger for those with prior entrepreneurial experience; risk-averse and low core-self-evaluation people are more likely to choose hybrid entry. — [AOM abstract](https://journals.aom.org/doi/abs/10.5465/amj.2012.0522); [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/43589291)
- **[UNSURE]** The specific "33% lower hazard of exit" number for hybrid-then-full-time founders is widely repeated in secondary sources; the abstract says only "much higher rates of survival." Treat the direction as solid and the magnitude as unverified here.
- **[PR]** Folta, Delmar & Wennberg (2010, *Management Science* 56(2):253–269; ~822 citations), "Hybrid Entrepreneurship": hybrids (simultaneous wage work and self-employment) "represent a significant share of all entrepreneurial activity"; the paper models hybrid entry as a low-cost way to learn before committing. — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v56y2010i2p253-269.html)
- **[PR]** Westhead & Wright (2003, *Regional Studies* 37(2):187–200; ~261 citations): portfolio entrepreneurs (owning several businesses at once) have more diverse experience and more resources than serial or novice entrepreneurs; businesses owned by portfolio entrepreneurs reported larger absolute sales growth than novices'. — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/regstd/v37y2003i2p187-200.html); [Durham repository PDF](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1411820)
- **[PR]** Ropęga et al. (2025) and a 2026 *Scientific Reports* paper examine differences among novice, serial and portfolio entrepreneurs, but the search snippets did not give income figures. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12448965/); [Nature Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-59807-x)
- **[PR]** Hamilton (2000, *J. Political Economy* 108(3); ~3,163 citations), "Does Entrepreneurship Pay?": the self-employed have lower initial earnings *and* lower earnings growth than in paid employment; median self-employment earnings after 10 years are 35% below the predicted alternative wage, and below the predicted *starting* wage at any business tenure. — [U. Chicago Press abstract](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/262131); [full PDF](https://www.edegan.com/pdfs/Hamilton%20(2000)%20-%20Does%20Entrepreneurship%20Pay%20An%20Empirical%20Analysis%20Of%20The%20Returns%20To%20Self%20Employment.pdf)
- **[PR]** Camuffo et al. (2024, *Strategic Management Journal*, 759 firms across 4 RCTs): founders taught a scientific hypothesis-testing method pivot in a "focused" way (once or twice) and avoid pivoting indefinitely; "the pivoting pattern of scientific entrepreneurs correlates with higher performance." — [SMJ full text](https://sms.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/smj.3580)

### Inferences
- The switching literature says the cost of a switch is highest when the tasks are unfamiliar and rule-heavy and when the prior task is left unfinished. For this profile, four very different domains (recruitment BD, marketplace product, agency setup, parked ideas) are exactly the high-cost case. The remedy that is supported by evidence is not "never switch" but *finish a bounded unit before switching* (Leroy) and batch same-type work.
- Hybrid-entry evidence supports keeping the paying BD lane as the "day job" while testing other ventures, rather than dropping it to go all-in on the marketplace.
- Portfolio-entrepreneur studies are about owners of several *operating* businesses with resources and teams, not a solo founder with several pre-revenue ideas; they do not justify parallel building for this profile.
- Hamilton's result is a reminder that self-employment income typically underperforms a wage for years; the 6–12 month income goal argues for the lane closest to cash, which is commission BD.

### Gaps
- No study found that measures income or revenue loss as a function of the number of ventures a solo founder runs simultaneously. The "40%" multitasking figure has no paper behind it.
- No income comparison found for portfolio vs novice *solo* founders at the pre-revenue stage.
- The exact Raffiee & Feng hazard reduction could not be verified from the abstract (full text paywalled).

## Key question 2: Sales activity and income — volumes, speed-to-lead, follow-up cadence, referrals

### Takeaway
Benchmarks from sales-development surveys and large call datasets give the activity levels that commission sellers actually sustain (roughly 100+ touches and ~4 real conversations a day for a full-time SDR, producing ~10 held meetings a month), and show that connect rates are low (~5%) so volume and timing matter; speed-to-lead and at least one follow-up have measured effects, but the famous "80% of sales need five follow-ups" statistic has no traceable study behind it. Referred customers are measurably more valuable and word-of-mouth is the most trusted source.

### Cited Findings
**Activity volumes that sellers sustain**
- **[PRAC, survey n=351]** The Bridge Group, *SDR Models, Metrics & Compensation*, 10th ed. (Feb 2025): median SDR daily activity 112 touches (44 phone, 41 email, 19 LinkedIn, 8 text/other) yielding 4.1 quality conversations/day; phone-centric teams average 56 dials and 4.6 QCs/day vs email-centric 28 dials and 3.4 QCs. Median monthly quota 10 held first meetings (16 if introductory meetings count, 10.4 semi-qualified, 9.0 fully qualified); Stage-1 converted median 6. Only 60% of SDRs hit quota, lowest on record. Median OTE $80K (68:32 base:variable). Average ramp 3.0 months. Report notes it is observational survey data, 83% B2B SaaS. — [Bridge Group 2025 report](https://www.bridgegroupinc.com/research/2025-sdr-models-metrics-report-the-bridge-group)
- **[PRAC, 300M calls]** Gong Labs: the average rep connects with 5.4% of prospects dialled; top-quartile reps connect with 13.3%. — [Gong, "Hidden power of cold calling"](https://www.gong.io/blog/the-hidden-power-of-cold-calling-insights-from-300m-calls)
- **[PRAC]** Gong: opening a cold call with "Did I catch you at a bad time?" is associated with 40% lower meeting-booking; Wednesday/Thursday are the best days (citing InsideSales data). — [Gong cold call stats](https://www.gong.io/blog/cold-call-stats)
- **[PRAC, secondary]** A practitioner summary of the Gong 300M-call data reports connect rates fall after the second dial attempt to the same number, that 9–11 am local is the best window, and that combining calls with email raised email reply rates (3.44% vs 1.81%). — [30MPC summary](https://www.30mpc.com/interactive-resources/how-to-master-cold-calls-data-from-300m-calls-30mpc); [LinkedIn repost](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/cold-calling-dead-heres-what-data-from-300m-calls-says-gong-io-nwtwe) **[UNSURE: not verified against Gong's own page]**
- **[PRAC, survey]** Salesforce *State of Sales* (6th ed., 2024): reps spend only 30% of their week selling (28% in 2022); 70% goes to non-selling tasks. — [Salesforce report PDF](https://assets.ctfassets.net/f43wltp2j5se/2gHMpCURXzpMW7PJ3SlWZJ/3cad8d7e8496abbd3c0f99d5c7f16ef4/salesforce-state-of-sales-report-6-ed.pdf)

**Speed-to-lead**
- **[PRAC, HBR; large sample, correlational]** Oldroyd, McElheran & Elkington, "The Short Life of Online Sales Leads" (*HBR*, March 2011): audit of 2,241 US companies found 37% responded to a web lead within an hour, 16% within 1–24 hours, 24% took more than 24 hours, 23% never responded; average response among responders was 42 hours. Across 1.25 million leads at 42 companies, firms that attempted contact within one hour were nearly 7x as likely to *qualify* the lead (meaningful conversation with a decision maker) as those that waited one more hour, and more than 60x as likely as those that waited 24+ hours. Full article paywalled; figures taken from the article's public summary and secondary write-ups. — [HBR article](https://hbr.org/2011/03/the-short-life-of-online-sales-leads); [BYU ScholarsArchive record](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/9711/); [summary with figures](https://www.twinsai.com/resources/speed-to-lead); [second summary](https://www.zoye.io/blog/speed-to-lead)

**Follow-up cadence**
- **[Myth, traced]** "80% of sales require five follow-ups / 44% of reps give up after one" has no study behind it. The "80% of sales on the 5th–12th contact" ladder traces, per the successor body SMEI, to a 1942 survey of one Long Island chapter of the National Sales Executives Association with fewer than 40 respondents; the "80% + 44%" package first appears in a 2009 Marketing Donut column that names no study. Every later version (Brevet 2015, Invesp, HubSpot 2026) cites another page or none. — [AInora source trace](https://ainora.lt/blog/sales-follow-up-80-percent-5-contacts-study-2026); [Bebop follow-up statistics](https://bebop.ai/statistics/sales-follow-up-statistics); [SMEI sales statistics page](https://smei.org/sales-statistics/); [VentureBeat 2014 debunk, cited therein](https://venturebeat.com/marketing/these-incredible-sales-stats-everyone-cites-are-actually-completely-false)
- **[PRAC, 12M emails]** Backlinko/Pitchbox analysis of 12 million outreach emails (2019): only 8.5% of outreach emails get a reply; a single additional follow-up message was associated with 65.8% more replies; contacting several people at the same organisation was associated with a 93% higher response rate; follow-ups to multiple contacts gave a 1.6x reply rate vs one message to one contact. Context is link-building/PR outreach, not B2B sales. — [Backlinko study](https://backlinko.com/email-outreach-study); [PRNewswire release](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/only-1-of-8-outreach-emails-receive-a-reply-new-study-by-pitchbox-and-backlinko-finds-300833306.html)

**Referrals**
- **[PR]** Schmitt, Skiera & Van den Bulte (2011, *Journal of Marketing*), ~10,000 customers of a German bank tracked ~3 years: referred customers had higher contribution margins (about 4.5 cents/day more, eroding over time), higher retention (82.0% vs 79.2% over the observation window; the difference persists), and customer value at least 16% higher than non-referred customers with the same demographics and acquisition date (40 euros over six years in the base case). — [Wharton PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/04/Schmitt-Skiera-vandenBulte-2011-Referral-Programs-Customer-Value.pdf); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2476354)
- **[LD, survey]** Nielsen *Trust in Advertising* 2021 (40,000 respondents, 56 countries): 88% trust recommendations from people they know more than any other channel; the 2012 edition reported 92%. — [Nielsen 2021](https://www.nielsen.com/insights/2021/beyond-martech-building-trust-with-consumers-and-engaging-where-sentiment-is-high/); [Nielsen 2012](https://www.nielsen.com/insights/2012/trust-in-advertising-paid-owned-and-earned/)
- **[PRAC, UNSURE]** A dialler vendor's benchmark page claims referral-sourced calls connect 35–50% of the time vs 3–6% for switchboard numbers; no methodology given. — [Skipcall benchmarks](https://skipcall.io/en/blog/cold-call-connect-rate-benchmarks)

### Inferences
- A full-time SDR converts ~112 touches/day into ~4 conversations/day and ~10 held meetings/month. A part-time solo seller with roughly a third of that selling time should expect ~1–1.5 real conversations a day and ~3–4 held meetings a month from cold outbound alone; referrals and warm introductions are the lever that changes those ratios most (the only channel with peer-reviewed evidence of higher customer value, and the highest-trust channel).
- Because connect rates are ~5%, outbound income is a volume game *per hour of selling*; batching calls into the 9–11 am window on Tue–Thu, and protecting those hours, is the evidence-consistent structure.
- Follow-up: plan at least 3 touches per prospect (one follow-up alone adds ~two-thirds more replies in the only large dataset found), and contact more than one person per target firm; do not plan around "five follow-ups" as if it were a measured threshold.
- Any inbound enquiry (marketplace studio sign-up, agency lead) should be answered within the hour; this is one of the few large-sample results in sales.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed dose-response study of outbound activity volume to commission income for solo sellers or small agencies was found; all activity benchmarks are vendor or survey data from SaaS SDR teams.
- No conversion-rate data specific to recruitment-agency business development (client acquisition) was found.
- Gong's underlying data are proprietary; the 300M-call figures cannot be independently checked.

## Key question 3: Commission-based income volatility — how volatile, how long to a stable base, what reduces it

### Takeaway
Large administrative and survey datasets show self-employed and gig incomes are far more variable than wages (59% of self-employed vs 28% of employees report month-to-month variation), that platform/gig earnings are sporadic (participants earn in only ~40–56% of subsequent months and over half exit within a year), and that the median self-employed person earns less than the wage alternative for years. No study gives a "months to stable base" for commission BD; the volatility-reducers in the evidence are a steady primary income stream, diversified sources, and persistence (those who stay become more reliant and more regular). Retainer-vs-project claims are practitioner-only.

### Cited Findings
- **[LD, 1M customers]** JPMorgan Chase Institute, *Paychecks, Paydays, and the Online Platform Economy* (2016): median-income individuals saw nearly $500 in month-to-month labour-income fluctuation; 86% of volatility came from variation within the same job (72% paycheque amount, 28% pay frequency); nearly 4 in 10 had a job transition in a year. Labour-platform participants averaged $533/month, 33% of their income, but after the first month earned in only 56% of subsequent months; labour-platform earnings mainly *offset* a 14% dip in other income rather than adding to it. — [JPMC Institute report](https://www.jpmorganchase.com/institute/all-topics/labor-markets-and-workforce/report-paychecks-paydays-and-the-online-platform-economy); [JPMC summary](https://www.jpmorganchase.com/institute/news-events/insight-online-platform-econ-income-volatility)
- **[LD, 240,000 participants]** JPMorgan Chase Institute, *The Online Platform Economy: Has Growth Peaked?* (2016): one in six participants in any month is new; more than half exit within 12 months; labour-platform participants earned in only 41% of subsequent months; those who persist past year one rely on platform income for 47% of income in active months vs 30% for those who drop out; platform income is 24% of annual income for labour-platform participants. — [JPMC Institute brief](https://www.jpmorganchase.com/institute/all-topics/labor-markets-and-workforce/jpmc-institute-online-platform-econ-brief)
- **[LD, national survey]** Federal Reserve SHED, *Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households in 2024* (May 2025): 59% of self-employed adults said income varied month to month vs 28% of employees; 41% of gig workers vs 26% of non-gig; 20% of adults did gig activity; 49% of gig workers wish pay were more consistent and 31% would have trouble making ends meet without gigs; 19% of adults with income under $25,000 had difficulty paying bills because of varying income vs 3% at $100,000+. — [SHED Income and Expenses](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2025-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2024-income-and-expenses.htm); [SHED Employment and Gig Work](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2025-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2024-employment-and-gig-work.htm); [SHED full PDF](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/2024-report-economic-well-being-us-households-202505.pdf). The May 2026 edition (2025 data) executive summary is published but the gig section was not retrieved. — [SHED 2025 data executive summary](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2025-executive-summary.htm)
- **[PR]** Hamilton (2000): self-employed earnings are below the alternative wage at all tenures; median gap 35% at 10 years; the gap is much smaller at the upper quartile. — [U. Chicago Press](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/262131)
- **[PRAC, unsourced]** Agency/freelance blogs claim retainer-dominant agencies have "35 to 40 percent more predictable monthly income" and that freelancers earning over $100K derive 60–70% of revenue from retainers; neither cites a dataset. — [Taskip](https://taskip.net/retainer-vs-project-based-pricing/); [Client Growth Engine](https://www.clientgrowthengine.com/blog/freelance-retainer-vs-project-pricing); [Brisk](https://www.staybrisk.com/blog/retainer-vs-project-pricing-which-is-better-for-freelancers) **[UNSURE]**

### Inferences
- Expect the BD commission lane to pay irregularly for the first several months (recruitment placement fees arrive on placement/start date, often with rebate periods); the gig data suggest the people who stabilise are those who stay in the lane continuously rather than dipping in and out.
- The structural reducers of volatility that the data support are: one stream that pays monthly (a retainer or a part-salary), several paying clients rather than one, and continuity of activity. Minimum fees and retainers are sensible but their effect size for small service firms is not measured in any source found.
- For planning, treat month-to-month BD income with a wide band and set the kill/continue review on *leading* activity metrics (meetings, proposals) rather than on cash in any single month.

### Gaps
- No dataset found on time-to-stable-income for commission-based BD or recruitment sales.
- No Spanish (INE/Seguridad Social autónomo) income-volatility data retrieved; the volatility evidence is US-based.
- No peer-reviewed evidence on retainers vs per-placement fees and survival for small service firms.

## Key question 4: Pricing science — anchoring, value-based pricing, retainers, cheap willingness-to-pay methods

### Takeaway
First-offer anchoring is one of the most robust negotiation findings (meta-analytic correlation ~0.5 between first offer and outcome, weaker when the other side has good information); most firms still price on cost or competition (value-based adoption ~17–37% in Hinterhuber's surveys); the Van Westendorp meter is biased by its hypothetical questions but tracks incentive-aligned WTP measures reasonably, so it is an acceptable cheap first pass if followed by a real price test; the Mom Test is a practitioner method whose core (ask for commitment, not opinions) is consistent with the RCT evidence on hypothesis testing.

### Cited Findings
- **[PR, meta-analysis]** Orr & Guthrie (2006, *Ohio State J. on Dispute Resolution*), meta-analysis of 16 studies: correlation of roughly 0.5 between first offer/anchor and negotiated outcome; weaker (.38) when information was shared than when it was not (.497). — [Vanderbilt Law full text](https://scholarship.law.vanderbilt.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1727&context=faculty-publications); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228195748_Anchoring_Information_Expertise_and_Negotiation_New_Insights_from_Meta-Analysis)
- **[PR]** Galinsky & Mussweiler (2001, *JPSP*): in three experiments the party making the first offer obtained the better price, unless the counterpart focused on information inconsistent with the anchor (e.g., their own BATNA/target). Later work found the first-offer effect robust across culture, power and issues, and precise numbers (e.g., €14,875) anchor more strongly. — [NCMR process-model review](https://ncmr.lps.library.cmu.edu/article/id/574/)
- **[PR]** Hinterhuber (2008; and Hinterhuber & Liozu): adoption of value-based pricing was just 17% in one survey and averaged 37% across surveys; "more than 80 percent of companies continue to price their products and services primarily on the basis of costs and/or competitive price levels." — [Hinterhuber JRPM 2012 PDF](https://hinterhuber.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Value_based_pricing_conceptualization_JRPM_2012.pdf); [Hinterhuber & Liozu, "why companies resist"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228711296_Customer_value-based_pricing_strategies_why_companies_resist); [PDF](https://lp-website.s3.amazonaws.com/pdfs/Customer_value_based_pricing_strategies.pdf)
- **[PR]** Study of the Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter as a WTP measure: it "yields biased results because of its hypothetical nature and its focus on minimum customer resistance," but "the measurement results are comparable to those of the incentive-aligned Becker-DeGroot-Marschak mechanism," so it has "high predictive quality"; tested on a single low-priced product. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/304658564_THE_VAN_WESTENDORP_PRICE-SENSITIVITY_METER_AS_A_DIRECT_MEASURE_OF_WILLINGNESS-TO-PAY)
- **[PRAC]** Van Westendorp limitations: assumes respondents know what the product is worth (works better in B2B), ignores competitors' responses, and the "optimal price point" is not a profit-maximising price; live market tests should override survey results. — [Forbes guide](https://www.forbes.com/sites/rebeccasadwick/2020/06/22/how-to-price-products/)
- **[PRAC, book]** Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test*: three rules (talk about their life not your idea; ask about specifics in the past not generics or the future; talk less, listen more); the required outcome of a customer meeting is *commitment* (time, reputation or money) and *advancement* to the next funnel step; "the only way to fail is by not asking for any commitment." Not peer reviewed. — [Book site](https://www.momtestbook.com/); [chapter summary](https://mtlynch.io/book-reports/the-mom-test/); [PDF](https://inkubator.si/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/The-Mom-Test-by-@robfitz.pdf)

### Inferences
- For the outsourced-BD agency: make the first offer, make it precise, and anchor on value delivered (e.g., value of a placement or a client win) rather than hours; the anchoring effect is strongest where the buyer lacks reference prices, which is the case for a new service category.
- For the marketplace: a 4-question Van Westendorp survey to 20–30 studios is a cheap, defensible first pass; treat it as a range, then run a real price (pre-sale) to validate, because hypothetical bias inflates stated WTP.
- Hinterhuber's data mean that pricing on value is itself a differentiator; the small agency's low cost base makes cost-plus pricing a trap.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed evidence found on which pricing structures (retainer, per-placement, hybrid) small service businesses that survive actually use.
- No academic test of the Mom Test method itself; its support is indirect (the Camuffo RCTs on hypothesis testing).
- Conjoint "lite" methods were not researched in this pass.

## Key question 5: Sequencing and "sell before you build" — lean/customer-development evidence and base rates

### Takeaway
The only randomised evidence on startup method (Camuffo et al. 2020, replicated at scale in 2024 with 759 firms) shows that a disciplined hypothesis-testing approach raises revenue, raises the rate of idea termination (+9.8 pp) and produces focused pivots; failure post-mortems and survival statistics show that lack of market need/product-market fit is the dominant root cause (43% of 431 failed VC-backed startups) and that about one in five new establishments fails in year one and half by year five. There is no RCT on pre-selling or letters of intent as such; the inference is strong but indirect.

### Cited Findings
- **[PR, RCT]** Camuffo, Cordova, Gambardella & Spina (2020, *Management Science* 66(2):564–586; ~815 citations): 116 Italian startups, 16 data points over about a year. Both groups got 10 training sessions; treated founders were taught to frame hypotheses and test them rigorously. Treated startups "perform better, are more likely to pivot to a different idea, and are not more likely to drop out"; the approach "reduces the odds of pursuing projects with false positive returns." — [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2018.3249); [INSEAD summary](https://www.insead.edu/faculty-research/publications/journal-articles/a-scientific-approach-entrepreneurial-decision); [APA record](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2020-13963-003)
- **[PR, RCT replication]** Camuffo et al. (2024, *Strategic Management Journal*): 759 firms in four RCTs; the intervention raised the probability of terminating the idea by 9.8 percentage points (p = .001); 34% of all firms terminated in the window; treated firms pivot once or twice rather than never or repeatedly, and this pattern correlates with higher performance; effects are consistent across contexts. — [SMJ full text](https://sms.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/smj.3580); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/stratm/v45y2024i6p1209-1237.html)
- **[PR, preliminary draft]** Camuffo et al. working paper (2020, 250 nascent startups in a pre-acceleration programme): more treated startups closed (59 vs 46); cumulative revenue per startup at week 66 was ~€3,020 (treated) vs ~€1,280 (control); most pre-accelerator startups had zero revenue. — [U. Arizona PDF](https://eller.arizona.edu/sites/default/files/Small%20changes%20with%20big%20impact-%20experimental%20evidence%20of%20a%20scientific%20approach%20to%20the%20decision-making%20of%20entrepreneurial%20firms.pdf)
- **[PRAC, 431 post-mortems]** CB Insights (2024 update): of 431 failed VC-backed companies that had raised $17.5B combined, 70% ran out of capital (described as the symptom), with root causes poor product-market fit 43%, bad timing 29%, unsustainable unit economics 19%; 20 Series B+ companies also cited poor PMF. The older 2014 analysis (~100 post-mortems) put "no market need" at 42%. — [CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/); [summary of 2014 vs 2024](https://preuve.ai/blog/why-startups-fail-market-fit)
- **[PRAC, 2,000+ companies]** Shikhar Ghosh (HBS) research on 2,000+ VC-backed companies raising $1M+ in 2004–2010: ~75% never return cash to investors; 30–40% liquidate all assets. — [GeekWire on the WSJ report](https://www.geekwire.com/2012/hard-truth-report-75-percent-startups-fail/); [Inc.](https://www.inc.com/john-mcdermott/report-3-out-of-4-venture-backed-start-ups-fail.html)
- **[LD, official]** BLS Business Employment Dynamics, Table 7 (survival of private-sector establishments by opening year): establishments born March 2023 had 78.2% surviving at one year; the March 2015 cohort had 50.2% surviving at five years (March 2020); the March 2020 cohort had 63.6% at three years and 46.8% at six years (March 2026). — [BLS Table 7](https://www.bls.gov/bdm/us_age_naics_00_table7.txt); [BLS establishment age and survival](https://www.bls.gov/bdm/bdmage.htm)
- **[LD, official]** BLS: 34.7% of establishments born in 2013 were still operating in 2023; 10-year survival by industry: professional, scientific and technical services 30.9%, information 29.1%, health care 35.7%, accommodation and food 38.2%. — [BLS TED](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2024/34-7-percent-of-business-establishments-born-in-2013-were-still-operating-in-2023.htm)
- **[LD, official]** Eurostat: EU enterprise birth rate 10.5% and death rate 8.5% in 2023; Spain's enterprise death rate 7.3% (2021). — [Eurostat news 2025](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/products-eurostat-news/w/ddn-20251013-1); [Key figures on European business 2024 PDF](https://www.efta.int/sites/default/files/uploads/2025-03/key%20figures%20on%20european%20business-KSET24001ENN.pdf); [Eurostat business demography explained](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Business_demography_statistics)
- **[PR]** Mollick (2014, *J. Business Venturing*), "The dynamics of crowdfunding": ~48% of Kickstarter projects succeeded; success is associated with personal network size and signals of project quality; all 25 projects with goals above $1M failed. Crowdfunding is the closest large-sample evidence on pre-selling. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S088390261300058X); [PDF](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/bhagat/Dynamics-of-Crowdfunding.pdf)

### Inferences
- The strongest evidence-based sequencing rule is: state the revenue hypothesis for each venture, run the cheapest test that produces a paying or committing customer, and terminate on a negative result; this is what moved revenue in the RCTs and what the post-mortems say founders fail to do.
- For the marketplace, the relevant base rate is not "startups fail" but "no market need" as the dominant cause; a working product with no paying users is precisely the state the RCT protocol tells a founder to test and, if payment does not come, to terminate or pivot once.
- Service businesses (the agency, the BD lane) have the same establishment survival base rates as everything else in BLS data; their advantage for *income* is time-to-first-cash, not survival odds.

### Gaps
- No randomised or large-sample study found that tests pre-selling or letters of intent against build-first.
- No marketplace-specific or SaaS-specific failure base rate from an official source; CB Insights is VC-backed only.
- Spain-specific survival rates (INE) were not retrieved.

## Key question 6: Decision rules — kill criteria, stage gates, review cadence

### Takeaway
Discovery-driven planning (McGrath & MacMillan 1995) and real-options reasoning (McGrath 1999) are the standard frameworks: start from required profit, list every assumption, test the cheapest/most critical assumptions at milestones, and release resources only to the next checkpoint. The Camuffo RCTs are the only experimental evidence that such protocols change behaviour, and they show the main effect is *more terminations* and *fewer, better pivots*. No evidence found on the optimal review frequency.

### Cited Findings
- **[PRAC, HBR]** McGrath & MacMillan, "Discovery-Driven Planning" (*HBR*, July–Aug 1995): four documents, a *reverse income statement* (start with required profit, derive necessary revenue and allowable cost), *pro forma operations specs*, a *key assumptions checklist*, and a *milestone planning chart* that specifies which assumptions are tested at each milestone; "postpone major commitments of resources until the evidence from the previous milestone event signals that the risk of taking the next step is justified"; designate a "keeper of the assumptions"; if the revised reverse income statement does not hang together, "the venture should be scrapped." — [HBR](https://hbr.org/1995/07/discovery-driven-planning); [full text PDF](http://mengwong.com/school/HarvardBusinessReview/Discovery%20Driven%20Planning.pdf)
- **[PR, theory]** McGrath (1999, *Academy of Management Review* 24(1):13–30; ~2,567 citations), "Falling Forward: Real Options Reasoning and Entrepreneurial Failure": treats ventures as options, arguing for small, staged commitments and prompt termination of losers so that failures are cheap and informative. — [AOM](https://journals.aom.org/doi/abs/10.5465/AMR.1999.1580438); [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/259034)
- **[PR]** Raffiee & Feng (2014) apply real-options theory to hybrid entry: keeping the job is the "option" that lets the founder learn before committing. — [AOM abstract](https://journals.aom.org/doi/abs/10.5465/amj.2012.0522)
- **[PR, RCT]** Camuffo et al. (2024): the hypothesis-testing protocol raised termination by 9.8 pp and concentrated pivots at one or two; the 2020 study's protocol was delivered as 10 sessions with 16 measurement points over about a year. — [SMJ 2024](https://sms.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/smj.3580); [Management Science 2020](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2018.3249)

### Inferences
- A workable kill rule for a side venture, consistent with all three sources: write the reverse income statement (what monthly income it must produce by when), list the three assumptions that must be true, set a dated milestone to test each with a commitment (money, signed LOI, or scheduled paid pilot), and stop funding time if the milestone fails. The Camuffo result suggests founders who do this *will* kill more ideas, which is the intended effect.
- Review cadence is unmeasured; the RCTs measured roughly every 3–4 weeks. A monthly stage-gate with a short weekly metrics check is a defensible default, not an evidence-based optimum.

### Gaps
- No study found on how often to review side projects or on the effect of review frequency on outcomes.
- Discovery-driven planning has case support but no controlled test of its own.

## Key question 7: Energy and habit — deep work, decision fatigue, open task lists

### Takeaway
The best-supported finding for someone carrying ~35 open tasks is Masicampo & Baumeister (2011): unfulfilled goals intrude and impair unrelated work, and *writing a specific plan* for each eliminated the interference even without doing the task. The classic Zeigarnik memory effect itself does not replicate (2025 meta-analysis ratio 0.99), though the tendency to *resume* unfinished tasks and rumination about pending work do hold. Decision-fatigue/ego-depletion claims rest on a theory that failed large preregistered replication (d ≈ 0.04), so plans should not lean on "decide early in the day." Cal Newport's deep-work rules are practitioner advice, not peer-reviewed.

### Cited Findings
- **[PR]** Masicampo & Baumeister (2011, *JPSP* 101(4):667–683), "Consider It Done!": activating unfulfilled goals produced intrusive thoughts, more mind-wandering, worse reading comprehension and worse anagram performance; participants who formed specific plans for their unfulfilled goals showed none of these effects, "even though the goal remains unfulfilled"; the benefit was strongest for plans people actually intended to use. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21688924/); [author PDF](https://users.wfu.edu/masicaej/MasicampoBaumeister2011JPSP.pdf)
- **[PR, meta-analysis]** Meta-analysis of the Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effects (2025, *Humanities & Social Sciences Communications*): across 38 publications the recall ratio for interrupted vs completed tasks was 0.99 (no memory advantage), vs 1.61 in Zeigarnik's 1927 study; but there is "a general tendency to resume tasks" (Ovsiankina effect). — [Nature HSSC](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05000-w); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/393261073_Interruption_recall_and_resumption_a_meta-analysis_of_the_Zeigarnik_and_Ovsiankina_effects)
- **[PR, meta-analysis]** A 2026 meta-analysis in a work-recovery context finds unfinished work tasks are related to work-related thoughts (rumination, problem-solving pondering) during off-job time. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41554526/)
- **[PR, registered replication]** Hagger, Chatzisarantis et al. (2016, *Perspectives on Psychological Science*), 23 labs, N = 2,141: ego-depletion effect d = 0.04, 95% CI [−0.07, 0.15]; "if there is any effect, it is close to zero." — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1745691616652873)
- **[PR, multilab]** Dang et al. (2021), 12 labs, N = 1,775: small significant ego-depletion effect d = 0.10 (0.16 after exclusions); notes earlier meta-analyses found the effect "severely overestimated due to small-study biases." — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8186735/)
- **[PR]** Leroy (2009): completing a task before switching, especially under time pressure, reduces attention residue and improves performance on the next task. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749597809000399)
- **[PR]** Mark et al. (2008): interruptions raise stress and effort; workers compensate with speed. — [CHI PDF](https://ics.uci.edu/~gmark/chi08-mark.pdf)
- **[PRAC, book]** Cal Newport, *Deep Work* (2016): recommends long uninterrupted blocks and claims even experts sustain only about four hours of deep work a day, drawing on Ericsson's deliberate-practice studies; this is a practitioner synthesis, not a peer-reviewed result. — [Newport on deliberate practice](https://calnewport.com/the-deliberate-creative/); [review noting the four-hour claim](https://medium.com/write-a-catalyst/deep-work-by-cal-newport-book-review-8fd9cb2e1999) **[UNSURE: four-hour figure not traced to a primary study in this pass]**

### Inferences
- The 35 open tasks are a cognitive tax only while they lack a specific plan. The evidence-backed fix is cheap: give every task a dated next step or an explicit "parked until [date/trigger]" note. This directly supports putting the two parked ideas into a written parking file with a review date and otherwise not thinking about them.
- Batch selling into fixed morning blocks (connect-rate data) and finish each block's unit (e.g., a call list) before switching (Leroy). Do not schedule around "decision fatigue."

### Gaps
- No field study found linking open-task-list size to founder output or income.
- Deep-work duration limits have no primary-source backing located here.

## Key question 8: Synthesis — an allocation rule and weekly operating rhythm, stated as a testable plan

### Takeaway
Combining the evidence: protect the one lane that converts activity to cash (commission BD), stack the pre-client agency onto the same selling motion so it costs few extra switches, time-box the marketplace into a pre-sell test with a dated kill criterion, and park the two ideas with written plans. Run selling in fixed morning blocks, answer inbound within the hour, follow up at least three times, ask every meeting for a referral, and review on leading metrics weekly with a monthly stage gate.

### Cited Findings
The plan below is built only from findings already cited in questions 1–7: hybrid entry and survival ([Raffiee & Feng 2014](https://journals.aom.org/doi/abs/10.5465/amj.2012.0522)); attention residue and finishing before switching ([Leroy 2009](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749597809000399)); switch costs rise with unfamiliar rules ([Rubinstein et al. 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11518143/)); activity benchmarks ([Bridge Group 2025](https://www.bridgegroupinc.com/research/2025-sdr-models-metrics-report-the-bridge-group); [Gong](https://www.gong.io/blog/the-hidden-power-of-cold-calling-insights-from-300m-calls)); speed-to-lead ([HBR 2011](https://hbr.org/2011/03/the-short-life-of-online-sales-leads)); follow-up ([Backlinko](https://backlinko.com/email-outreach-study)); referral value ([Schmitt et al. 2011](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/04/Schmitt-Skiera-vandenBulte-2011-Referral-Programs-Customer-Value.pdf)); anchoring ([Orr & Guthrie 2006](https://scholarship.law.vanderbilt.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1727&context=faculty-publications)); hypothesis testing and termination ([Camuffo et al. 2020](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2018.3249); [2024](https://sms.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/smj.3580)); discovery-driven planning ([McGrath & MacMillan 1995](https://hbr.org/1995/07/discovery-driven-planning)); plan-making for open goals ([Masicampo & Baumeister 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21688924/)); income volatility ([SHED 2025](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2025-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2024-income-and-expenses.htm); [JPMC Institute](https://www.jpmorganchase.com/institute/all-topics/labor-markets-and-workforce/report-paychecks-paydays-and-the-online-platform-economy)).

### Inferences
**Allocation rule (hypothesis to test for 12 weeks):**
- ~60% of available work hours to the BD commission lane (the only lane with a buyer who already pays). Rationale: hybrid-entry evidence, Hamilton's earnings gap, volatility data showing persistence stabilises income.
- ~25% to the outsourced-BD agency, *but only as an extension of the same selling motion*: same buyer persona (firms that need pipeline), same call blocks, same CRM. The pitch to a BD prospect can carry both offers, which keeps switch costs low (same rules, same familiarity). Price with a precise first-offer anchor and a monthly retainer plus success fee.
- ~15% to the marketplace, strictly as a *pre-sell test*: a reverse income statement (what it must earn per month by month 6), one Van Westendorp survey to 20–30 studios, then an ask for a paid commitment. Kill or single pivot if fewer than N studios pay/commit by a dated milestone.
- 0% to the two parked ideas beyond a one-page plan each with a review date (removes the intrusion cost; Masicampo & Baumeister).

**Weekly rhythm:**
- Mon–Thu 9:00–11:00 local: outbound block (calls first, email/LinkedIn after), one list finished before switching; no product work in this block. Target for a part-time solo seller scaled from SDR medians: 25–40 touches/day, 1–2 quality conversations/day, 8–10 held meetings/month across BD + agency combined.
- Within 60 minutes: respond to any inbound lead (studios, agency enquiries).
- Each prospect: minimum 3 touches over 2–3 weeks, more than one contact per target firm.
- Every held meeting ends with a referral ask (two names) and a commitment ask (next step with a date).
- Friday 60 minutes: weekly review on leading metrics (touches, conversations, meetings held, proposals out, referrals asked/received, marketplace commitments), plus the plan-making pass over the open task list (every task gets a next step or a park date).
- Monthly: stage gate on each venture's reverse income statement; apply the kill/pivot rule.

**Metrics for the 12-week experiment (per CLAUDE.md rule 1 — hypothesis, metric, time frame, sample):**
- Hypothesis: concentrating ~85% of selling time on one buyer persona (BD + agency) produces more held meetings and more signed revenue than the current spread.
- Metrics: held meetings/month; proposals/month; signed monthly recurring revenue (retainers) and placement fees invoiced; marketplace paid commitments; hours logged per lane.
- Time frame: 12 weeks, reviewed weekly, gated monthly. Sample: all outbound activity in the period (expect ~1,200–2,000 touches).

**Three objections and answers (rule 3):**
1. "The marketplace already works; dropping to 15% wastes the build." Answer: a working product with no payers is the exact false-positive the RCT protocol targets; 15% spent on a pre-sell test is the cheapest way to learn whether it deserves more.
2. "BD commission is too volatile to be the anchor lane." Answer: the volatility data say it stabilises with continuity and with retainer-style structures; the agency retainer is designed to add that base.
3. "Spreading bets reduces risk." Answer: portfolio evidence applies to owners of several *operating* businesses; for one person with pre-revenue ideas, the switching and residue costs are real and the diversification benefit is not shown.

### Gaps
- The percentages are judgement scaled from the evidence, not measured optima; no study gives an optimal split for this profile.
- Scaling SDR medians to a part-time student seller is a linear assumption that the Bridge Group data do not test.
- Spain-specific conversion and fee norms for recruitment BD were not researched.

**Prompt that worked:** "Research the scientific and empirical evidence on how a solo operator with several early ventures should allocate time to maximise income within 6–12 months. Cover task-switching and attention residue, hybrid/serial/portfolio entrepreneurship, sales activity benchmarks and speed-to-lead, the '80% need five follow-ups' claim, referral value, commission/gig income volatility, pricing (anchoring, value-based, Van Westendorp, Mom Test), the Camuffo RCTs and failure base rates, discovery-driven planning and kill criteria, and open-task/plan-making research. Cite every claim with a URL, mark peer-reviewed vs practitioner, flag UNSURE and unsourced popular claims, and end with a testable weekly plan with metrics."
