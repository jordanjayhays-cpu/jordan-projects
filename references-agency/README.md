# Reputations & References — B2B counterparty checking for markets where the database is empty

**Jordan, 2026-09-28, verbatim intent:** for B2B — importers, exporters, sales, anything with large
contracts — so you know who you are doing business with, especially in foreign markets.

---

## The one-line reframe

**This should not be your ninth venture. It should be a second product line inside Placewell.**

Placewell already does the hard part. An international recruitment agency spends every day calling a
candidate's previous employer in another country, in another language, and forming a judgement about
whether what they were told is true. **Checking a counterparty is structurally the same work with a
different subject.** Same people, same phone calls, same market network, same legal entity, same
insurance, different buyer.

Standing it up as a separate company means a new entity, new brand, new website, new bank account and
a tenth thing to lose track of. Standing it up as a Placewell service line means you could sell it
this month.

---

## Is there actually a gap? Yes, and it is a documented one

The incumbents are **Dun & Bradstreet, Bureau van Dijk (Moody's), Experian, CRIF, Creditsafe**, with
**Coface** and **Allianz Trade** selling trade credit insurance as the substitute product. They are
large, they are good, and they sell database subscriptions.

Two weaknesses in that model are stated openly in the industry's own literature:

1. **Emerging markets break the database model.** Basic KYC — document check plus sanctions screening
   — is described as *necessary but insufficient* in emerging markets, because of **data scarcity and
   unclear ownership structures.** A registry that is thin, out of date, or purchasable does not
   produce a usable report no matter how good the software is.
2. **Cost excludes the people who need it most.** "High cost of comprehensive due diligence" is
   listed as a market restraint limiting accessibility for smaller businesses. Enterprise pricing is
   built for banks, not for a mid-sized exporter shipping a €200,000 container.

**The gap is therefore specific:** nobody sells a *human* reference check, in a market where the
database is empty, at a price an SME can actually pay.

That is not a software gap. It is a fieldwork gap — and fieldwork is a service business, which is
what you already run.

## Who buys, and why they buy now

| Buyer | The moment they need you | What they are afraid of |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Exporter shipping on open terms** | Before releasing goods to a new overseas buyer | Non-payment. The container leaves and the money does not arrive |
| **Importer prepaying a new supplier** | Before wiring a deposit | The factory does not exist, or exists and cannot deliver |
| **Company appointing a distributor or agent** | Before signing exclusivity | Handing their market to someone who will not perform, then being unable to exit |
| **Anyone signing a large services contract abroad** | Before signature | Counterparty is a shell, is sanctioned, or is litigating with three former clients |
| **Placewell's own employer clients** | Before they contract with a foreign staffing partner | **This is your warmest channel and it costs nothing to reach** |

The trigger is always a **transaction with a date on it.** That matters commercially: people will pay
a real fee, quickly, when money is about to move. Nobody buys this on a subscription out of general
prudence.

## What the product actually is

Not a database. A **dossier a human produced, with a named analyst behind it.**

1. **Legal existence and standing** — registry extract, incorporation date, filed accounts where they
   exist, licences for regulated activity, current litigation.
2. **Ownership and control** — who really owns it, who signs, whether the person you are dealing with
   has authority. This is where emerging-market registries fail and human work wins.
3. **Sanctions, PEP and adverse media** — the commodity layer. Screen it, but it is table stakes.
4. **Trade references, taken by phone** — three to five of their actual customers and suppliers,
   called by a human in the local language. *This is the product.* Everything else is available
   elsewhere; this is not.
5. **Site confirmation** — someone stands in front of the address and photographs it. Is it a
   factory, an office, or a residential flat?
6. **A written opinion with a grade and a stated confidence level**, plus what you could not verify
   and why.

**Point 6 is the discipline that makes it defensible.** Say what you could not confirm. A report that
claims certainty it does not have is the thing that ends this business.

## Three ways to start — pick one

### SAFE — a Placewell service line, sold to existing clients
- Same entity, same insurance, same team. New one-pager, new price list.
- First customers are Placewell's current employer clients and Amigo Sales prospects.
- Price per report, no subscription.
- *Tradeoff:* slowest growth, near-zero risk, and it could be selling within weeks. Brand stays
  "Placewell Due Diligence," which is less exciting and more credible.

### BOLD — its own brand, one corridor, priced at the SME level  ← **recommended if you want a venture**
- Pick **one corridor** and own it: Philippines ↔ Europe is the obvious one, because that is where you
  have people and where registry data is genuinely weak.
- Positioning: *"We phone their customers. In their language. In three days."*
- Two products only: a **Standard report** and a **Deep report** with site visit and five references.
- *Tradeoff:* needs a brand, a site and a first named client. But one corridor is defensible against
  D&B in a way "global coverage" never is, because they will not send anyone to Cebu for one report.

### WEIRD — sell the guarantee, not the report
- The report is free. You charge for a **conditional opinion**: if you grade a counterparty "clear"
  and they default within 90 days on the transaction you assessed, you refund a multiple of the fee.
- *Tradeoff:* it is the strongest sales proposition in the entire category and it makes you a
  quasi-insurer. **Do not do this without a lawyer and a capped liability schedule.** Named here
  because it is the idea with real teeth, not because it is safe.

## Why this is a better idea than most of your others — stated plainly

- **No product to build.** It is people and process. You can sell it before anything exists.
- **B2B, high ticket, low volume.** Ten clients at €1,500 a year is a real business. Massage Club
  needs hundreds of customers to reach the same place.
- **It uses a competency you already have** rather than a new one. Nothing else in your portfolio is
  true of.
- **The buyer has a deadline**, so the sales cycle is short.
- **Placewell's network is the moat.** Boots on the ground in a market where data is thin is exactly
  what cannot be bought as software.

## Top 3 ways this goes wrong

1. **You clear someone and they defraud your client.** The business-ending risk. Mitigations, all
   non-optional: a written **limitation of liability** in every engagement letter, a stated
   **confidence level** on every grade, never the word "guarantee" outside the WEIRD model, and
   professional indemnity insurance before the first paid report. **Get a lawyer on the engagement
   letter before you sell one.**
2. **You cannot actually get the references.** The whole product is phone calls to a counterparty's
   customers. If people will not talk, you have sold a registry extract at four times the price. Test
   this on **three** dossiers before you sell anything, one of them in the Philippines.
3. **It becomes venture number nine.** Most likely failure of all, and the least dramatic. You have
   eight lanes and one of you. If this does not launch as part of Placewell, it will sit at 80% built
   like Vaya.

## First three steps, in order, none of which need a company

1. **Do three unpaid dossiers.** Real companies, one Philippine, one European, one you already have
   doubts about. Prove you can get references on the phone. This is the only real question.
2. **Show them to two Placewell clients** and ask one question: *would you have paid €400 for this
   before your last new supplier?* Their answer is your business case.
3. **Only then** decide SAFE or BOLD, and get the engagement letter drafted.

**BOARD:** Name one real company you would run dossier #1 on. Not a category — a company.
