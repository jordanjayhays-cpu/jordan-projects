# Checking the person, not just the company

**Jordan, 2026-09-28:** *"i can have a service to call people around the individual we're doing
business with and do a background check."*

---

## Your instinct is the established methodology. It has a name.

This is called **integrity due diligence** or **reputational due diligence**, and the specific
technique you described — calling people around the subject — is the industry's own term of art:
**human source inquiries**.

Kroll describes its own service as assessing reputation *"through review of public records and local
human intelligence and source inquiries"*, and specifically *"targeted local human intelligence and
source inquiries to validate risks identified within public records research."* Mintz Group, Control
Risks, K2 Integrity and Nardello & Co sell the same thing.

**So you are not inventing a product. You are describing a premium service that a handful of global
firms sell at law-firm rates, and proposing to do it in a corridor they under-serve.** That is a much
better position than inventing something.

**It is also the higher-value half of the idea.** A company registry extract is a commodity. A
judgement about whether a named individual honours their commitments is not, and cannot be
automated. This is the part worth paying for.

---

## The bright line, and it matters more than anything else on this page

There are three activities here and they are legally worlds apart. **Confusing them is how this goes
from a business to a criminal problem.**

| | Activity | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1** | **Checking a company.** Registry, filings, ownership, sanctions, trade references about the *business* | **Low.** Largely public-record and commercial-relationship work |
| **2** | **Integrity check on a named individual you are about to contract with**, restricted to their professional conduct and business dealings | **Regulated but legitimate.** This is what Kroll does. Needs a lawful basis, a method, and possibly a licence |
| **3** | **Pretexting** — misrepresenting who you are or why you are calling, to get information | **Illegal in multiple jurisdictions. Do not go near it.** |

### On pretexting, the finding that should stop you cold

**"The person who instructs pretexting can be as legally exposed as the person who carries it out."**

If you hire someone in Manila or Madrid to "just find out" and they pose as a customer, a recruiter,
or a former colleague, **your exposure is the same as theirs.** Professional investigation codes
require members to *reject* instructions that would involve pretexting, impersonation, or unlawful
databases — which tells you how seriously the industry treats it.

**Rule, non-negotiable:** whoever makes the call says who they are and who they act for. *"I'm calling
on behalf of a client considering a commercial agreement with X, and I'd like to ask about your
experience working with them."* If a source will not talk on that basis, the answer is that you could
not verify it. That goes in the report. It does not become a reason to lie.

## THE GATE: you are in Spain, and Spain licenses this

This is the finding you need before you do anything else.

**Several European countries require a private investigator licence, and enforcement is real:**

- **Spain** — private investigators must hold equivalent qualifications to licence-holders, with
  training and sufficient knowledge of Spanish language and law. **You are in Madrid.**
- **Ireland** — since November 2015 it is illegal to employ an unlicensed private investigator, and
  illegal for a foreign investigator to conduct an investigation in Ireland without a licence first.
- **Belgium** — every private investigator must be licensed by FPS Home Affairs.

**UNSURE, and it is the single question to put to a lawyer first:** whether *commercial counterparty
integrity due diligence* — checking a business principal for a client about to contract with them —
falls inside Spain's private-investigator regime, or sits outside it as business intelligence. **Both
readings are plausible and I will not guess.** The distinction usually turns on whether you are
investigating a *person* or researching a *commercial entity*, which is exactly the line this product
straddles.

**One hour with a Spanish lawyer answers it, and the answer determines the whole shape of the
business.** If a licence is needed, the options are: get one, partner with a licensed firm, restrict
Spain-based work to companies only, or base the individual work where you are licensed.

**BOARD:** One hour with a Spanish lawyer — does commercial counterparty integrity due diligence
require a private investigator licence in Spain, and does it matter where the subject is located
versus where I am?

## GDPR — the lawful basis, and what it demands

For any individual in Europe, you are processing personal data. The standard basis is **legitimate
interests under Article 6(1)(f)** — which is available to you, but it is not a box to tick.

It requires a documented **balancing test**: the business benefit named, the data types listed, why
each is necessary, and the subject's rights weighed against it. The test the guidance applies is that
the processing must be **targeted and proportionate to the specific role and responsibilities.**

**What that means in practice, and it is a discipline not a formality:**

- **Business conduct only.** Their commercial track record, litigation, regulatory history,
  directorships, whether they pay. **Not** their health, family, politics, religion, sexuality or
  finances unrelated to the transaction. Those are special-category or plainly disproportionate.
- **Proportionate to the deal.** A €200,000 supply contract justifies more than a €5,000 one. Write
  the justification per engagement, not once for the whole business.
- **Write the balancing test down** for each engagement. If a regulator or the subject ever asks, the
  document is your defence. Without it, "legitimate interests" is an assertion.
- **The subject has rights** — including, in principle, access to what you hold about them. Write
  every report as though the subject will one day read it. That single habit prevents most of the
  trouble in this business.

## What the individual report actually contains

1. **Identity and role** — is this person who they say they are, do they hold the position claimed,
   do they have signing authority.
2. **Directorships and corporate history** — what else they run or have run, and what happened to it.
   **Prior insolvencies and disqualifications are the highest-signal item in the whole report.**
3. **Litigation and regulatory record** — as a party, not just as a name in a filing.
4. **Sanctions, PEP and adverse media** — commodity layer, still required.
5. **Human source inquiries** — three to five people in their professional orbit: former
   counterparties, ex-colleagues, industry figures. Identity disclosed, business conduct only.
6. **A written opinion with a grade, a confidence level, and an explicit list of what could not be
   verified and why.**

**Point 6 again, because on individuals it matters far more than on companies.** A wrong adverse
finding about a person is defamation. A hedged, sourced, evidenced finding is professional work.

## Why this makes the business better, honestly

- **It is the part that cannot be automated.** D&B cannot sell it. Software cannot replace it. It is
  permanently a service.
- **It commands real fees** because Kroll-tier firms charge law-firm rates for it, which sets the
  reference price high — you can undercut dramatically and still price well.
- **Recruitment is the same muscle.** Placewell already calls strangers in other countries and forms
  judgements about whether a person is what they claim. You are re-pointing an existing capability at
  a buyer with a bigger budget.
- **The corridor logic gets stronger, not weaker.** Kroll will not run human source inquiries in Cebu
  or Ilocos for a single €1,500 engagement. You can.

## Top 3 ways the individual version goes wrong

1. **You operate without a licence you needed.** Regulatory, personal, and it can be criminal rather
   than a fine. **Mitigation: the lawyer hour, before the first engagement. Not after the first
   client.**
2. **Someone you subcontract pretexts, and it lands on you.** You are exposed as the instructing
   party. **Mitigation: a written method statement every subcontractor signs, stating identity is
   always disclosed and pretexting is grounds for immediate termination. Keep the calls logged.**
3. **You report an adverse finding you cannot evidence, and get sued for defamation.** **Mitigation:
   grade with a confidence level, attribute every claim to its source, publish what you could not
   verify, and never state as fact what a single unnamed source alleged. Professional indemnity
   insurance before the first paid report, not after.**
