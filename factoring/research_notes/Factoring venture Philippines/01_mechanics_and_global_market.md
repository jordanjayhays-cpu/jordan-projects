## Key Question 1 — Precise definitions of the product variants and where each risk sits

### Takeaway
The industry's reference taxonomy is the Global Supply Chain Finance Forum (GSCFF, members: BAFT, FCI, ICC, ITFA, EBA) 2016 *Standard Definitions*, which splits receivables purchase into receivables discounting, forfaiting, factoring (and variants) and payables finance (reverse factoring). The recourse/non-recourse distinction only concerns *debtor credit risk* (insolvency/protracted default); dilution, dispute, performance and fraud risk almost always remain with the seller in every variant, and the factor's advance-rate reserve is the first buffer against them.

### Cited Findings

**Core taxonomy (GSCFF, 2016)**
- Factoring = "a form of Receivables Purchase, in which sellers of goods and services sell their receivables (represented by outstanding invoices) at a discount to a finance provider (commonly known as the 'factor'). A key differentiator of Factoring is that typically the finance provider becomes responsible for managing the debtor portfolio and collecting the payment of the underlying receivables." Ownership of the receivable passes to the factor; the buyer settles with the factor; "Factoring is normally disclosed to the buyer"; it "is provided with or without recourse depending on aspects such as credit insurance, jurisdiction and market practice." By UNIDROIT (1988) convention factoring includes functions beyond finance: collection, debtor management, and protection against default. — [GSCFF Standard Definitions, 2016](https://ec.europa.eu/digital-building-blocks/sites/download/attachments/42356694/SCF%20Forum%20Supply%20Chain%20Definitions%20-%20Final%20March%202016.pdf?api=v2)
- Receivables Discounting (synonyms: invoice discounting, receivables finance, early payment) = sale of individual or multiple receivables to a finance provider at a discount; "usually offered … to larger corporate clients selling to multiple buyers"; can be with or without recourse ("the act of discounting does not by itself connote whether a transaction is 'with' or 'without' recourse"); "may be disclosed or undisclosed to the buyer (i.e. confidential)"; the provider "may discount up to 100% of the receivables up front or apply a security margin or advance ratio to account for potential dilutions or cover for possible credit deterioration"; if disclosed, collection may be done by the seller as agent or by the provider. — [GSCFF 2016](https://ec.europa.eu/digital-building-blocks/sites/download/attachments/42356694/SCF%20Forum%20Supply%20Chain%20Definitions%20-%20Final%20March%202016.pdf?api=v2)
- Payables Finance (synonyms: reverse factoring, approved payables finance, confirming, supplier finance, buyer-led SCF) = "buyer-led programme within which sellers in the buyer's supply chain are able to access finance by means of Receivables Purchase … typically at a financing cost aligned with the credit risk of the buyer." The buyer gives "an unconditional, irrevocable commitment to pay"; "the finance provider relies on the creditworthiness of the buyer and typically grants the financing 'without recourse' to the seller … It is common that certain elements of recourse are retained against the seller, such as relates to breaches of representations and warranties"; "if there are any dilutions between the buyer and seller, it would be resolved outside of this Payables Finance structure." — [GSCFF 2016](https://ec.europa.eu/digital-building-blocks/sites/download/attachments/42356694/SCF%20Forum%20Supply%20Chain%20Definitions%20-%20Final%20March%202016.pdf?api=v2)
- Forfaiting = "without recourse purchase of future payment obligations represented by financial instruments or payment obligations (normally in negotiable or transferable form)" e.g. promissory notes/bills of exchange. — [GSCFF 2016](https://ec.europa.eu/digital-building-blocks/sites/download/attachments/42356694/SCF%20Forum%20Supply%20Chain%20Definitions%20-%20Final%20March%202016.pdf?api=v2)
- Loan or Advance against Receivables (synonyms: receivables lending, invoice financing, trade receivable loans) = lending "on the expectation of repayment from funds generated from current or future trade receivables and is usually made against the security of such receivables" — i.e. a secured loan, not a purchase. — [GSCFF 2016](https://ec.europa.eu/digital-building-blocks/sites/download/attachments/42356694/SCF%20Forum%20Supply%20Chain%20Definitions%20-%20Final%20March%202016.pdf?api=v2)

**Recourse vs non-recourse (World Bank / Klapper)**
- "In non-recourse factoring, the lender not only assumes title to the accounts, but also assumes most of the default risk because the factor does not have recourse against the supplier if the accounts default. Under recourse factoring, on the other hand, the factor has a claim (i.e., recourse) against its client (the 'borrower') for any account payment deficiency. Therefore, losses occur only if the underlying accounts default and the borrower cannot make up the deficiency." — [Klapper, World Bank WPS3593 (2005)](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/bitstreams/87e35f41-2ac8-5566-916b-1e7549b807a5/download)
- "A factor will typically advance less than 100% of the face value of the receivable even though it takes ownership of the entire receivable. The difference … creates a reserve held by the factor. This reserve will be used to cover any deficiencies in the payment of the related invoice. Thus, even in non-recourse factoring there is risk sharing between the factor and the client in the form of this reserve account." The reserve is a liability of the factor to the client, so the client is exposed to the factor's own insolvency. — [Klapper 2005](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/bitstreams/87e35f41-2ac8-5566-916b-1e7549b807a5/download)
- "In developed countries it appears that factoring is more frequently done on a non-recourse basis. In Italy, for example, 69% of all factoring is done on a non-recourse basis (Muschella 2003) … a study of publicly traded firms in the U.S. found that 73% of firms factored their receivables on a non-recourse basis … Since in emerging markets it is often problematic to assess the default risk of the underlying accounts, most factoring is done on a recourse basis." — [Klapper 2005](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/bitstreams/87e35f41-2ac8-5566-916b-1e7549b807a5/download)
- Notification vs non-notification: "factoring can be done on either a notification or non-notification basis. Notification means that the buyers are notified that their accounts … have been sold." — [Klapper 2005](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/bitstreams/87e35f41-2ac8-5566-916b-1e7549b807a5/download)
- FCI's GIAR product categories are literally labelled "Recourse Factoring (without credit protection)", "Non-recourse Factoring (with credit protection)", "Reverse Factoring or Supplier Finance", "Invoice Discounting", plus "Collections", "Non-Notification Factoring" and "Islamic Factoring" in the member turnover table. — [FCI Annual Review 2025, GIAR 2024, pp.24-29](https://fci.nl/en/media/34571/download?attachment)

**What non-recourse actually covers (practitioner sources)**
- US practitioner guide (trucking-focused): non-recourse means the factor "absorbs the loss if your customer becomes legally insolvent" (e.g. bankruptcy filing) during the covered period; coverage may be capped per account. Excluded: "disputes, short-pays, offsets, missing or late proof of delivery, delivery errors, missing documentation, breach of contract, and fraud." Recourse: if the customer does not pay within the set window (often 60–120 days; "often 90 days") the seller must "buy back the invoice or replace it with another eligible invoice." — [FreightWaves, Recourse vs Non-Recourse Factoring](https://www.freightwaves.com/checkpoint/recourse-vs-non-recourse-factoring/)
- UK practitioner guide: "Most UK providers cover formal insolvency only (liquidation, administration, or CVA). Customers who refuse to pay or dispute invoices are generally not covered. Full non-recourse, covering any non-payment after a fixed period, is described as rare and significantly more expensive"; typical bad-debt cover up to 90% of insolvent debts; recourse window typically 90–120 days past due. — [ExpertSure UK](https://www.expertsure.com/uk/business-finance/recourse-and-non-recourse-factoring/)
- "Limited/partial recourse" and "modified recourse" are used in the market for structures where recourse is capped (e.g. to a guarantee amount or first-loss) or applies only to defined events; GSCFF notes "there are many situations where recourse or limited recourse is maintained" and IFRS commentary treats "factoring with guarantee" (recourse capped at a guarantee amount) as "the most common arrangement". — [GSCFF 2016](https://ec.europa.eu/digital-building-blocks/sites/download/attachments/42356694/SCF%20Forum%20Supply%20Chain%20Definitions%20-%20Final%20March%202016.pdf?api=v2); [CPDbox IFRS debt factoring](https://www.cpdbox.com/ifrs-debt-factoring/)

**Export (two-factor) factoring**
- FCI two-factor model: an export factor in the seller's country and an import factor in the buyer's country; the import factor assesses the buyer, takes the buyer credit risk and collects; governed by FCI's General Rules for International Factoring (GRIF) and the Inter-Factor Agreement, which "is in effect a service and guarantee contract between the Export Factor and the Import Factor." — [FCI, International factoring – how does it work](https://fci.nl/international-factoring-how-does-it-work)
- "Payment Under Approval" (PUA): if an approved receivable is still unpaid at day 90 after due date, the import factor must pay the export factor the full purchase price of that receivable. — [Trade Treasury Payments on FCI cross-border framework](https://tradetreasurypayments.com/articles/fci-launches-cross-border-trade-finance-framework-to-close-the-sme-financing-gap-and-what-this-means-for-business) (single secondary source; verify against GRIF text)
- Klapper's description: the seller's factor pays the seller 70% of face value, and the import factor "for a fee, will take on the responsibility of collecting the amount due from the buyer." — [Klapper 2005](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/bitstreams/87e35f41-2ac8-5566-916b-1e7549b807a5/download)
- Scale: among FCI members, two-factor export business was ~6% of members' export factoring volume in 2024 (direct export 94%); import factoring was 2% of FCI members' total, of which 55% two-factor. FCI's "Four Corners" two-factor model volume in the Americas was EUR 5.05bn in 2024. — [FCI Annual Review 2025 pp.13, 29](https://fci.nl/en/media/34571/download?attachment)

**Risk allocation table (synthesised from GSCFF 2016 risk lists and the practitioner sources above)**

| Variant | Debtor credit (insolvency) risk | Late-payment/timing risk | Dilution (credit notes, offsets, disputes) | Fraud (fake/duplicate invoices) | Disclosed to debtor? | Who collects |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Recourse factoring | Seller (factor charges back after 60–120 days) | Seller (interest accrues; chargeback) | Seller | Seller (plus factor's own loss if seller cannot repay) | Usually yes | Factor |
| Non-recourse factoring | Factor (often insured; usually insolvency only, capped per debtor) | Usually seller/shared (protracted default cover is rarer and pricier) | Seller | Seller | Usually yes | Factor |
| Limited/partial/modified recourse | Shared (capped guarantee, first-loss or defined events) | Seller | Seller | Seller | Varies | Factor |
| Maturity factoring (no advance; factor pays at due date/average maturity) | Factor if non-recourse | Factor (pays on fixed date) | Seller | Seller | Yes | Factor |
| Advance factoring | As per recourse/non-recourse; funds advanced on assignment | — | Seller | Seller | Yes | Factor |
| Confidential / undisclosed invoice discounting | Usually seller (recourse) | Seller | Seller | Seller — highest exposure (no debtor verification) | No | Seller as agent |
| Reverse factoring / payables finance | Factor takes *buyer* (anchor) risk, non-recourse to supplier | Buyer pays at fixed maturity | Resolved outside programme; buyer's irrevocable approval removes it | Low (buyer-confirmed invoices) | Buyer initiates | Buyer pays provider at maturity |

GSCFF's factoring risk list and mitigants: buyer default/insolvency (credit assessment, credit insurance); existence of valid invoices (sampling, individual verification); concentration (limits); factorability (exclude construction/stage payments, perishables, long warranties); dilutions (security margin/advance ratio, reserve); bans on assignment (waivers); seller insolvency "claw-back" and co-mingling (collection account in the finance provider's name); seller fraud by inflating or inventing invoices (transaction verification, credit controls); double financing (security interest in receivables, KYC, perfecting the assignment). — [GSCFF 2016](https://ec.europa.eu/digital-building-blocks/sites/download/attachments/42356694/SCF%20Forum%20Supply%20Chain%20Definitions%20-%20Final%20March%202016.pdf?api=v2)

### Inferences
- For an emerging-market start-up factor, "non-recourse" in practice means buying insolvency risk on a vetted, limited debtor list — it does not mean buying dispute, dilution or fraud risk. The economic choice is therefore less "recourse vs non-recourse" than "which debtors can I underwrite (or insure) and at what reserve."
- Non-notification/confidential structures are the fraud-prone end of the spectrum (FCI itself notes "most of the largest fraud cases in traditional factoring over the past two decades involved non-notification services" — [FCI](https://fci.nl/en/news/after-best-year-factoring-century-whats-ahead?language_content_entity=en)); a new entrant should default to disclosed, verified, notified structures.

### Gaps
- The GSCFF 2021 update to the definitions (clarifying payables finance) was announced but I could not confirm its final text; the 2016 document remains the cited standard.
- I did not obtain the GRIF text itself to confirm the PUA 90-day mechanics in FCI's own wording.

## Key Question 2 — Pricing structures and factor economics (advance rates, fees, non-recourse premium, yields/margins)

### Takeaway
Pricing has two legs — a service/factor fee on invoice face value (roughly 0.5–3% of turnover in the UK; 1–5% per 30-day cycle in US SME/trucking factoring) plus a funding charge on advances (base + 1.5–3% in the UK) — with non-recourse adding roughly 0.5–1 pt of turnover (UK) or ~1–2 pts per invoice cycle (US). Advance rates cluster at 80–90% (non-recourse slightly lower). Hard data on factor-level gross yields and net margins is thin; the one emerging-market sector datapoint found (Turkey 2021) shows sector ROE 18.2%, NPL 2.8%, cost of risk 1.0%.

### Cited Findings

**Advance rates**
- GSCFF: provider "may discount up to 100% of the receivables up front or apply a security margin or advance ratio to account for potential dilutions." — [GSCFF 2016](https://ec.europa.eu/digital-building-blocks/sites/download/attachments/42356694/SCF%20Forum%20Supply%20Chain%20Definitions%20-%20Final%20March%202016.pdf?api=v2)
- Klapper (2005): "most factors offer sellers financing up to 70% of the value of an account receivable and pay the remaining 30% – less interest and service fees – when payment is received." — [Klapper 2005](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/bitstreams/87e35f41-2ac8-5566-916b-1e7549b807a5/download)
- US (2025–26 practitioner): recourse 80–95%; non-recourse 80–90%; reserve/holdback 5–20%. — [FreightWaves](https://www.freightwaves.com/checkpoint/recourse-vs-non-recourse-factoring/)
- UK: 80–90% for both recourse and non-recourse (Novuna, Close Brothers, RBS up to 90%). — [ExpertSure UK](https://www.expertsure.com/uk/business-finance/recourse-and-non-recourse-factoring/)
- US SME blog: 80–90% upfront. — [SMB Compass](https://www.smbcompass.com/recourse-vs-non-recourse-factoring-2/)
- Advance rates are cut when dilution is high: "many factoring companies limit advances to 70% to 80% of invoice value when dilution exceeds 20%"; advance rates cut 10–25% on invoices with pending disputes (vendor blog, unsourced). — [Resolve](https://resolvepay.com/blog/statistics-that-expose-hidden-dilution-in-invoice-finance)

**Fees and the non-recourse premium**
- UK structure: service fee 0.5–2% of turnover (recourse) vs 1–3% (non-recourse); discount charge Bank Base + ~2.5% (worked example 6.25%); related guide cites margins of 1.5–3% over base; non-recourse premium 0.5–1% of turnover. Worked example on £500k turnover: recourse service charge 1.25% = £6,250, non-recourse 2.0% = £10,000; total annual cost ~£21,875 vs ~£25,625 (+£3,750). Rule of thumb in the same guide: recourse suits books with no debtor >20% of revenue and historical bad debt <1–2% of turnover; non-recourse suits books where one or two customers exceed 30% of revenue or bad debt >2%. — [ExpertSure UK](https://www.expertsure.com/uk/business-finance/recourse-and-non-recourse-factoring/)
- US trucking/SME: recourse discount fee ~1–5% per invoice cycle, tiered upward as invoices age; non-recourse 3–7% per cycle. — [FreightWaves](https://www.freightwaves.com/checkpoint/recourse-vs-non-recourse-factoring/)
- US trucking (DAT): recourse ~2–4% of invoice value; non-recourse 3–5%. — [DAT](https://www.dat.com/resources/recourse-vs-non-recourse-factoring)
- US SME blog illustrative: 3% (recourse) vs 4% (non-recourse); claims "88% of the invoice financing industry practices recourse factoring, citing the International Factoring Association" (IFA = US association; unverified). — [SMB Compass](https://www.smbcompass.com/recourse-vs-non-recourse-factoring-2/)
- Monthly-rate framing (vendor): 1–3% per month recourse vs 2–5% per month non-recourse. — [NerdWallet summary via search](https://www.nerdwallet.com/article/small-business/recourse-factoring-vs-non-recourse-factoring) (page body not retrievable; figure from search snippet)
- Alternative to non-recourse: standalone trade credit insurance (Atradius, Coface, Allianz Trade) "often costs less than the non-recourse premium"; one UK source cites 0.1–0.4% of insured turnover (search snippet, vendor). — [ExpertSure UK](https://www.expertsure.com/uk/business-finance/recourse-and-non-recourse-factoring/)
- Reverse factoring pricing is "at a financing cost aligned with the credit risk of the buyer." — [GSCFF 2016](https://ec.europa.eu/digital-building-blocks/sites/download/attachments/42356694/SCF%20Forum%20Supply%20Chain%20Definitions%20-%20Final%20March%202016.pdf?api=v2); in Mexico's Nafin programme banks "charge only interest and not service fees" because Nafin absorbs platform and legal costs. — [Klapper 2005](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/bitstreams/87e35f41-2ac8-5566-916b-1e7549b807a5/download)

**Factor-level economics**
- Turkey (sector data via TEB Faktoring investor report): 2021 factoring sector NPL ratio 2.8%, cost of risk 1.0%, sector ROE 18.2% (TEB Faktoring ROE 25.5%). — [TEB 2021 integrated report](https://www.teb.com.tr/Yatirimci/interaktif_2021/en/page/teb-financial-and-non-financial-services-group/index.htm)
- Turkey (TEB Faktoring 2014): ROE 22.93%, ROA 1.27%, interest income the main revenue line. — [TEB 2014 annual report](https://www.teb.com.tr/Yatirimci/interaktif_2014/en/m-1-12.htm)
- Europe: EUR 307bn of funding (advances outstanding) supports ~301,000 clients against EUR 2.479trn of 2024 EU turnover (EUF data) — i.e. funds-in-use ≈ 12% of annual turnover, implying ~45-day average funding. — [FCI Annual Review 2025 p.13](https://fci.nl/en/media/34571/download?attachment)
- UK: ~40,100 businesses used invoice finance/ABL at end-2024 (≈30,200 confidential invoice discounting, ≈9,900 factoring); >£22bn advanced; client turnover >£315bn. — [UK Finance via search summary](https://www.ukfinance.org.uk/our-expertise/commercial-finance/invoice-finance-and-asset-based-lending)
- Market structure (GIAR 2024, FCI members' view): top-5 concentration (CR5) 86% of national markets; bank divisions 41% of players, bank-owned subsidiaries 30%, independents 15%. — [FCI Annual Review 2025 p.26](https://fci.nl/en/media/34571/download?attachment)
- MENA: receivables finance "experiences growth despite high competition and margin pressures from lenders." — [FCI Annual Review 2025 p.14](https://fci.nl/en/media/34571/download?attachment)

### Inferences
- A simple yield model: on a 90%-advance, 60-day invoice with a 2% flat fee + 12% p.a. funding charge, gross yield on funds employed ≈ (2%/0.9) × 6 turns + 12% ≈ 25% p.a. before losses and opex; non-recourse adds ~0.5–1 pt of turnover ≈ 3–6 pts of annualised yield, from which credit-insurance premium (0.1–0.4% of turnover per the UK vendor figure, likely higher in EM) and expected insolvency loss must be paid.
- In emerging markets the funding leg dominates: with policy rates/bank funding costs materially higher than in Europe, the all-in cost to the SME is driven more by the discount charge than by the recourse/non-recourse choice.
- Turkey's 2021 sector numbers (ROE ~18%, cost of risk ~1%) are the best available proxy for a mature EM non-bank factoring sector; the Philippines venture should expect higher cost of risk initially (no debtor history, weaker credit bureau coverage).

### Gaps
- No published, audited factor-level gross yield or net margin data for Southeast Asian factors was found; nor current (2024–25) Turkish BDDK or Türkiye Faktoring Birliği sector ROE/NPL figures (search budget exhausted before reaching them).
- No Philippine factoring rate sheets or BSP/SEC data on financing-company receivables yields were found in this task (another researcher may cover the Philippines market).
- No primary (association) data on the exact non-recourse premium; all premium figures are from vendor/practitioner pages.

## Key Question 3 — Loss rates, dilution, fraud types and mitigation

### Takeaway
Credit losses in well-run factoring are low (Turkey sector cost of risk ~1% in 2021; FCI members report "very low default rates and limited loss" for receivables finance per the ICC Trade Register), but dilution (credit notes, offsets, disputes) is structurally large (vendor estimates 2–15% typical, up to 20–30% in services/small firms) and fraud — fictitious invoices, double financing, payment diversion — is the tail risk that has destroyed fintech factors (Stenn, 2024; Greensill, 2021). FCI members manage this with notification, verification, concentration limits, collection accounts and credit insurance (40% of FCI members insure buyers, covering ~44% of their debtor books in 2024).

### Cited Findings

**Loss and dilution levels**
- Turkey factoring sector 2021: NPL 2.8%, cost of risk 1.0%. — [TEB 2021](https://www.teb.com.tr/Yatirimci/interaktif_2021/en/page/teb-financial-and-non-financial-services-group/index.htm)
- IFC (citing ICC 2021 Trade Register): receivables finance shows "very low default rates and limited loss at time of default." — [IFC Knowledge Guide on Factoring Regulation and Supervision, 2023/24, p.5](https://www.ifc.org/content/dam/ifc/doc/2024/knowledge-guide-on-factoring-regulation-and-supervision-ifc-2024.pdf)
- Dilution (vendor blog, unsourced): "typical dilution rates range from 2% to 15%"; manufacturing 12–18%, services 20–30%; companies under $5m revenue 22–28% vs 10–15% for larger firms; average dispute rates 2–8% of invoice volume, 15–20% in construction/manufacturing; credit memos <5% of invoices "healthy"; non-specific chargebacks >2% of receivables "a serious collection problem." — [Resolve](https://resolvepay.com/blog/statistics-that-expose-hidden-dilution-in-invoice-finance) (treat as indicative only)
- Another vendor cites portfolio dilution "averaging 6–8%" (search snippet, unsourced). — [Digiqt via search](https://digiqt.com/ai-agent/financial-services/invoice-factoring/invoice-factoring-risk-ai-agent-for-invoice-factoring-in-financial-services/)
- Dilution definition used by lenders: "the difference between the face value of invoices submitted and the amount actually collected"; agreements let the lender "adjust advance rates or reduce the eligible ledger if dilution exceeds an agreed threshold." — [Spark Finance glossary via search](https://www.sparkfinance.co.uk/glossary/dilution)
- Fraud statistics are weakly sourced: a vendor claims "44% of companies reported being affected by invoice fraud [in 2024], with an average loss per incident of $22,000" and another "invoice factoring fraud losses … $4.5 billion globally in 2025" — neither traceable to a primary survey. — [Invensis via search](https://www.invensis.net/blog/factoring-companies-invoice-fraud-losses)

**Fraud typology**
- International Factoring Association magazine: fake invoices (transactions that never occurred), double financing (same invoice sold to multiple factors), misrepresentation of creditworthiness; cases cited: Greensill ("billions in losses"), Wirecard (€1.9bn of non-existent assets), TransCare, BHS. Mitigants: cross-verifying invoices with debtors, client due diligence, AI anomaly detection, a centralised invoice registry that alerts an existing financier when another tries to finance the same receivable, linking it to collateral registries, mandatory audits, adoption of the UNIDROIT Model Law on Factoring. — [IFA Commercial Factor magazine](https://magazine.factoring.org/magazine-articles/the-hidden-risks-of-fraud-in-factoring-and-invoice-discounting)
- Double pledging mechanics: "a business pledges, assigns, or sells the same commercial invoice to multiple lenders or factoring companies"; non-notification structures carry the highest exposure. — [Klearstack glossary via search](https://klearstack.com/glossary/invoice-double-financing-fraud)
- US criminal case: "double factoring" — the same invoices submitted to two factors (Amerifactors and Prestige Funding) as part of a $72m fraud. — [US DOJ, M.D. Florida](https://www.justice.gov/usao-mdfl/pr/two-sentenced-prison-over-72-million-fraud)
- Stenn (UK fintech invoice financier, valued ~$900m, c.£16bn financed to ~800 businesses) entered administration 4 Dec 2024 after HSBC Innovation Bank called a £20m revolver on suspicion of fraud: allegedly bogus invoice owners, supplier "fronts" whose purported large-corporate debtors denied any relationship, revenue concentrated in a few "legacy book" clients handled only by senior management, staff unable to recognise >40% of Hong Kong clients, EY had resigned as auditor in 2018; as much as £32m from unknown/potentially bogus sources. Lessons listed: verify suppliers and debtors directly with the large corporates, take auditor resignations seriously, limit concentration, give investors transaction-level transparency, treat credit insurers declining cover as a signal, hold capital to survive a funder pulling a line. — [Trade Finance Global, "Where they went wrong: Stenn and Kimura"](https://tradefinanceglobal.com/posts/where-they-went-wrong-the-collapse-of-stenn-and-kimura); [GTR: Stenn claims it was sold fake invoices](https://www.gtreview.com/news/europe/stenn-claims-uk-trade-finance-firm-sold-it-fake-invoices/)
- FCI: "most of the largest fraud cases in traditional factoring over the past two decades involved non-notification services." — [FCI](https://fci.nl/en/news/after-best-year-factoring-century-whats-ahead?language_content_entity=en)

**Mitigation practice among FCI members (GIAR 2024)**
- 40% of FCI members (45% in 2023) use a credit-insurance company to cover their buyers, with an average 44% (42%) of buyers insured. — [FCI Annual Review 2025 p.26](https://fci.nl/en/media/34571/download?attachment)
- 46% of members accept e-invoices; among those, ~61% of invoices processed are e-invoices. — [FCI Annual Review 2025 p.26](https://fci.nl/en/media/34571/download?attachment)
- FCI members' client books are ~65% SMEs (73% in 2023) — 35% small, 26% medium, 39% corporate; sector weighting manufacturing 50%, distribution 13%, transportation 10%, services 8%. — [FCI Annual Review 2025 p.25](https://fci.nl/en/media/34571/download?attachment)
- GSCFF-listed controls: sampling/individual verification of invoices, concentration limits, reserve against dilution, waivers of bans on assignment, collection account in the provider's name (against co-mingling/claw-back), regular reconciliation of balances with the buyer's records (against "counter-trading"), KYC/AML, perfected assignment/security interest (against double financing). — [GSCFF 2016](https://ec.europa.eu/digital-building-blocks/sites/download/attachments/42356694/SCF%20Forum%20Supply%20Chain%20Definitions%20-%20Final%20March%202016.pdf?api=v2)
- Registry-based mitigation in EMs: Peru's Factrack (SUNAT/Cavali) centralises negotiation and gives "unique and reliable registration" of invoices; 3,483,543 invoices negotiated in 2024 (1,549,161 in 2023; 14,401 in 2016), average payment term 80 days. — [Andina, Apr 2025](https://andina.pe/agencia/noticia-desembolsos-factoring-superaron-los-s-43000-millones-2024-1027470.aspx)
- Chile: mandatory e-invoicing "has stimulated the development of factoring due to the greater security and speed"; the Chilean commodity exchange trades receivables of large buyers on a without-recourse basis under 2007 legal safeguards against encumbrances. — [IFC Knowledge Guide p.16](https://www.ifc.org/content/dam/ifc/doc/2024/knowledge-guide-on-factoring-regulation-and-supervision-ifc-2024.pdf)

### Inferences
- Expected credit loss is a second-order cost for a disciplined factor (~1% of funds employed in Turkey); dilution and fraud are the first-order risks, and both are controlled by structure (disclosed, verified, concentration-capped, collection account) rather than by pricing.
- The Philippines' e-invoicing roll-out and PPSA registry (see Q5) are the local analogues of the Peru/Chile infrastructure that made registry-based fraud control possible; absent a central invoice registry, a new factor must rely on direct debtor verification.

- No primary dilution dataset; all dilution ranges are vendor estimates.

## Key Question 4 — Accounting (IFRS 9 / ASC 860) and legal treatment; why non-recourse matters for seller balance sheets

### Takeaway
Under IFRS 9 a receivable is derecognised only if "substantially all" risks and rewards (chiefly credit risk and, where material, late-payment risk) are transferred; non-recourse sales generally qualify, recourse sales generally fail and are booked as a secured borrowing (cash in, liability up, receivable stays). ASC 860 uses a control/legal-isolation test, so a US-GAAP recourse factoring can still be a sale if the assets are legally isolated and the seller has no repurchase right or effective control. Legally, the key question for an EM factor is whether domestic law treats factoring as a true sale (so factored receivables sit outside the seller's bankruptcy estate) — the core reason the World Bank sees factoring as suited to weak-enforcement jurisdictions.

### Cited Findings

**IFRS 9**
- Decision steps: (1) has the entity transferred the contractual rights to cash flows? (2) has it transferred *substantially all* risks and rewards → derecognise; retained substantially all → keep recognising; neither → does it retain control? The comparison is of the entity's exposure "to variability in the amount and timing of net cash flows" before and after the transfer; "there is no specific guidance … on what constitutes 'substantially' all" so judgement is required; "generally, dispute risk and other legal risks is not considered for the risks and rewards analysis" because it concerns whether the asset exists. "In a 'with recourse' arrangement, all or most of the credit risk remains with the entity. Such an arrangement will almost always fail the risks and rewards tests." Partial recourse can still qualify depending on exposure retained. — [KPMG India, Application of derecognition requirements to a factoring arrangement (2023), via search summary](https://assets.kpmg.com/content/dam/kpmg/in/pdf/2023/05/chapter-2-application-of-derecognition-requirements-to-a-factoring-arrangement.pdf) (direct fetch returned 404; findings from search excerpt)
- Worked entries (CPDbox): non-recourse sale of CU300,000 at 90% → Dr Bank 270,000 / Dr Finance expense 30,000 / Cr Receivables 300,000 (derecognised; fee is a financing cost accrued over the period if material). Recourse sale at 96% → Dr Bank 288,000 / Dr Finance expense 12,000 / Cr Refund liability 300,000; receivables stay on balance sheet; uncollectible amounts taken back reduce the liability against bank; collections by the factor reduce liability against receivables. "Factoring with guarantee" (recourse capped at a guarantee) = continuing involvement, no full derecognition. — [CPDbox, How to account for debt factoring](https://www.cpdbox.com/ifrs-debt-factoring/)
- IFC's regulatory guide (Box 10, "IFRS and Factoring"): in undisclosed factoring with recourse "the collection risk resides with the assignor … the assignee's credit exposure will be with the assignor and the provisioning is calculated using the default risk of the assignor"; in without-recourse (e.g. reverse factoring) the assignee recognises the receivable at fair value net of transaction costs and provisions on the *debtor's* default risk. — [IFC Knowledge Guide p.48](https://www.ifc.org/content/dam/ifc/doc/2024/knowledge-guide-on-factoring-regulation-and-supervision-ifc-2024.pdf)
- FCI GIAR 2024 explicitly attributes the persistence of non-recourse at ~half of product volume to "risk mitigation and off-balance sheet treatment." — [FCI Annual Review 2025 p.24](https://fci.nl/en/media/34571/download?attachment)
- GSCFF: "if Receivable Discounting is executed without recourse to the seller, the expectation of the seller is that the receivable is removed from its balance sheet, subject to a confirmation of the relevant auditor." — [GSCFF 2016](https://ec.europa.eu/digital-building-blocks/sites/download/attachments/42356694/SCF%20Forum%20Supply%20Chain%20Definitions%20-%20Final%20March%202016.pdf?api=v2)

**ASC 860 (US GAAP)**
- Sale accounting requires all three of: (1) transferred assets are isolated from the transferor, "presumptively beyond the reach of the transferor and its creditors, even in bankruptcy"; (2) the transferee can pledge or exchange the assets; (3) the transferor does not maintain effective control (e.g. via an agreement that both entitles and obliges it to repurchase before maturity, or a unilateral right to reclaim specific assets other than a clean-up call). Otherwise it is a secured borrowing. In the Journal of Accountancy example, a 90-day chargeback of unpaid accounts to the seller (title stays with the factor) did *not* defeat sale treatment. — [Journal of Accountancy, ASC 860 sidebar](https://www.journalofaccountancy.com/news/2011/aug/20113992sidebars.html)
- Deloitte: "although transfers of receivables with recourse may meet the conditions in ASC 860-10 for sale accounting, the recourse provisions must be considered in the derecognition analysis"; factoring "often [involves] various forms of the transferor's continuing involvement (e.g., servicing, guarantees, other forms of recourse)." A 2017 SEC comment letter (Noah Group) pushed a significant-recourse arrangement into secured-borrowing treatment. — [Deloitte DART ASC 860 roadmap §1.2 via search summary](https://dart.deloitte.com/USDART/home/codification/broad-transactions/asc860-10/roadmap-transfers-financial-assets/chapter-1-overview/1-2-types-transfers); [Noah Group SEC response](https://ir.noahgroup.com/node/10376/html)

**Legal treatment (true sale, bankruptcy remoteness, registries)**
- Klapper: "A key legal issue is whether a financial system's commercial law recognizes factoring as a sale and purchase. If it does, then creditor rights and enforcement of loan contracts diminish in importance for factoring because factors are not creditors … factored receivables would not be part of the bankruptcy estate." Tax impediments common in EMs: interest on factoring not tax-deductible like bank interest, VAT charged on the whole transaction rather than the service fee, stamp duty on each factored receivable, burdensome prudential rules on non-deposit-taking factors, capital controls preventing non-banks holding FX accounts for cross-border assignments. — [Klapper 2005](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/bitstreams/87e35f41-2ac8-5566-916b-1e7549b807a5/download)
- UNIDROIT Model Law on Factoring (MLF) adopted 2023: "a comprehensive, standalone private law framework for the creation, perfection, registration, priority, and enforcement of transfers of receivables, both in 'security' as well as outright (sales)." IFC notes newly enacted factoring laws (UAE; leasing+factoring laws in Egypt, Turkey) and reform projects under way in "Azerbaijan, Georgia, Jordan, the Philippines, Ukraine, Uzbekistan, West Bank and Gaza" that combine private-law and regulatory rules. — [IFC Knowledge Guide pp.17-18](https://www.ifc.org/content/dam/ifc/doc/2024/knowledge-guide-on-factoring-regulation-and-supervision-ifc-2024.pdf)
- IFC Knowledge Guide policy options include a governance framework, a general prohibition on factoring without authorisation, and prudential provisioning; it notes "factoring companies are more commonly required to comply exclusively with accounting standards" rather than bank-style capital rules, and cites a jurisdiction requiring factors to put ≥10% of annual net profit into a statutory reserve until it equals 50% of capital. — [IFC Knowledge Guide pp.47-48](https://www.ifc.org/content/dam/ifc/doc/2024/knowledge-guide-on-factoring-regulation-and-supervision-ifc-2024.pdf)
- IAS 7 / IFRS 7 amendments on supplier-finance (reverse factoring) disclosures took effect for periods beginning 1 Jan 2024 (buyer must disclose terms, carrying amounts and ranges of payment due dates) — relevant to anchor-buyer appetite for reverse-factoring programmes. — [IFRScommunity factoring page via search summary](https://ifrscommunity.com/knowledge-base/factoring/) (page returned 403 on fetch; verify wording)

### Inferences
- Non-recourse matters to sellers for leverage ratios and covenants (receivables and debt both leave the balance sheet), which is why large/listed sellers and SCF anchor-buyers prefer it, while SMEs in EMs care more about cash and cost than accounting optics.
- For the factor, recourse vs non-recourse flips the credit exposure from debtor to seller (IFC Box 10): a recourse factor is effectively an SME lender secured on receivables; a non-recourse factor is a buyer-credit underwriter. This should drive which credit data the venture invests in (bureau/registry data on buyers vs. financials on sellers).

### Gaps
- The Philippine-specific status of the factoring/secured-transactions law reform IFC references (and of PFRS 9 = IFRS 9 application to Philippine factors) was not researched here.
- I did not retrieve the IFRS 9 paragraphs (3.2.6, B3.2.1–B3.2.17) directly; findings rely on Big-Four/IFC commentary.

## Key Question 6 — Why factoring matters more in developing markets (SME finance gap, weak collateral law, long payment terms; World Bank / IFC / ADB views)

### Takeaway
The IFC/World Bank put the formal MSME finance gap in 119 EMDEs at USD 5.7trn (19% of GDP; demand USD 10.3trn vs supply USD 4.6trn, 2019 data), rising to ~USD 8trn with informal firms, and estimate up to ~20% of that gap sits in factoring/SCF-type instruments; the ADB sizes the trade finance gap at USD 2.5trn (~10% of global trade) with SME rejection rates of 41%. The World Bank's core argument (Klapper 2005/06) is that factoring lets opaque, high-risk SMEs borrow on the credit of their stronger buyers and, because receivables are *sold* rather than pledged, works even where collateral enforcement and bankruptcy regimes are weak — provided the law recognises true sale and credit information on buyers exists.

### Cited Findings

**MSME finance gap**
- "The MSME finance gap now stands at $5.7 trillion – a number that swells to $8 trillion when informal enterprises are included. In emerging markets, 70% of MSMEs lack adequate financing"; MSMEs are >90% of firms, ~70% of employment and ~50% of GDP worldwide. — [IFC FIG MSME factsheet, Oct 2025](https://www.ifc.org/content/dam/ifc/doclink/latest/msme-s-factsheet-ifc-financial-institutions-group.pdf)
- Methodology: across 119 EMDEs the gap is ~US$5.7trn = 19% of GDP and 20% of private-sector credit; potential demand US$10.3trn vs supply US$4.6trn; underlying data 2019; informal enterprises add ~US$2.1trn of demand. — [SME Finance Forum, MSME Finance Gap (Mar 2025)](https://www.smefinanceforum.org/news/11526737); the World Bank said updated 2023-based estimates were expected by Nov 2025 — not found in this research. — [World Bank SME finance topic page](https://www.worldbank.org/en/topic/smefinance)
- IFC estimate that "up to 20% of the $5.7 trillion-plus SME finance gap is locked in factoring and supply chain instruments" and "receivables account for about 40% of SME total assets on average." — [IFC speech launching the Factoring Knowledge Guide, FCI 2023 (search excerpt; PDF 404 on fetch)](https://www.smefinanceforum.org/sites/default/files/QS%20Speech-IFC%20Knowledge%20Guide-FCI%20Annual%20Meetings_FINAL.pdf)
- IFC MSME Finance Platform (May 2024): up to US$4bn from IFC's own account plus a Catalytic First Loss Guarantee aiming to crowd in another US$4bn; IFC FIG MSME committed portfolio US$18.2bn. — [IFC MSME factsheet](https://www.ifc.org/content/dam/ifc/doclink/latest/msme-s-factsheet-ifc-financial-institutions-group.pdf)

**Trade finance gap (ADB)**
- ADB Global Trade Finance Gap Survey (9th ed., released Jan 2026; data 2023–2025 from >110 providers covering up to a third of the market): gap US$2.5trn in 2025, unchanged from 2023 (USD 1.5trn in 2015; USD 1.7trn in 2020); ~10% of global trade (down from 10.6%); SME rejection rate 41% vs 40% for large/mid-cap corporates ("first time" near-parity, "requires more research"); >80% of banks have dedicated SME strategies; 80% expect demand to rise; recommendations: expand the Trade Finance Register, digitalise trade by 2030, scale SCF, develop deep-tier SCF using anchor buyers' credit. — [ADB news release](https://www.adb.org/news/demand-trade-finance-rise-amid-supply-chain-realignment-adb-report); [ADB publication page](https://www.adb.org/publications/adb-global-trade-finance-gap-survey); [IFC Knowledge Guide fn.18 for 2020 figure](https://www.ifc.org/content/dam/ifc/doc/2024/knowledge-guide-on-factoring-regulation-and-supervision-ifc-2024.pdf)
- Critique: the survey over-represents large banks and may measure a compliance (KYC) gap more than a financing gap. — [Trade Finance Global](https://www.tradefinanceglobal.com/posts/the-global-trade-finance-gap-is-still-2-5-tn-so-what/)
- IFC: the trade finance gap "doubled between 2017 and 2025 to $2.5 trillion"; IFC raised its Global Supply Chain Finance Program from US$1bn to US$3bn; IFC–WTO studies find SCF "scarcer than letters of credit or trade loans" in emerging markets; ~half of global trade is supply-chain trade. — [IFC interview 2025](https://www.ifc.org/en/interviews/2025/scaling-up-supply-chain-finance-could-unlock-billions-for-sme-s)

**World Bank / IFC analytical case for factoring in EMs**
- Klapper (WB WPS3593, 2005; JBF 2006): factoring "allows high-risk suppliers to transfer their credit risk to their high-quality buyers"; "may be particularly useful in countries with weak judicial enforcement and imperfect records of upholding seniority claims because receivables are sold, rather than collateralized, and factored receivables are not part of the estate of a bankrupt SME"; panel of 48 countries (479 obs.): factoring/GDP is higher with GDP per capita, growth and the credit-information index; creditor rights index *not* significant. Factoring/GDP averaged 1.01% in middle-income vs 2.56% in high-income countries. Reverse factoring "can mitigate the problem of borrowers' informational opacity … if only receivables from high-quality buyers are factored." — [Klapper 2005](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/bitstreams/87e35f41-2ac8-5566-916b-1e7549b807a5/download); [abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/wbkwbrwps/3593.htm)
- Nafin (Mexico) case: state development bank's online reverse-factoring platform grew from 2% of Mexican factoring in 2001 to 60% in 2004; all factoring non-recourse; "multi-bank" competition among lenders; Nafin bears platform and legal costs so banks charge only interest. — [Klapper 2005](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/bitstreams/87e35f41-2ac8-5566-916b-1e7549b807a5/download)
- IDB Invest (2026) firm-level evidence from Mexican reverse-factoring platform data (2021–24): access to RF is associated with a statistically significant increase in MSME sales (~27% per summary), mainly via serving more active clients; sustained use needed; descriptive evidence RF lowers financing costs but does not change payment terms. — [IDB Invest](https://idbinvest.org/en/publications/impact-reverse-factoring-msmes-firm-level-evidence-mexico)
- IFC Knowledge Guide (2023/24): "factoring enables high-risk MSMEs to transfer credit risk to high-quality buyers"; "the factoring industry is larger in economies with more developed legal, regulatory, and institutional frameworks"; during COVID global trade receivables were "comparable to that of outstanding corporate bonds" (BIS); Africa's factoring grew from <€18bn (2015) to >€22bn (2018), hindered by legal uncertainty; domestic factoring = 79% of global volume (UNIDROIT); e-invoicing mandates in Latin America enabled receivables exchanges; the Philippines is among the jurisdictions with factoring-law reform projects. — [IFC Knowledge Guide pp.5,16-18](https://www.ifc.org/content/dam/ifc/doc/2024/knowledge-guide-on-factoring-regulation-and-supervision-ifc-2024.pdf)
- FCI's SME framing: "SMEs often wait 30, 60, or even 90 days to receive payment"; factoring/SCF "are based on the buyer's creditworthiness, not the SME's"; non-recourse shields SMEs from bad debt in downturns. — [FCI Annual Review 2025 p.20](https://fci.nl/en/media/34571/download?attachment)
- Emerging-region constraint highlighted by FCI members: "limited access to credit insurance and risk mitigation tools … hinder expansion, especially in emerging regions"; Africa faces "limited credit insurance, legal inconsistencies, and political risks." — [Trade Treasury Payments, FCI Annual Review 2026](https://tradetreasurypayments.com/articles/fci-annual-review-2026-a-deep-dive-into-the-factoring-industry)
- Philippines legal enablers (secondary): the PPSA registry lets MSMEs "leverage receivables and other movable assets as collateral, making factoring a more secure and accessible financing tool"; factoring is not a standalone licence but falls under the SEC-regulated "financing company" regime, now open to 100% foreign ownership. — [Daily Tribune, Mar 2025](https://tribune.net.ph/2025/03/21/factoring-unlocking-msme-growth-in-service-economy); [NDV Law](https://ndvlaw.com/factoring-companies-the-sec-regulatory-setup-for-foreign-investment-in-invoice-discounting-in-the-philippines/)

### Inferences
- The Klapper result that credit *information* (not creditor rights) predicts factoring depth is the single most actionable finding for a Philippine venture: the binding constraint is buyer-level credit data and invoice verifiability, which argues for starting with reverse-factoring / anchor-buyer programmes (Nafin/TReDS model) where only a few buyers must be underwritten.
- IFC's "20% of the gap is receivables-type" implies a ~USD 1.1trn global EM opportunity in receivables finance; the Philippine share is unquantified but the First Circle/IFC activity (Q7) shows DFIs see it as under-served.

### Gaps
- Updated (2023-data) IFC MSME finance-gap figures, and the Philippines-specific MSME gap number, were not retrieved (older IFC figure of US$2.0bn as of 2017 is cited by a fintech alliance — likely outdated).
- The ADB survey's breakdown of rejection reasons (KYC/AML, collateral) and any Southeast Asia-specific figures were not obtained (only the press release was readable).

## Key Question 7 — Emerging business models (fintech marketplaces, embedded B2B finance, securitisation) and how they change the recourse/non-recourse choice

### Takeaway
Three models are reshaping EM receivables finance: (1) regulator-sponsored or DFI-backed receivables exchanges (India TReDS, Mexico Nafin, Chile, Peru Factrack) that are non-recourse by design because the anchor buyer confirms the invoice; (2) fintech invoice-financing originators funded by bank lines and, increasingly, receivables securitisation (IFC's first Philippine SME securitisation, up to US$110m for First Circle, 2026; Stenn's US$200m Natixis programme before its 2024 collapse); (3) embedded finance in B2B marketplaces/AP platforms where the platform itself absorbs buyer credit risk. Securitisation and marketplace models push toward non-recourse/true-sale structures (needed for bankruptcy-remote SPVs and investor appetite) but shift the critical risk to origination integrity — exactly where Stenn and Greensill failed.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- Securitisation and DFI funding effectively force a non-recourse/true-sale architecture at the SPV level (bankruptcy-remote, derecognised by the originator), but the originator usually retains first-loss/mezzanine and reps-and-warranties recourse — so for a Philippine fintech factor the practical model is "recourse to the SME on the front end, non-recourse true sale to the SPV on the funding end," with the originator's equity as the risk cushion.
- Anchor-buyer (reverse) programmes are the cheapest route to genuine non-recourse economics in a market with thin buyer credit data, because the buyer's irrevocable approval removes dilution and most fraud risk; this is the model every successful EM platform (Nafin, TReDS, Factrack) used.
- The Stenn/Greensill failures mean institutional funders in 2025–26 will demand debtor-level verification and transaction transparency from any new originator — a cost of doing business that favours disclosed, notified, registry-verified structures.
