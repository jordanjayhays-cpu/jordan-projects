# Ma'at

Research notes on the ancient Egyptian concept, for writing. Sources at the bottom.
Everything here is sourced; the things I could not verify are marked as such.

## The word

Egyptian **mꜣꜥt**, pronounced roughly *moo-at*, usually written Ma'at or Maat.
It is both a goddess and an idea, and the idea came first in importance.

There is no English word for it. It covers, all at once:

- the order of the cosmos, stars and seasons
- truth, in the sense of what is actually the case
- justice, in the sense of a fair ruling
- the right conduct of an ordinary person
- straightness, levelness, the flat base of a thing

Scholars translate it as order, truth, justice, rightness, balance, true witness,
depending on the sentence, and all of those are partial. The important point for
writing is the one underneath: **Egyptian had no word for justice that was separate
from the word for the way the universe actually is.** A corrupt judge and a river
that floods at the wrong time are the same category of wrong.

Siegfried Morenz and others note it describes at once "the relation between the
government and the gods, the standard established for the Netherworld, and the
ultimate force operating the cosmos."

## Its opposite: isfet

Ma'at is always paired against **isfet**: chaos, falsehood, violence, disorder.

Worth noting that this is not good versus evil in the later Christian sense. It is
order versus disorder, and the Egyptians did not think order had won permanently. It
had to be re-made. The sun had to be dragged through the underworld every night and
come back. Isfet concentrates on exactly the vulnerable moments: the birth of the
creator, the first sunrise, the rebirth of the sun god. Order is not a state, it is
a daily act of maintenance, and it can lose.

## Ma'at is something you do, not something you believe

This is the part that separates it from most of what we call ethics.

The formula in the texts is imperative: **"Speak Ma'at, do Ma'at."** From *The Tale
of the Eloquent Peasant*:

> Speak Maat, do Maat,
> for it is mighty;
> it is great, it endures.

There is no creed, no belief requirement, no conversion. You are not asked to hold
an opinion about Ma'at. You are asked to do it and to say it. An Egyptian official
did not profess Ma'at, he *performed* it, and the test was whether the widow got
heard.

## Assmann's reading: connective justice

The major modern scholarly treatment is Jan Assmann's *Ma'at: Gerechtigkeit und
Unsterblichkeit im Alten Ägypten* (1990). Two terms of his have entered the
literature:

**Connective justice.** Ma'at is what connects people to each other through acts.
Assmann quotes a Middle Kingdom definition that he calls very serviceable:

> The reward of one who does something lies in something being done for him.

Society holds together because action creates obligation and obligation is
remembered. When solidarity falters, connective justice crumbles. Assmann puts it
bluntly: *Ma'at is not inherently "world order", but solidarity, humanity,
reliability, altruism.*

**Vertical solidarity.** Egyptian society was steeply hierarchical and Ma'at did not
pretend otherwise. The deal was that the strong protect the weak and the weak give
the strong their obedience. Assmann's distinction is sharp and usable: natural
verticality comes from covetousness, the instinctual drive of the heart; the
hierarchical variant is based on solidarity, an awareness of cohesion and
responsibility. Same shape, opposite engine.

This is also the honest part. Ma'at is not egalitarian. It is a hierarchy that
justifies itself by its duty of care, which is a live political idea and not
necessarily a flattering one.

## The weighing of the heart

The famous scene, Book of the Dead **Spell 125**, with the illustration everyone
knows.

The dead person's **heart** is placed on one pan of a scale. On the other is a
single **ostrich feather** of Ma'at. Anubis works the scale, Thoth records the
result, Osiris presides, and 42 judges sit in attendance.

Three details that are better than the popular version:

**1. The standard is a feather.** Not a stone, not a counterweight. The bar is
weightlessness. The heart does not have to be good, it has to be *light*.

**2. The heart is a witness, and it can turn on you.** The Egyptians left the heart
in the body during mummification specifically because of this, while other organs
came out. The heart was understood to hold your character, personality and
intellect, and it would be handed over to the gods to speak. It knows what you did.
It was there.

**3. So people tried to silence it.** This is the detail worth the whole song. A
**heart scarab** amulet was laid over the heart, inscribed with **Spell 30B**, whose
express purpose, in the Oxford Encyclopedia's words, was "to bind the heart to
silence whilst it was being weighed." The spell addresses the organ directly.
Faulkner's standard translation runs:

> O my heart of my mother... do not stand up against me as a witness...
> do not tip the balance against me.

A civilisation built an entire ethical cosmology around truth, and then sold you an
amulet to keep your own heart from testifying. That tension is real and it is
ancient, not a modern joke about hypocrisy.

**What happens if you fail:** the heart is dropped and eaten by **Ammit**, a
composite of crocodile, lion and hippopotamus. This is *not* hell. There is no
torture, no eternal punishment. You stop existing. The "second death" is deletion.
Philosophically this is a long way from Dante and much closer to a modern fear.

## The 42 declarations

Also Spell 125, recited to the 42 judges, one each. Usually called the **Negative
Confession**, and the name matters: they are not confessions of sin, they are
**declarations of innocence**. Not "forgive me" but "I did not."

The familiar ones are what you would expect:

> I have not committed sin. I have not slain men and women. I have not uttered lies.
> I have not been an eavesdropper. I have not been angry without just cause.
> I have not shut my ears to the words of truth.

The ones almost nobody quotes are the interesting ones. From the UCL Digital Egypt
translation of the spell:

> I have not reduced the measuring-vessel, I have not reduced the measuring cord.
> I have not held back water in its time.
> I have not dammed a dam at rapid waters.
> I have not put out the fire in its moment.

And from the Papyrus of Ani:

> I have not snatched away the bread of the child.

Short-weighting a customer, diverting irrigation water from people downstream,
damming a river, taking food from a child. Three thousand years before anyone used
the phrase, the moral test at the gate of the afterlife includes **environmental and
economic justice**, stated as concretely as a trading standards regulation. That is
the single most striking fact in this whole subject and it is almost never the part
people repeat.

Note also what is *absent*: no declarations about belief, worship frequency, or
doctrine. It is almost entirely about what you did to other people and to the
shared world.

## The Eloquent Peasant

*The Tale of the Eloquent Peasant*, Middle Kingdom, before 1800 BCE. Many surviving
copies, so it was popular.

A peasant named Khunanup is robbed by a minor official. He petitions a high official
for justice, nine times, in increasingly magnificent speeches. The joke, and the
point, is that the rustic out-argues the elite on the nature of justice. The high
official deliberately withholds judgment, partly so the beautiful speeches keep
coming and can be written down for the king.

The first petition has a famous image, and a pun that works in Egyptian: *mꜣꜥt*
(justice) against *mꜣꜥw* (fair wind).

> When you go down to the sea of justice
> And sail on it with a fair wind,
> No squall shall strip away your sail,
> Nor will your boat be idle...
> You will not taste the river's evils,
> You will not see a frightened face.

Justice as weather you can sail in. Officials who fail it are called "repellers of
evil" who have sunk to the level of "a wretch of a washerman."

Parkinson's observation is that in this text you cannot separate the value of
rhetoric from the value of justice. Speaking well *is* doing justice. For a rapper
that is not a metaphor, it is the thesis.

## The king offers Ma'at back to the gods

A strange, recursive ritual worth knowing.

The daily temple cult included two symbolic acts: the offering of incense, and **the
offering of Ma'at** — a small figurine of the goddess, presented by the pharaoh (in
practice by priests) to the gods. Kings and queens offered these as a sign of
commitment to maintaining the order that made them legitimate.

The ruler hands order itself back to the gods, as tribute, to prove he is still
keeping it. The one with the most power is the one who has to keep demonstrating
that he is subject to the thing he enforces. Ferguson glosses it as "the return to
the god of his bounty."

## Related ideas elsewhere

Useful if you want to place it:

- **Rta** and later **dharma** (Vedic/Indian): cosmic order that is also moral duty
- **Tao / 道** (Chinese): the way things actually go, which you align with
- **Logos** (Greek): rational order of the cosmos
- **Ubuntu** (Southern African): there is a real scholarly literature arguing Ma'at
  as a predecessor, though the comparison is contested precisely because Ma'at is
  hierarchical and ubuntu is not

For the Chinese cut, **天道** (the way of heaven) is the closest single term, and
**天人合一** is close to the cosmic-plus-human unity. 正义 alone loses almost
everything.

## Things circulating online that are NOT ancient

Flagging these because they dominate search results and would be easy to repeat.

**"The Seven Principles of Ma'at"** — truth, justice, harmony, balance, order,
reciprocity, propriety. This appears on tourism blogs, wellness sites and social
media constantly. I could not find it in any scholarly source: not in Assmann, not
in Ferguson, not in the Egyptological literature I checked. It looks like a modern
synthesis. It is not wrong in spirit, but it is not an ancient Egyptian list and
should not be presented as one.

**"The 42 Laws of Ma'at"** — this is a modern relabelling of the 42 declarations of
Spell 125. The 42 statements are genuinely ancient. Calling them "laws" is not:
they are things the dead say they did not do, not a code that was promulgated.

**Caution on the Papyrus of Ani wordings.** The widely copied English list ("Hail,
Usekh-nemmt, who comest forth from Anu, I have not committed sin") is E. A. Wallis
Budge's Victorian translation. It is out of copyright, which is why it is
everywhere, and it is dated. Modern translations read quite differently. Fine to use
for flavour, but it is not the current scholarly text.

**One open question.** Geraldine Pinch has argued that success at the weighing
depended on mystical knowledge of the true names of the 42 judges rather than on the
deceased's actual moral behaviour, which would make the whole thing closer to a
password than a moral test. Ogden Goelet takes the opposite view: "without an
exemplary and moral existence, there was no hope for a successful afterlife." Both
are live positions. The heart-scarab practice is evidence for Pinch's side.

## Angles that look strongest for a song

Not writing it, just marking what has the most charge.

1. **The heart that testifies.** You are judged by the one organ that was present
   for everything, cannot be coached, and is handed to the court to speak. And
   people bought amulets to keep it quiet. Modern rhyme: everything is recorded,
   and we pay for the right to delete it.

2. **The feather.** The standard is not goodness, it is lightness. What you carry
   is the problem.

3. **"I have not held back water in its time."** A line about water rights inside a
   death ritual, thirty centuries early.

4. **Ammit is not hell.** The punishment is deletion. Non-existence as the worst
   outcome is a very contemporary fear and very un-Christian.

5. **Ma'at must be re-made daily.** Order is not a state you arrive at. The sun has
   to be fought back up every night.

6. **Speaking well is doing justice.** The peasant beats the official with speech,
   and the text treats eloquence and righteousness as the same virtue. That is a
   rapper's thesis stated in 1800 BCE.

7. **The king offers order back.** Power proving it is subject to the thing it
   enforces.
