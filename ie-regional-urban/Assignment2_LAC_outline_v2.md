## The new thesis — use this arc

> **Latin America wrote the theory of its own underdevelopment, acted on it for thirty years, then
> did the exact opposite for thirty more. Growth disappointed both times. That is the real puzzle.**

Four beats:

1. **1950 — the region diagnoses itself.** Prebisch and Singer: the terms of trade systematically
   move against primary commodity exporters. Peripheral economies are locked into structural
   disadvantage. Written at ECLAC, in the region, about the region.
2. **1950s–80s — it acts on that diagnosis.** Import Substitution Industrialisation, the policy
   Ríos's own deck lists under "Breaking Dependency." Protect, substitute, industrialise.
3. **ISI fails.** It produced small, protected, uncompetitive industry and ended in the 1982 debt
   crisis and the lost decade. *(You already have the evidence: the Mexico (C) case from Country
   Economics opens on exactly this — "the misguided fiscal expansion of the late 1970s," the
   "lost decade.")*
4. **1990s — the region does the opposite.** Liberalise, privatise, open, sign NAFTA. Mexico is the
   showcase. **That disappointed too**, and in Mexico's case ended in the 1994 peso crisis.

**So the question is not "why hasn't Latin America tried anything." It has tried both cures.**

### The answer to give

Both ISI and the Washington Consensus operated on **trade and macro policy**. Neither operated on
what the region actually **knows how to make**. The binding constraint is **capability**, not
openness and not protection.

> You cannot protect your way to complexity, and you cannot liberalise your way to it either.
> Capabilities have to be built deliberately.

That single sentence is your deck. It uses his dependency material, his economic complexity
material, and the growth-diagnostics framework, and it explains why thirty years of each policy
produced disappointment.

## The three causes, now in his frameworks

### Cause 1 — Dependency never actually broke (Prebisch-Singer → Economic Complexity)
The region still exports what it digs up and grows. Commodity concentration persists; complexity
has not risen. **Frame it as: Prebisch diagnosed the symptom correctly in 1950, but ISI treated it
by closing the economy rather than by building capability. Complexity is the modern, measurable
version of the same insight** — and unlike Prebisch's, it comes with an index you can plot.

### Cause 2 — The region's growth poles produced backwash, not trickle-down (Perroux, Myrdal, Hirschman)
This is the strongest use of his material and it is sitting right there in his deck.

His slide sets out **trickling-down vs backwash**: brain drain, capital flight to the core, rural
markets undercut, cumulative depopulation, widening disparities. **That list is a description of
Latin America.** The region has the most primate urban structures in the world — Mexico City, Lima,
Santiago and Buenos Aires each dominate their national economies in a way European or US cities do
not. Myrdal's cumulative causation ran, and backwash beat trickle-down.

**Chiapas is your case**, and it is already in your own course materials.

Note his own seminar question, which tells you what he cares about:
> *"Can Growth Pole policy ever durably benefit peripheral rural areas, or does it inevitably create
> backwash effects? Draw on one empirical case."*

**Answer that question inside your deck and you are answering him directly.**

### Cause 3 — Informality as a productivity trap
Not in his deck, but essential and well-evidenced, and it explains why the core itself is
unproductive rather than only unequal. Roughly half the workforce is informal; informal firms stay
small, cannot borrow, never accumulate productivity. **Santiago Levy's mechanism:** financing social
protection through payroll taxes formality and subsidises informality, so policy sustains the trap.

## The recommendations, now with mechanisms from his decks

### Rec 1 — Build capability where the window is open
**Now grounded in Vernon's Product Life Cycle, which is section 03 of his deck.** Stage 3 of the
cycle is standardised production relocating to low-cost locations. **Nearshoring is PLC stage 3
re-running, with production leaving China.** The region does not need to invent this opportunity,
it needs to be chosen.

**And Industrial Location theory tells you what "being chosen" requires.** From his deck: firms are
resource-oriented, input-oriented, market-oriented or **footloose**. Nearshoring candidates are
footloose — minimal transport constraint, so they choose on labour cost, regulatory environment and
reliability. Those are policy variables. **Mexico and Central America are competing on the isocost
side, and that is a thing a government can actually move.**

Precedents: Chile's salmon via Fundación Chile, Costa Rica's shift from bananas to medical devices.

### Rec 2 — Design growth poles that spread instead of drain
**Use his five-stage growth-pole process as the design spec:** propulsive industry → backward and
forward linkages → innovation diffusion → self-sustained growth → **periphery impact, which is where
it either trickles down or backwashes.**

The policy is to engineer stages 2 and 5 deliberately rather than hope for them: local supplier
development requirements, transport and digital connection into the periphery, and **Medellín-style
social urbanism**, which is in his International Cities deck. Medellín is your empirical case for
the seminar question above: a growth pole that was made to spread.

### Rec 3 — Make formality cheaper than informality
Shift health and pension financing off payroll onto general taxation; simplify registration and tax
for small firms. **Mechanism:** today formalising a worker triggers a large per-head cost, so firms
stay small. Remove the penalty, remove the reason to hide.
Precedents to check: Brazil's SIMPLES, Mexico's RIF.
