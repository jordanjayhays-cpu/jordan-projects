## The spine of the argument

Do not present eight problems. Present **one diagnosis with three causes, and three matching
policies.** That is the structure that worked on the Manila report and it fits 20 minutes.

### The diagnosis in one line

> Latin America's problem is not that it failed to accumulate capital, educate people or open to
> trade. It did all three. The problem is that **productivity never followed.**

That single claim is the whole deck. It separates LAC from the usual "poor country needs
investment" story, and it is what the growth-diagnostics framework from the course is built to test.

### Use the binding constraint framework explicitly

Hausmann, Rodrik and Velasco: is growth constrained by (a) low returns to investment, (b) the cost
of finance, or (c) poor appropriability of returns? Walk through the three, rule out the ones that
do not bind, and name the one that does. **Doing this on a slide is the single clearest signal to
Ríos that you used his framework rather than a generic consulting template.**

### The three causes to argue

1. **Low economic complexity.** The region exports few products, and simple ones. Use the Harvard
   Growth Lab's Atlas and the product space. Complexity predicts future growth; LAC's has been flat
   while Vietnam, Poland and Korea climbed.
2. **Informality as a productivity trap.** Roughly half the workforce is informal. Informal firms
   stay small, cannot borrow, do not invest and never accumulate productivity. Santiago Levy's
   argument is the one to cite: social protection financed through payroll **taxes formality and
   subsidises informality**, so the policy itself sustains the trap.
3. **The spatial periphery is disconnected.** Growth concentrates in a few metropolitan cores while
   lagging regions stay outside it. This is where **Chiapas** belongs, which is already a case in
   your own course materials.

Optional fourth if you have room: **premature deindustrialisation** (Rodrik). Manufacturing
employment peaked in Brazil and Mexico at far lower income levels than in Korea or Japan. You also
saw this in the Jones paper for Geopolitics, which cites the same peak-year data.

## Write each recommendation in the same three-part shape

The brief asks for "proposed programs with **impact and implementation**". Use the structure that
worked on the Manila report:

> **Policy.** [The specific instrument.]
> **Mechanism.** [Why it changes behaviour.]
> **Outcome.** [The measurable result, with an indicator.]

### Recommendation 1 — Make formality cheaper than informality
Shift the financing of health and pension coverage from payroll contributions to general taxation,
and pair it with a simplified tax and registration regime for small firms. **Mechanism:** today a
firm that formalises takes on a large per-worker cost, so it stays small; removing that penalty
removes the reason to hide. **Precedents to check:** Brazil's SIMPLES, Mexico's RIF.
**Indicator:** informal employment share, and the share of firms above 10 employees.

### Recommendation 2 — Build complexity where the window is open
A capability-building agency on the ProChile or Singapore EDB model, targeting products "near" the
subregion's existing capabilities in the product space, anchored on the nearshoring shift in US
supply chains. **Mechanism:** diversification happens into adjacent capabilities, not random sectors,
so target adjacency rather than picking glamorous winners. **Precedents:** Chile's salmon industry
via Fundación Chile; Costa Rica's move from bananas to medical devices.
**Indicator:** export complexity index, number of products with revealed comparative advantage.

### Recommendation 3 — Bring the periphery inside the economy
Transport and digital connection to lagging regions, plus Medellín-style social urbanism investment
in excluded urban districts. **Mechanism:** agglomeration benefits only reach people who can
physically or digitally reach the core; otherwise the core grows and the periphery supplies
migrants. **Precedent:** Medellín's cable cars and library parks, which is in your own course deck.
**Indicator:** regional GDP per capita dispersion, travel time to the nearest market of a given size.

## Q&A preparation

All members speak, and the professor asks questions immediately. Prepare these:

- **"Why is this the binding constraint and not the others?"** Have the ruling-out logic ready.
- **"Hasn't this been tried?"** Yes, much of it has, and badly. Know why your version differs.
- **"Who pays for it?"** Have a rough fiscal number and a financing route.
- **"Why would a government with a four-year cycle do a ten-year reform?"** The hardest question.
  Answer honestly: sequence the early wins first, and say which parts need a cross-party pact.
- **"Isn't nearshoring just luck?"** It is an opportunity, not a strategy. The strategy is the
  capability to capture it.
- **"Latin America is not one place."** Agree immediately, and say that is exactly why you scoped
  to a subregion.
