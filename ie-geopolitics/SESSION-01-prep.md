## 1. Friedman — the method

**The claim.** Geopolitics separates what is eternal and long-duration from what is transitory, through
the prism of geography and power. It does not just describe, it predicts, and its predictions usually
run against common sense. Friedman's phrase: geopolitics is "the next generation's common sense."

**The method is deliberately naive.** "Successful forecasting should begin by being stupid." Start from
what is obvious, because clever people skip past the obvious and their forecasting "floats in air
rather than being firmly anchored in reality."

**The chain he builds from that:**

1. Humans are born helpless and need years of nurture, so they form families.
2. A small family is easy prey, so families combine into communities, and division of labour follows.
3. Why trust a relative over a stranger? This is the "eccentric core" of the problem: **love of one's
   own** — obligation you are born into.
4. Against it sits **acquired love** — what you choose. Romeo and Juliet is the case study: a clan you
   are born to versus a person you choose. Protestantism, the Enlightenment and ideology all elevate
   the chosen over the inherited. The last 500 years is the struggle between the two.
5. His sharpest illustration: you are born *American*, but you are a *citizen of the United States*.
   The first is natural and unchosen, the second artificial and elective. "Citizenship and alienage
   are built into the system."
6. **Nationalism is the modern form of love of one's own, and it has not weakened.** The 20th century
   intensified economic rationalism and nationalism *simultaneously*. Interdependence did not dissolve it.

**Then place.** Where you are born matters as much as who you are born to. Place imposes both
capabilities and vulnerabilities: Poland sits between Germany and Russia with no natural defences;
Germany depended on French iron ore; Japan on American steel and oil. "Had French steel been located
one hundred miles to the east... history might have evolved differently."

**Then fear, which is the load-bearing idea.** "Wars originate far less in greed than they do in fear."
Hobbes: you cannot know another's intentions, so where your family's survival is at stake you must
assume the worst, and the worst case justifies striking first. Germany tried exactly that three times
and failed each time. Interdependence *creates* fear rather than removing it, because dependence is a
vulnerability someone else controls.

**Two refinements worth having ready:**

- **Class and shared fate.** The wealthy can insulate themselves from their nation's fate (money held
  abroad, children sent abroad); the poor cannot. So the poor are rationally more risk-averse and more
  nationalist. "In a democratic age, the class struggle is not as Marx envisioned it. It is a struggle
  between the wealthy internationalists and the common nationalists." The long run "is a perspective
  that only the wealthy can enjoy."
- **Clocks.** Societies count in generations, individuals in years. A change that doubles national GDP
  over 30 years can still destroy one engineer's entire productive life. "Focus on the clock."

## 2. Kerbel — the bias

**The claim.** The US debate on China is distorted by an unrecognised *cognitive* bias: applying a
**linear** template to a **nonlinear** system. Not ideology, not self-interest, something more buried.

**The distinction.**

- *Linear* systems are additive (the whole equals the sum of the parts), have identifiable cause and
  effect, and are repeatable and proportional. Hence predictable and manipulable. Planets, ballistic
  missiles.
- *Nonlinear* systems are not reducible, because the parts change and adapt as they interact. The
  international system is a "system of systems."

Western, and especially American, default processing is reductionist: break it up, understand the
pieces, add them back. That works only where the system is genuinely reducible.

**Two illusions this produces:**

1. **Certainty.** Single-outcome forecasts, bounded by "rising China" and "doomed China." The giveaway
   word is *trajectory* — a Newtonian term for smooth continuous motion, so using it "is really just
   another way of predicting continuity or evolutionary change."
2. **Influence.** That the US can pressure, lever or propel China. Both sides of the Normal Trade
   Relations argument assumed NTR was an effective lever; they disagreed only on which way to pull.
   A *New Republic* editorial he quotes ends: "Levers, levers, levers."

**The line most worth memorising:** linear cognitive bias explains **how** single-outcome predictions get
made; the more visible non-cognitive biases (ideological, cultural, organisational, self-interest)
largely determine **what** gets predicted.

**His four nonlinear corrections:**

1. Synergistic, not additive, so resist the urge to simplify. Separating economics from human rights
   in the NTR debate was convenient and artificial.
2. Cause and effect are uncertain, so side effects are inevitable. Confident, contradictory forecasts
   were made about WTO accession, each implying precision that did not exist.
3. **Behaviour cannot be repeated, so arguments by analogy never apply precisely.** Containment of the
   USSR is not a valid template for China. Historical precedent is more useful for its *contrasts*
   than its comparisons.
4. Inputs and outputs are disproportionate, so ripeness and timing decide. Taiwan took four decades
   from 1949; sanctions on Cuba and North Korea have produced no reordering in over fifty years.

**The prescription.** Change the metaphor. Drop the mechanical vocabulary (trajectory, leverage,
inertia, momentum, tipping, delinking) for the life sciences (evolution, adaptation, side effects,
interactions, ripeness). Analysts should think like doctors: drug combinations, allergic reactions,
patient particularity, stage of illness.

**Conclusion, and it is the title of his last section: "Stop Trying to Think Straight."** Nonlinear
thinking will not make China predictable, and linear tools stay useful *provided their imperfections
are acknowledged*. The goal is a blurred picture. He borrows Jonathan Spence: "The more blurred and
multifaceted our perceptions of China become, the closer we may be to that most elusive thing: the truth."

## Why these two are paired

This looks like the argument of the session.

**Friedman predicts. Kerbel attacks prediction.** But they are not simply opposed, and saying so is
probably the cheap answer. Friedman forecasts *constraints and directions* that come from things
nobody chose: place, fear, the need to be born into something. Kerbel attacks *single-outcome point
forecasts* and the belief that you can steer another nation's outcome.

The real tension is narrower, and sharper: Friedman's whole method runs on historical analogy
(Germany and French iron ore, Japan and American oil, Poland's geography), and Kerbel says in terms
that arguments by analogy **never apply precisely**.

## Questions to take in

The syllabus asks for questions, literally: *"If you take notes, end every sentence with a question mark."*
Participation is 20 percent and they cold-call.

- Friedman says geopolitics predicts; Kerbel says possibilities, not prediction, should be the goal.
  Are they doing different things, or does one of them have to be wrong?
- If analogies never apply precisely, what exactly is Friedman doing when he explains 2008 through
  Germany in 1914?
- Friedman argues the wealthy are insulated from their nation's fate and therefore see it differently.
  Does that make senior business leaders systematically unreliable geopolitical forecasters?
- Kerbel wrote in 2004 and refused both "rising China" and "doomed China." Twenty-two years on, which
  bias has aged worse, and does that vindicate him or just make him unfalsifiable?
- Whose clock does a company run on, the individual's or the society's? What follows for a firm
  planning a 30-year investment in a place?
- If fear rather than greed drives states, what is the business equivalent? Do firms pre-empt out of
  fear of rivals' unknown intentions?
