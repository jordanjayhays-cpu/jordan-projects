# It's Easier to Imagine the End of the World

There's a line that follows you around once you've read it. Mark Fisher didn't invent it — he borrowed it, and half the internet has borrowed it since — but he's the one who made it mean something:

**It is easier to imagine the end of the world than the end of capitalism.**

Test it on yourself. You can picture the flood, the fire, the asteroid, the collapse. Hollywood sells you that image four times a year and you buy the popcorn. Now picture a different way of organizing a Tuesday. Not a utopia. Just an ordinary Tuesday where the logic is different.

Harder, isn't it?

That gap — between how easily we imagine annihilation and how badly we imagine alternatives — is what Fisher called **capitalist realism**. Not an ideology you believe. An atmosphere you breathe.

## The trick isn't that you agree

Ideology used to need believers. This one doesn't.

Ask almost anyone whether the system is fair and they'll tell you it isn't. Ask whether it's sustainable and they'll say no. Ask what should replace it and watch the sentence die in their mouth.

That's the whole machine. It doesn't need you to be a fan. It needs you to be **fluent** — to know how rent works, how insurance works, how a résumé works, and to have no equally detailed knowledge of anything else. Fisher's point wasn't that we love capitalism. It's that it has become the water: the invisible default that makes every alternative sound like a student's fantasy.

## It sells you your own rebellion

The sharpest part of Fisher's argument is what happens to resistance.

Anti-capitalism doesn't get censored. It gets *merchandised*. The rebellion becomes a Netflix documentary, the protest becomes a brand campaign, the counterculture becomes a font. Your anger isn't suppressed — it's inventoried, priced, and sold back to you at a markup, and the transaction is counted as growth.

I wrote a line about this before I could explain it:

> *rebellion is sold — your anger, a commodity, your passions controlled*

Nothing has to be banned. Everything just has to be purchasable.

## Then it hands you the bill for your own exhaustion

Here's where Fisher gets uncomfortable, because he was writing about himself.

Depression and anxiety are treated as private malfunctions. Your chemistry. Your mindset. Your failure to optimize. You get a prescription, an app, a mindfulness subscription — all of which are products, all of which locate the fault inside you.

Fisher asked the forbidden question: what if a system that demands infinite flexibility, permanent availability and self-branding from precarious people *produces* the misery it then charges them to treat?

He called it the privatization of stress. You break under conditions built to break you, and then you're billed for the repair — and the repair is another market.

## The doors are painted on

The name of the song is **The Illusion of No Escape**, and that phrase is doing precise work. Not *no escape* — the *illusion* of it.

Fisher never argued that nothing can change. He argued we've been trained out of imagining it, and that the training is the point. Capitalist realism survives because it convinced you the arguing is over. But a world that arrived recently — this arrangement is a few centuries old, a blink — can leave the same way.

Half the locked doors in a life are painted on. The only way to find out which is to walk over and test the handle.

That's not optimism. It's the minimum condition for anything else.

> *to imagine a new, to break the mold, feels like a dream…*

The dream is the tell. When imagining an alternative feels *embarrassing* rather than *difficult*, that's not analysis — that's the atmosphere doing its work on you.

## Where to start, since you asked

Not with a manifesto. Fisher was allergic to those.

Start by noticing the moment your imagination stops. The next time you catch yourself thinking *that's just how it is* — pause there. That sentence is never a description. It's always a decision someone made, wearing the costume of physics.

Then say the alternative out loud, badly, without a plan. Being unable to describe a better world is not proof that none exists. It's proof of what you've been given to describe it with.

Mark Fisher died in 2017. He wrote a small book that keeps getting more accurate. The least we can do is keep testing the handles.
