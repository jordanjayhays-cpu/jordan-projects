## Choice: Cote d'Ivoire (return-maximizing pick; Botswana is the capital-preservation alternative)

Applying the course framework to the data, sub-Saharan Africa sorts into three groups. The first grows fast but destroys real returns through inflation and debt: Nigeria and Ghana post 9.4% and 7.4% growth with inflation of 23% and 14%, Ghana carries public debt of 73% of GDP, and Zimbabwe, Sudan, and South Sudan run inflation above 90%. The second is stable but exposed to the sudden-stop dynamic the Mexico case illustrated: Senegal, Mozambique, and Rwanda run current-account deficits of 12 to 20% of GDP financed by external debt of 94% to 350% of GNI. The third group pairs real growth with price stability and sustainable debt, and within it Cote d'Ivoire offers the best expected return. It grows at 6.5%, the second-fastest rate among macro-stable economies in the dataset, with inflation of 0.1%, public debt of 47% of GDP, and external debt of 49% of GNI. By the Solow logic, its $8,200 PPP income per capita means capital is still scarce and earns high marginal returns, unlike Botswana or Mauritius, which are already close to their steady states. Unemployment of 2.3% and an agriculture share of only 16.8% show labor moving into productive sectors, which is the labor-force-plus-productivity engine of potential output.

The decisive advantage is the exchange-rate regime. Cote d'Ivoire's -4.5% current-account deficit would normally be a warning sign, but the CFA franc's hard peg to the euro, backed by French Treasury guarantee, removes the devaluation risk that turns deficits into crises and explains why inflation sits near zero. For a euro-based investor this converts a frontier market into one with near-domestic currency risk, something no other fast-growing economy in the dataset offers. The country is also the export-oriented hub of the eight-country WAEMU zone, with Abidjan's port and a trade ratio of 55% of GDP, so an investment there sits inside a 140-million-person customs and currency zone rather than a 30-million-person national market. That is a zone of 140 million people, not 140 million consumers: as the Africa lecture noted, only about 14% of Africans earn above $5,500 a year, so the sector choice must target the segment that actually has income. Net FDI inflows of +2.0% of GDP, against Botswana's -3.3%, show that international capital has already reached the same conclusion. The income distribution is also workable: a Gini of 41.5 and poverty of 29.8% describe a growing middle rather than the bimodal structure the Brazil case warned about.

The risks are institutional, and the data are honest about them. Control of Corruption at -0.3 and Political Stability at -0.7 are mid-table, well below Botswana's +0.6 and +1.1, the country sits beside the coup belt of Mali and Burkina Faso, and its ease-of-doing-business rank of 110 is outside the top 100. The currency peg also cuts both ways: optimal currency area theory says a union needs fiscal transfers and labor mobility to absorb asymmetric shocks, and WAEMU has neither, so a cocoa crash cannot be cushioned by devaluation, which is what forced the 50% CFA devaluation of 1994. Finally, fast-growth periods carry the seeds of their own destruction, and Cote d'Ivoire has been growing fast since 2012, so the honest horizon is five to ten years, not twenty. Cocoa dependence adds commodity risk, though as an agricultural export with rising local processing it is less prone to the Dutch disease dynamics of oil or diamond enclaves. These are risks an investor is paid to carry, and they can be managed by choosing sectors that ride the growth rather than the crop, such as logistics, cement, banking, and consumer goods, and by holding liquid, euro-linked positions rather than illiquid greenfield assets. Botswana remains the better choice for capital preservation, with its surplus, low debt, and strong institutions, but its market of 2.6 million people, negative FDI, and reliance on a single commodity facing lab-grown substitutes cap the upside. The weights shift with the type of investment: for a long-horizon real investment such as a plant, a logistics operator, or a bank stake, Cote d'Ivoire is the choice; for a liquid portfolio allocation that must be exitable within weeks, Botswana or Mauritius would rank higher. For return on invested capital over a five-to-ten-year horizon, Cote d'Ivoire is the choice.
