## Choice: Botswana

Applying the lecture framework to the data, the countries of sub-Saharan Africa split into three groups. A large group shows growth but with macro instability that would erode any return: Zimbabwe, Sudan, and South Sudan post inflation of 105%, 139%, and 91%, and Angola, Nigeria, and Ghana combine fast growth (8 to 9%) with inflation above 14% and, in Ghana's case, public debt above 70% of GDP. A second group is stable but structurally dependent on external financing, which the lectures flagged as sudden-stop risk: Senegal, Mozambique, and Rwanda run current-account deficits of 12 to 20% of GDP with external debt between 94% and 350% of GNI. The third, much smaller group combines growth, low inflation, low debt, and credible institutions. Botswana is the clearest member of that group, which is why I chose it. It grows at 5.3%, inflation is 2.7%, public debt is 19.6% of GDP, external debt is only 11.9% of GNI, and it posts a current-account surplus of 2.9%. On governance it scores +0.6 on Control of Corruption and +1.1 on Political Stability, the best in mainland Africa and comparable to the island states of Mauritius and Seychelles, but with a far larger land economy. GDP per capita of roughly $20,700 (PPP) puts it in the top tier of the region, which means there is purchasing power behind the growth, not just population.

Botswana also fits the lecture's "Norway, not Dutch disease" test for a commodity economy. Diamonds dominate exports, yet the data show none of the symptoms the lectures associated with resource curse: inflation is contained, public debt is low, the external balance is in surplus, and institutions score well, which is consistent with the country's long-standing practice of saving mineral revenue through the Pula Fund rather than spending it. Its Ease of Doing Business rank of 87 is mid-table globally but second among mainland SSA economies in this dataset, behind only Kenya and Rwanda, and the political stability score removes the single biggest tail risk an investor faces in the region: expropriation or civil conflict.

The risks are real and the data show them. Unemployment is 24.5% and the Gini coefficient is 53.3, meaning the income is concentrated and the mass market is thin, exactly the "bimodal distribution" problem the Brazil case warned about. Net FDI inflows are negative (-3.3% of GDP), which suggests capital is leaving or that mining investment has plateaued, and the near-total dependence on diamonds leaves the economy exposed to a single commodity price and to lab-grown substitutes. For an investor, that argues for sectors that ride Botswana's stability rather than its diamonds: logistics and financial services for the southern African corridor, tourism, and import-substituting consumer goods aimed at the upper-income segment. The alternative I considered was Cote d'Ivoire, which has faster growth (6.5%), near-zero inflation, and a larger addressable market, but its weaker corruption and stability scores (-0.3 and -0.7) and 47% debt load mean the extra return comes with meaningfully more institutional risk. Botswana is the choice for an investor who, following the course's logic, prices downside protection first.
