# PART A: The 25 facts most likely to be tested

1. **Investment grade = BBB- and above** on the S&P scale. Below BBB- is speculative grade. Use the local-currency, long-term rating. Germany AAA, Spain AA-, India BBB, State Bank of India BBB, Société Générale A. S&P rated Lehman Brothers AAA shortly before it collapsed. S&P is free to use.
2. **Factoring** = selling your whole book of receivables to a factor for immediate liquidity (up to 90 percent of invoice value). **Discounting** is one bill at a time, always with recourse. **Without recourse** = the factor takes the default risk and receivables leave the balance sheet. **With recourse** = unpaid invoices come back to you, so it is really a loan. Cost roughly 1 to 2 percent. Only works if your debtors are high quality. Plamar's debtors included PDVSA.
3. **Plamar needed about USD 300,000** for a plant abroad (300,000 handles and 300,000 seals per month). Balance sheet at 31 Dec 2002 in bolívares: cash 97.7 million, receivables 211.5 million, total assets 621.6 million, sales 331.2 million, earnings 17.8 million. At the deck's rate of about 1,380 VEB per USD that is cash USD 70,361, receivables USD 152,313, total assets USD 447,526. Receivables are one third of assets. Always ask what currency and what year before reading a balance sheet.
4. **Why Guatemala** (class answer): free trade agreement with Mexico giving NAFTA access to the US, low cost, close to Venezuela, stable currency, inflation about 2 percent, GDP per capita USD 3,646. Brazil dismissed: Lula just elected (Jan 2003), too big and expensive for USD 240,000. Dominican Republic: peso devalued, inflation 8.6 percent, growth under 3 percent. Today Plamar operates in Guatemala, the Dominican Republic, Brazil and Costa Rica.
5. **Asian crisis policy response:** finance ministries and the IMF raised interest rates and defended dollar pegs. Both made it worse. Korea's short-term interbank rate went to 30 percent. The Thai stock market fell 75 percent. "Do not raise interest rates when there is already a large debt burden."
6. **Malaysia was the only country in IMF history (since 1944) to reject IMF aid**, in 1997. It used capital controls and cut government spending instead, helped by oil revenues. Mahathir fired Anwar Ibrahim on 2 Sept 1998, one day after imposing capital controls.
7. **Original sin** = developing countries borrow in hard currency (USD, EUR), not their own. When the dollar strengthens or US rates rise, their debt grows automatically.
8. **Trigger of the Asian crisis:** loss of confidence. First warning: 5 Feb 1997, Somprasong Land missed a USD 3.1 million interest payment. Thailand floated the baht on 2 July 1997 and it fell 15 percent immediately.
9. **IMF packages:** Thailand USD 16 billion (Aug 1997, USD 17 billion with China), Indonesia USD 23 billion rising to 40 billion (Oct 1997), Korea USD 55 billion rising to 58 billion (Dec 1997), the largest ever at the time. The IMF gives loans, not donations, at rates below private banks, but with austerity conditions.
10. **Société Générale lost EUR 4.9 billion** on Jérôme Kerviel's unauthorized trades, announced 24 Jan 2008, plus about EUR 2 billion on subprime. Positions of about EUR 50 billion were unwound over three days. Capital increase of EUR 5.5 billion. 74 alerts over 13 months were closed procedurally. He took only 4 days of vacation in 2007. He came from the middle office, so he knew when controls ran.
11. **Other rogue traders:** Nick Leeson, Barings 1995, GBP 827 million (USD 1.3 billion), bank founded 1762 collapsed. Kweku Adoboli, UBS 2011, USD 2 billion.
12. **Lesson from SocGen for an exporter:** never speculate on currency. Close or hedge every open position with a forward. "Too many controllers means nobody controls."
13. **Forward formula:** swap points = (SONIA minus EURIBOR) x days x spot / (36,000 + EURIBOR x days). Forward = spot + swap points. Class example 8 Oct 2026: spot EUR/GBP 0.8479, EURIBOR 180 days 3.013 percent, SONIA 3.732 percent, forward for 8 Apr 2027 about 0.8509. If both rates are equal the forward equals spot. Banks earn the bid-ask spread, not by predicting rates.
14. **Convertible currency** (UN definition, any one of): residents and non-residents can buy unlimited foreign currency with it; no central bank authorization needed; traded on international FX markets and widely used. Hard currencies: USD, EUR, GBP, JPY, CHF, CAD, AUD, NZD, SEK, DKK, NOK. Not fully convertible: CNY (managed band), INR, RUB, TRY. Non-convertible pairs route through USD: double fees, double risk.
15. **Turkish lira:** 1 USD = 9 TRY in 1966, 1,650,000 in 2001, six zeros dropped in 2005, 18.30 in Sept 2022, 41.82 in Oct 2025, 49.22 on 8 Oct 2026.
16. **ICC international contract, 8 points:** parties, object, transport, terms of payment, guarantees of payment, courts, legislation, risks. The three transaction risks: transport, currency, credit. Term of payment (how long) is different from guarantee of payment (the security). Letters of credit cover about 44 percent of international trade.
17. **Basel capital ratio: 8 percent** of risk-weighted assets (Basel I, 1988, in force end 1992). Basel II (June 2001 proposal) kept 8 percent but tied risk weights to borrower quality and added three pillars: minimum capital, supervisory review, market disclosure. Basel III added liquidity buffers. Basel IV is current. Schröder called Basel II "not acceptable for Germany" in Oct 2001. The July 2002 fix: loans under EUR 1 million treated as retail, firms with EUR 1 to 50 million turnover get lower weights, no long-term surcharge up to EUR 500 million, deadline moved to Jan 2007.
18. **Mittelstand:** up to 499 employees and EUR 50 million revenue by official statistics (EU SME definition is 250 employees). 3.6 million firms, 68 percent of workers, 80 percent of apprentices. Equity below 20 percent for small firms, the Achilles' heel. Hausbank relationship banking "from cradle to grave." Anglo-Saxon model: equity, shareholder value, external ratings, arm's-length capital markets.
19. **German banking pillars (case):** three. Public sector (Landesbanken and Sparkassen), credit cooperatives, private commercial banks. Aguirre listed four by splitting Landesbanken from Sparkassen. Germany is overbanked: loan margins about 1 percent versus 2 to 3.5 percent in Europe and 4 percent in the US. State guarantees for public banks to be phased out after 2005 (EU deal July 2001).
20. **Negative working capital** = short-term liabilities exceed short-term assets. Normal for supermarkets (paid cash, pay suppliers at 180 days), a red flag for a carmaker like Porsche. Main reason companies default. Creditor order in liquidation: government, banks, employees, suppliers.
21. **CAGE** (Ghemawat): Cultural, Administrative, Geographic, Economic distance. Frankel and Rose effects on trade: common language +200 percent, common regional trading bloc +330 percent, common currency +340 percent, colony-colonizer +900 percent, common border +80 percent, access to ocean +50 percent, a 1 percent increase in distance cuts trade 1.1 percent. Trade between countries 5,000 miles apart is 20 percent of that at 1,000 miles. Star TV lost about USD 500 million in Asia. Tricon's Mexico moved from 16th of 20 to 2nd once distance was counted.
22. **Market selection funnel:** preliminary screening (macro, PESTEL) to second screening (CAGE) to in-depth screening (Porter's five forces plus detailed market analysis) to final selection (competitive advantage equation: company resources and capabilities matched with country specifics and industry success factors; all three must align). Goals must be SMART. Górecka: selecting foreign markets is one of the most critical decisions.
23. **Readiness** = three analyses in the home country: external (Porter's five forces), internal (Porter generic strategies and Ansoff matrix), resources and capabilities (resource-based view and VRIO). Market development (existing product, new market) is the Ansoff box for internationalization. Expect a 6 to 18 month ramp-up before profit.
24. **Entry mode ladder** by resource commitment, risk and return, low to high: indirect export (importer, agent, distributor, trading company) to direct export (internet, wholesaler, branded manufacturer, export department) to contractual agreements (turnkey, subcontracting, licensing, franchising, strategic alliances and JVs) to FDI (representative office, branch, subsidiary via greenfield or M&A). Franchising is the fastest expansion. Alliances fail about 60 percent of the time, mainly through poor partner selection and lack of trust. Sogo shoshas handle 50 percent of Japan's exports and 75 percent of imports.
25. **Zara:** 10 to 15 days from sketch to shipment, about 12,000 designs a year, stores order twice a week, replenishment rate 2.8 percent, customers visit 17 times a year versus 4.1, advertising 0.3 percent of revenue versus 3.5 percent industry, prices about 15 percent below competitors, over 60 percent made in Spain, Portugal and nearby, gross margin 57.4 percent versus industry 38.3 percent, FY2017 revenue EUR 25.3 billion, 7,475 stores in 96 markets, Zara is 70.1 percent of Inditex EBIT.

# PART B: Neira's strategy half (Sessions 1 to 9)

## B1. Global trends (Sessions 1-2 and 5-6, no deck uploaded)

- Fortune 500 mortality: about half of today's list will be gone in 7 years; almost 90 percent of the 1955 list is gone.
- Three drivers of disruption: technology (the enabler), demographics (the silent force), globalization (the amplifier).
- Technology: democratized data and empowered consumers. Combinatory technology (Amazon Go stacks computer vision, deep learning, sensor fusion). Tesla can analyze only about 15 percent of the data it captures.
- Demographics: cities generate 80 to 85 percent of GDP; Asia has over half of the 32 megacities; 41 megacities by 2030.
- Globalization: trade liberalization now in question; emerging-market competitors win on price (McKinsey: 60 percent of international purchasing decisions driven by lower price; Chinese car brands took about 15 percent of the Spanish market in 5 years).
- Platform and asset-light models expand faster (Alibaba owns no warehouses, Airbnb no hotels, Uber no taxis).
- B2B to C2B: consumer power rising; 90 percent of shoppers are influenced by reviews; only 12 percent trust what the supplier says.
- Distance still matters online: only 12 percent of internet users buy cross-border; cross-border e-commerce about USD 700 billion, about 8 percent of the population; Facebook cross-border use about 50 percent, YouTube about 20 percent, e-commerce 11 to 15 percent.
- Growth is shifting to Southeast Asia, Africa, China and India; 50 percent of urban growth expected there.
- Incumbents fail from cultural resistance, not lack of data (Kodak, Blockbuster, Nokia, Ballmer laughing at the iPhone). Define yourself by the need you satisfy, not the product.

## B2. Benefits and reasons to internationalize (Session 5 deck)

Five main drivers on the deck: home market, foreign market, competition, product, economies of scale and scope. A sixth bucket covers both home and foreign markets.

**Foreign market pull**
1. Growth: increase sales and profits.
2. Resource seeking: be close to supply (minerals, agriculture); global sourcing flexibility; new technology and capacity; superior quality inputs; lower-cost factors; strategic assets (PM Guatemala port terminal); new talent pools.
3. Exploit a specific opportunity (Metrocompost in Vietnam).
4. Rewarding partnerships and trust with local players (General Kinematics JV; Netflix opened a Paris office to win the trust of French content makers).
5. Serve global customers who want the same product everywhere (auditors, banks, auto suppliers follow clients).
6. Learning: ideas, technologies, knowledge integration. IKEA entered Japan "to expose ourselves to the toughest competition in the world." Needs a global knowledge infrastructure.
7. Changes in entry barriers; avoid tariffs by producing inside the barrier.
8. High demand "pull effect" (consumer goods, healthcare, housing).

**Home market push**
1. Small local market (Singapore). 2. Slow domestic growth. 3. Saturation (consumer goods, Zara in Spain). 4. Level out seasonality (IDE snowmaking in Switzerland and South Africa; ham and cava at Christmas; Tanzanian fintech in tourist season). 5. Fill excess capacity (steel wire rod needs about 85 percent utilization). 6. Excessive regulation. 7. Recession at home (URBAR; Spanish firms going to Argentina after 2008). 8. Diversification and risk reduction.

**Both markets:** regulation changes (Uber in Spain), tax reasons, government incentives (ICEX in Spain).

**Product:** extend the product life cycle abroad (full-fat milk, cereals, Coke); exploit an exclusive product or technology.

**Competition:** intense competition at home; lower competition abroad; first-mover advantage; reaction to competitors' moves, either follower (deck: Zara entered India in 2010, H&M followed in 2017; the class recording reversed the order, trust the deck) or "offense as defense" (enter the rival's home market to force it to spend there).

**Economies of scale and scope:** scale = lower unit cost from volume in sourcing, production, marketing, R&D; large projects need global markets to earn ROI. Scope = savings from using the same resource across products. Operational scope shares tangible (labs, distribution) and intangible (brand, reputation, tech) resources; strategic scope transfers management capabilities across businesses. National Geographic licenses its brand to merchandise and stores (Spain, London, Panama).

Neira's rule: never walk into a board meeting saying "we should expand" without knowing which of these reasons you are pursuing and what success looks like.

## B3. Ready to internationalize? (Session 6 deck, taught 2 Oct)

**What strategy is not:** not goals ("be number 1 or 2"), not a single action ("merge", "outsource"), not vision or values. Strategy is how you compete and the competitive advantage you base it on: how you will be unique.

**Three analyses in the home country**

1. **External analysis: Porter's five forces.** Objectives: how attractive is the industry, what are the key success factors, what is changing, are we in the right business before going abroad. Apply it in three steps: identify the industry, identify the players, analyze each force in sequence. Ask of each force: is it strong enough to reduce or eliminate industry profits?
   - Substitutes: products with equivalent function. High substitution on the slide: newspapers, travel agencies, urban transport, Audi Q5 vs Land Rover, pizza vs burger, Amazon vs El Corte Inglés. Low substitution: gasoline, cigarettes, high-speed train vs bus, orange juice vs cola, cement in Madrid vs Paris.
   - Potential entrants, barriers: capital requirements (Boeing and Airbus vs apps), economies of scale (A380 needed about 400 units to break even on about USD 18 billion), absolute cost advantage (Aramco, Intel learning curve), product differentiation (cosmetics high, low-cost airlines low), access to distribution (Mercadona shelf space), government and legal barriers (traffic rights), retaliation (airline price cuts).
   - Rivalry: seller concentration (Gillette; Coke and Pepsi duopoly compete on marketing), differentiation (restaurants vs petrol stations), excess capacity and exit barriers (seaside hotels in winter), fixed vs variable cost (automotive).
   - Buyer power: price sensitivity (importance of the product in cost, buyer competition, differentiation) and bargaining power (size and concentration, information such as Booking and Kayak, vertical integration: Zara makes its own clothes unlike Gap and H&M).
   - Supplier power: the mirror of buyer power.
   - Class addition: define industry boundaries first; disruption is an extra angle.

2. **Internal analysis: how are we competing, is the strategy proven?**
   - Porter generic strategies, as printed on the slides: cost leadership whole market (Amazon), differentiation whole market (Apple), cost focus (Lidl), differentiation focus (Ferrari). In class Neira also named Walmart, Aldi, Porsche and Tesla. The two competitive advantages: cost advantage is a similar product at lower cost; differentiation advantage is a price premium from a unique product.
   - Ansoff matrix, only two levers: what is sold and to whom.
     - Market penetration: existing product, existing market. Only in a growing unsaturated market. Aims: hold or grow share, dominate growth markets, restructure a mature market by aggressive pricing, raise usage. Lowest risk (new locations, organic growth).
     - Product development: new product, existing market, usually with a trusted brand (Coke flavors, McDonald's salads).
     - Market development: existing product, new market. Three forms: new segments (discount to premium), new users or channels (e-commerce), new geographies, the prime example of internationalization (Uber adapted to Spain's VTC licence model).
     - Diversification: new product, new market, highest complexity and risk; justified by economies of scope (Alphabet, Samsung; Netflix cited in class).
   - Question to ask: is the current growth strategy sustainable and transferable abroad?

3. **Resources and capabilities: resource-based view (RBV).** Look inside for the sources of advantage. Do we have the resources to go international, and if not can we acquire them?
   - Step 1: list resources (tangible: financial, physical; intangible: brands, patents, know-how, culture) and capabilities by function (R&D, operations, design, marketing, sales and distribution, corporate). Resources alone are unproductive; they must work together. Disney: theme parks and studios plus enduring affection for its characters.
   - Step 2: tangible assets can be bought, so they confer little advantage. Intangible assets are the main source of sustainable advantage because they are heterogeneous (different bundles) and immobile (cannot be moved or copied quickly).
   - Step 3: VRIO (Barney). Not valuable: competitive disadvantage. Valuable but not rare: competitive parity. Valuable and rare but imitable: temporary advantage. Valuable, rare, costly to imitate but not organized to capture value: temporary advantage. All four: sustained competitive advantage. Costly to imitate comes from time, causal ambiguity and social complexity (culture). Organization means systems, processes, structure and culture.
   - Class addition: be honest about funding. Expect 6 to 18 months of ramp-up before profit; have cash for capex and working capital; "spreadsheets can say anything, reality cannot."

## B4. Where? International market selection (Session 7 deck, taught 28 Sep)

**Strategy design process:** strategic analysis (Ready) to set main goals and objectives to select potential markets to set country-specific goals to entry strategy to implementation.

**Set main goals:** clear reasons why you go international, aligned with resources and capabilities, SMART (specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-limited) and somewhat flexible as priorities change (COVID).

**Country screening funnel** (from about 200 countries down to one). Cost of entry, resource constraints and main goals are considered throughout.
- Preliminary or earliest screening: macro perspective, PESTEL. Reduce to 8 to 12 candidates.
- Second screening: the distance approach, CAGE. Decide how easy it is to replicate the business there.
- In-depth screening: industry attractiveness and barriers to entry with Porter's five forces in the 2 or 3 pre-selected countries, then detailed market analysis of the product: market size, product competition and acceptance, geography and transport, buying habits, pricing (customary markups, discounts, competitor prices and promotion and channels), distribution, advertising. Questions: will the country affect the product, do we need to adapt, how should it be offered.
- Final selection: the competitive advantage equation. Company resources and capabilities matched with country specifics and industry success factors. All three must align; strength in one cannot compensate for weakness in another. Compare countries on country specifics, industry success factors and attractiveness, and cost of entry; look for match or gap and how hard the gap is to close.

**CAGE in detail (deck plus Ghemawat article)**
- Cultural: language, ethnicity, religion, values, social norms, the unwritten rules. Most affects products with high linguistic content (TV), tied to national identity (food), consumer durables with size or standard preferences (Japan's small cars and appliances), country quality associations (wine). Chinese tolerance of copyright infringement traced to Confucius, not communism. Red means beautiful in Russian.
- Administrative or political: colonial ties, shared currency or trading bloc, political hostility, government policy, institutional weakness and corruption. Governments protect industries that are large employers (farming), national champions (aerospace), vital to national security (telecoms), staples (electricity), entitlements (drugs), natural resource exploiters (oil, mining) and high sunk-cost infrastructure. Weak institutions and corruption depress trade more than explicit barriers. India and Pakistan share history, border and language but trade almost nothing. Corruption Perception Index used in class.
- Geographic: not just miles. Country size, within-country distances, access to sea and rivers, topography, transport and communication infrastructure, time zones, climate. Most affects low value-to-weight products (cement, steel), fragile or perishable goods (glass, fruit), and anything needing communication links or local supervision (financial services, many services). Equity flows also fall with distance because information infrastructure falls.
- Economic: consumer income, and the cost and quality of natural, financial and human resources, infrastructure, inputs and knowledge. Most affects products whose demand varies with income (cars), industries relying on scale and standardization (mobile phones), labor-cost-sensitive industries (garments), and businesses with different distribution systems (insurance). Replicators (Walmart Canada is a carbon copy; Walmart India would be a different business) should target similar economies; arbitrageurs (garments, footwear) target different ones. US domestic distribution margins are a bigger import barrier than international transport and tariffs combined.
- Country portfolio analysis (CPA) is flawed: it plots per-capita income against product penetration and bubble size for market size, so it counts only potential sales and ignores the cost of distance.
- Company-specific features change distance: cosmopolitan managers reduce cultural distance; Tricon already owned four fifths of its Mexican outlets with 38 percent share.
- Class example: trading pairs sharing language, bloc, border and income can trade 10 to 15 times more. If a dimension cannot be measured, substitute another indicator.

**Set country-specific goals:** market objectives (sales, profit, penetration), product adaptation, marketing communications, distribution (ports, mode, usually ocean), packaging and labeling, documentation (bill of lading, commercial invoice, certificate of origin), freight forwarder, warehousing, final price including shipping, insurance, tariffs and markups, terms of sale (Ex Works, FOB, CIF), payment method (cash in advance, open account, letter of credit), marketing budget, resource requirements.

**Key takeaways slide:** systematic analysis beats intuition; progressive screening saves resources; alignment is critical; risk-return trade-off, match entry mode to risk tolerance.

## B5. How? Country entry alternatives (Session 9 deck, taught 28 Sep and 5 Oct)

Four levels, rising in resource commitment, risk and return. Choose by resource commitment and barriers, risk, return, and time frame or speed required.

**1. Indirect export** (a cross-border intermediary exports for you)
- Importers or export management companies: act as your export department, know local conditions, may work under your name, may get exclusivity, low cost.
- Agents and representatives: paid on commission, do not buy the goods, customers belong to the exporter, may carry competing products. A representative is an agent limited to an area or product line. Advantages: low entry cost, low staffing, immediate presence, intermediary handles entry problems, control over the product. Disadvantages: low profitability, high dependence, no international experience gained.
- Distributors: buy your product and resell, want exclusivity for a territory, customers belong to the distributor, may handle logistics and customs. You lose visibility on end customers and pricing. Prefer complementary, not competing, portfolios.
- Trading companies: full service from purchasing to warehousing and selling. They buy for foreign clients, so no loyalty to you. Japan's sogo shoshas handle 50 percent of exports and 75 percent of imports.
- Due diligence on intermediaries: marketing knowledge, industry expertise, financial capacity and facilities; do they pay promptly; how they handle competing lines; reputation and references.
- Set goals for the channel: volume, share, margin; capital and cost commitments; degree of control, terms of sale, channel ownership; coverage; duration and continuity. Put in the contract: minimum sales, duration with 60 to 90 day notice, exit clause, reporting.

**2. Direct export:** internet (unplanned foreign orders lead to localized sites), wholesalers and purchasing groups, branded manufacturers selling through foreign retail chains under their own brand (Lee, Wrangler), an export department for B2B high-tech or high-value products once indirect volume justifies it.

**3. Contractual agreements:** long-term, usually non-equity, involving transfer of knowledge, technology, trademarks or skills.
- Turnkey projects: complete construction of a plant or infrastructure handed over on completion; concessions (waste contracts with municipalities).
- Subcontracting: a local manufacturer produces your goods (Boeing, fast fashion, auto assembly).
- Licensing: grant rights to patents, trademarks, brand names, technology, software. Advantages: low capital, works where import restrictions block other modes, key where foreign ownership is sensitive. Risks: wrong partner, quality problems, payment problems, loss of marketing control. National Geographic: worldwide brand rights subject to approval, annual fee plus percentage of sales. Co-marketing = joint promotion of two existing products (Starbucks and Spotify). Co-branding = two companies create a new combined product (Meta Ray-Ban, McFlurry with Oreo, Nike and Apple Watch).
- Franchising: a form of licensing. Franchisor supplies the standard package of products, systems and management services; franchisee supplies market knowledge, capital and personal involvement. Blends skill centralization with operational decentralization. Fastest expansion of all, low investment, governments like it because it creates local ownership. Risks: adapting to local taste, control issues (McDonald's India). National Geographic combines a licence (Washington to the Spanish company) with franchise contracts to store operators.
- Strategic alliances and joint ventures: cooperation out of mutual need to share risk toward a common objective. Complementarity is crucial. Three distinguishing features: partners depend on each other's effort, knowledge is exchanged, trust matters because a contract cannot foresee every risk. Unavoidable issues: coordination cost, the relationship evolves unpredictably, alliances end, "today's ally may be tomorrow's rival." Formation process: do I need one, define form and objectives, select the partner (strategic fit plus organizational fit: chemistry, similar cultures, reputation, compatible decision systems, common time horizon), negotiate the contract. Seven contract questions: agreed objectives, contributions and rewards and penalties, integration with parent strategy, cultural and organizational fit, leverage of complementary strengths, exit strategy, monitoring. About 60 percent fail, mainly from poor partner selection and lack of trust. Types: equity joint venture (a separate jointly owned company, the classic alliance) and non-equity alliances (limited scope, no new company, easier to form and dissolve: technology exchange, joint R&D, joint distribution). JV advantages: speed, risk sharing, scale, learning, new opportunities, employee reaction. Risks: partner opportunism, partner failing to deliver, resources and management time absorbed. BP's John Browne: assuming you need not learn from others is arrogant.

**4. Foreign direct investment:** physical presence abroad, highest commitment and risk, realistic once you know the market as an exporter. Advantages: better market access, better customer service, join a supply chain, secure supply, access technology.
- Representative office: marketing and promotion only, cannot buy, sell or produce, an extension of the parent.
- Branch: same activities as head office, no separate legal entity, liabilities extend to the parent, 100 percent owned, double taxation treaties apply.
- Subsidiary: own legal entity, taxed where formed, activities can differ, ownership over 50 to 100 percent, liability limited to the subsidiary.
- Greenfield: start from zero. Advantages: no technology leakage, tight control, creates local jobs. Disadvantages: slow, faces competition before it is set up, research time.
- Cross-border M&A: merger combines two firms; acquisition buys a controlling stake. Advantages: fast, immediate market access, income from day one, learn from the acquired firm. Disadvantages: reaction of directors and employees, inherited risks.

# PART C: Aguirre's risk half (Sessions 3, 4, 10, 11, 12, 14)

## C1. Shifting wealth of nations and managing risk (Sessions 3-4 deck)

- Aguirre works at Coface, a trade credit insurer in 60 plus countries; has taught at IE since 1998. Coface's country risk tool used live (Colombia: reasonable business climate but weak peso; Angola: oil and diamonds).
- Thesis: today's allies are tomorrow's enemies. Iran and Iraq: 1979 embassy seizure, hostages 444 days; Iraq the Western ally in the 1980s; 1990 Kuwait; 2003 invasion; 2025 EU lifted Iran sanctions; 2025-26 US strikes. Film: Argo.
- Political risk is not only emerging markets: Iberdrola lost on a cancelled 80-turbine US offshore project (Dec 2025); Naturgy expropriated in Colombia.
- Developed vs developing threshold roughly USD 10,000 GDP per capita. Japan and China are the world's biggest lenders.
- The Economist's World Ahead missed COVID and the Ukraine invasion. Forecasting is unreliable.
- Four risks on the deck: 1 emerging-market capital flows and external vulnerability (foreign-currency corporate debt, original sin), 2 commodity prices, 3 debt (US went from 6 to 19 percent of GDP deficit-type jump in 2020 per the slide), 4 freight costs (Harpex index).
- Strait of Hormuz: about 20 million barrels a day transit; only two bypass pipelines (Saudi East-West, UAE Fujairah) with about 3.5 million barrels a day. About 27 percent of maritime trade; 33 to 35 percent of global fertilizer and about 35 percent of aluminum produced nearby. Malacca is the biggest strait by volume, Hormuz second.
- Trade wars: "reciprocal tariffs" are really based on trade imbalances; worst case is a crisis of confidence and dollar collapse. US tariff cost borne about 80 percent domestically, two thirds in margins, one third in consumer prices. Second China shock: Europe as dumping ground.
- Japan: third largest economy, biggest creditor, highest public debt of any developed country (over 220 percent of GDP, held domestically), raised the 10-year yield from 0.25 to 0.50 at the end of 2022 and raised rates in 2025 for the first time in 35 years.
- Strongmania: weak growth, inequality and corruption lead to protest votes and strong leaders, bad policy, weaker growth. Globalization popular where growth is (China 41 percent optimistic, France 3 percent).
- Asia-Pacific about 78 percent of world GDP growth 2016-2026. China, Germany and the US are the three biggest exporters. China now files the most patents; Huawei the most of any company. Productive investment (R&D, education, Korea) versus non-productive (real estate; Spain built 700,000 flats in 2007, 70,000 in 2008).

## C3. The Asian financial crisis (Session 10 deck plus HBS chronology)

**Causes (deck):**
- Government: fixed exchange rates, current account deficits, weak regulation and control.
- Companies and market: too many financial firms, dependence on short-term foreign funds, currency speculation, overpriced assets.
- External: governments unable to solve problems amid political uncertainty, the region seen as one unit not individual countries, speculation, IMF conditions.
- Class framing: 1992-97 massive capital inflows chasing 9 percent growth; banks lent 97 percent of deposits so withdrawals collapse liquidity; original sin; a surge in NPLs (non-performing loans) is the early warning. ICISA = International Credit Insurance and Surety Association (Allianz, Atradius, AXA).

**Chronology, the dates most worth knowing**
- 5 Feb 1997: Somprasong Land misses USD 3.1 million, first Thai default. 3 Mar: Bank of Thailand orders higher provisions. 11 Mar: 100 billion baht bailout; Camdessus sees no crisis coming. 14 May: Thailand imposes selective capital controls. 19 June: finance minister Amnuay resigns. 2 July: baht floated, falls 15 percent. 14 July: ringgit floated. 15 July: Kia under bankruptcy protection. 20 July: IMF USD 1 billion to the Philippines. 24 July: Mahathir blames Soros. 11 Aug: IMF USD 16 billion for Thailand. 14 Aug: rupiah floated. 20 Sept: Mahathir calls currency trading immoral; Soros calls him a menace. 17 Oct: Taiwan floats. 23-28 Oct: Hong Kong market loses 25 percent. 31 Oct: IMF USD 23 billion for Indonesia, 16 banks closed. 3 Nov: Sanyo Securities, first Japanese securities bankruptcy since WWII. 21 Nov: Korea seeks USD 20 billion. 24 Nov: Yamaichi collapses. 3 Dec: Korea USD 55 billion. 8 Dec: Thailand shuts 56 of 58 finance companies. 16 Dec: Korea removes the daily FX band. 22 Dec: Moody's cuts Indonesia, Korea, Thailand to junk. 23 Dec: Zhu Rongji says no yuan devaluation. 30 Dec: banks roll over Korea's short-term debt.
- 1998: 8 Jan ringgit record low (short-term debt 56 percent of foreign borrowing). 12 Jan Peregrine collapses, Hang Seng falls 8.7 percent. 15 Jan Suharto signs second IMF deal. 23 Jan rupiah loses a third in five days. 28 Jan Korea rolls USD 24 billion into guaranteed loans. 23 Feb Indonesia drops the currency board idea under IMF pressure. 24 Feb IMF eases Thai conditions. 31 Mar Korea rolls over 97 percent (USD 22 billion). 1 Apr Japan's Big Bang. 13-14 May Jakarta riots. 21 May Suharto resigns, Habibie takes over. 13 June Japan in recession, first in 23 years. 17 June US and Fed buy yen. 2 Sept Malaysia capital controls, Anwar fired. 27 Sept Japan Leasing bankruptcy, USD 16 billion. 25 Nov Camdessus: worst is over.

**High interest rates as medicine (deck):** Korea's IMF package pushed interbank rates to 30 percent, bankruptcies rose, confidence fell, outflows accelerated; Korea recovered after the IMF relented. Thailand: starved businesses of cash, stock market down 75 percent, 600,000 foreign workers sent home. Indonesia: defaults, inflation, riots, Suharto out, slow recovery. Malaysia: rejected the IMF, pegged deposit rates to leading banks, least affected. Overall: hot money, no export growth, devaluation raised private debt, high rates and austerity deepened recession, the IMF eventually changed course.

**Asian Tigers vs BRICS (deck):** Tigers (Hong Kong, Singapore, Taiwan, South Korea) had GNI per capita of about USD 8,000 to 23,000 in 1993-95; BRICs about USD 1,000 to 11,000 in 2008-11. Tigers: import substitution then export-led, small, dependent on the US, IT and financial centers. BRICs: large, diversified (Brazil cars and minerals, Russia oil and gas and arms, India IT, China cheap manufacturing), challenge the US. 2008 crisis: Singapore and Taiwan unscathed, Korea NPLs and crash, Hong Kong speculative attacks; BRICs stock markets fell then recovered from 2009, currencies appreciated except China. Class view: a simultaneous BRICS default is unlikely because they are too heterogeneous; risk is country by country.

**Sovereign debt treatments and company impact:** Argentina-Russia (unilateral default: credit crunch, higher financing costs, recession); Turkey-Brazil (deep fiscal adjustment: late public payments, anemic growth); India-Lebanon (banks "encouraged" to hold government debt: crowding out).

**2026 parallels (class):** high debt plus rising rates; US 10-year above 5 percent pulls capital from emerging markets; China and Japan selling US debt for the first time in 20 years; about 60 percent of AI investment is debt-financed in USD. Turkey's FX-protected deposits killed investment.

## C4. Currency risk (Session 11 deck)

- Rogue traders: Leeson 1995 (Barings, GBP 827 million), Kerviel 2008 (EUR 4.9 billion), Adoboli 2011 (USD 2 billion). Film: Rogue Trader.
- Cash ratio (liquidity coverage): cash and marketable securities over current liabilities; for a bank, cash as a share of deposits.
- Money versus currency: deposit money is money but not currency. Modern currency is intrinsically worthless; it is accepted because governments require it for taxes.
- 182 official currencies for 193 UN members; about 180 current currencies plus observers, partially recognized states and 32 dependencies.
- Floating: set by supply and demand, more efficient, more volatile. Fixed or pegged: linked to another currency or gold; needs reserves covering the currency in circulation. Class: pegs to the dollar have on average been a disaster because only the Fed prints dollars.
- Convertibility: ease of buying, selling or exchanging. UN criteria listed in Part A. Yuan not fully convertible (band of about 15 percent per class), kept weak to protect exports; likely convertible in future since China is the biggest lender.
- Currency future: standardized contract to exchange currencies at a future date at a rate fixed today, usually against USD, unit for example EUR 125,000, mostly closed out before delivery. Forward: the bank hedge, formula in Part A. Forwards on convertible currencies are cheap because the bank hedges both sides.
- Rising interest rates attract savings and appreciate the currency. US 10-year above 5 percent; euro area inflation about 4 percent so real return about 1 percent.
- Big Mac Index: purchasing power parity shorthand. Currency wars: competitive devaluation.
- Rating agencies: S&P, Moody's, Fitch; no credible alternatives despite 2008.

## C5. Credit ratings and German banking (Session 12, Banking on Germany case)

Ratings: see Part A fact 1. In 2012 Spanish banks CaixaBank, Sabadell and Bankinter fell below BBB-; Santander and BBVA kept investment grade through global diversification. Public banks carry their sovereign's rating.

**Case facts**
- Basel II announced June 2001 by the BIS Basel Committee (founded 1975 by G-10 central banks; the BIS itself dates from 1930 and sits in Basel). Three pillars: minimum capital (8 percent kept, risk weights refined), supervisory review, disclosure. Added market risk and operational risk to credit risk. Fears: pro-cyclicality, penalizing low-equity SMEs and long-term loans, information overkill (about 1,000 disclosure items), power of three US agencies, Lauder Institute "wrong track." Basel I: 1988, 8 percent, effective end 1992, response to the 1980s debt crisis.
- Politics: Schröder, Oct 2001, "not acceptable"; economics minister Müller "the German system of financing the Mittelstand must not be influenced"; Dresdner's Fahrholz "fundamentally on the right track."
- Mittelstand: hidden champions, mechanical engineering firms averaging 154 employees, Voith with 23,000 employees still counts because the family owns 93 percent. One third operated at a loss in 2001; about 500,000 firms facing succession within five years. Porsche: Piech family 57 percent, kicked off the mid-cap index for refusing quarterly reports, patient Landesgirokasse financing in the late 1980s. 40 percent of German firms rely on a single bank; 57 percent of executives saw shareholder value as a hidden social risk. US small firms run near 50-50 equity and debt.
- Three pillars of German banking: Sparkassen and Landesbanken (public, stakeholder-driven, AAA or AA+ thanks to state guarantees Anstaltslast and Gewährträgerhaftung, lending 50 basis points cheaper; 12 Landesbanken serving about 700 savings banks in 2001), cooperatives (DZ Bank, sixth largest, backed SAP's 1988 IPO), private commercial banks (Deutsche, Dresdner, Commerzbank, HVB). Universal banking and Allfinanz cross-selling. Merrill Lynch: a "graveyard for shareholder value." Deutsche Bank bought Morgan Grenfell 1989, Bankers Trust 1999, listed in New York 2001, half its staff abroad, first foreign (Swiss) CEO. Failed Deutsche-Dresdner and Dresdner-Commerzbank mergers in 2000; Allianz bought Dresdner in 2001 (bancassurance). KfW securitized Mittelstand loans as AAA-backed CLOs.
- Deutschland AG, five bank levers: lending, equity stakes and cross-shareholdings, securities issuance monopoly, supervisory board seats (two-tier boards since 1884, codetermination), proxy voting of bearer shares (banks voted about 90 percent at the largest 24 firms and rubber-stamped management). 2001 tax reform cut corporate tax to 25 percent and exempted capital gains on stake sales to unwind cross-holdings. Voluntary Corporate Governance Code Feb 2002. Vodafone-Mannesmann 2000: "goodbye to Deutschland AG."
- Capital markets: market cap 66.8 percent of GDP in 2000 versus US 154.9, UK 184, Switzerland 330; corporate bonds only 6 percent of bank loan volume; Neuer Markt (1997) peaked at 338 listings and lost about 95 percent, closed Sept 2002; of 540 listed firms only about 150 actively traded; 87 percent had a 25 percent-plus blockholder.
- Downturn 2002: all big commercial banks except Deutsche posted losses; Commerzbank and HVB in the red for the first time postwar; Ackermann floated a state-funded bad bank in March 2003; Schröder promised a Mittelstand bank; Breuer convicted over Kirch comments; Ackermann tried over Mannesmann severance. Closing quotes: "the classic bank credit is in retreat"; banks lend you an umbrella when it is sunny and take it back when it rains.
- Class additions: Germany had about 2,915 banks in 1990 for 80 million people; German resistance to Basel II came from corporates more than banks; the case was written in 2003, six years before the subprime crisis validated Basel's intent; banks in 2026 are tightening, so prepare a one-to-two-year financial plan before approaching one.

## C6. Trade finance and factoring (Session 14 deck, Plamar case)

- Factoring benefits: liquidity up to 90 percent of invoice value, more buying power with suppliers, off-balance-sheet treatment of receivables, improved ratios and rating, debtor risk cover up to 100 percent, outsourced ledger and collections. Invoice discounting or in-house factoring finances invoices but leaves ledger management with the client. Disbursement mechanics: the credit insurer sets a limit per debtor; guaranteed invoices are purchased; a reserve for credit notes and deductions is withheld; the fundable amount is paid up to the client's funding limit.
- Misconceptions on the deck: "too expensive" (cheaper than internal debtor management; early-payment discounts; stronger negotiating position); "only weak companies use it" (today a professional financing solution); "I lose control of my clients" (relationship is professionalized).
- Cash conversion cycle: cut DSO, minimize idle inventory, maximize DPO; permanent current assets are really fixed assets and need long-term financing. Deck claim: cost reduction and freeing up cash of about 16 percent.
- Recommendation slide: short term reposition liquidity, cut the CCC with factoring, improve capital structure; long term use liquidity to access foreign markets, become attractive for long-term financing and M&A, reduce cost of capital.
- Class points: factoring ties you to one bank because it buys all your receivables; listed corporates factor at quarter-end to dress ratios; fintechs offer non-recourse lines of USD 100 to 200 million with 20 to 25 staff; Coface insures the banks buying receivables.
- Venezuela: 1999 highest GDP per capita in Latin America, now near the bottom; about 7 million emigrated; price controls, 60 percent of manufacturers gone, Dutch disease, hyperinflation of 700 percent plus; only SWOT strength is the largest oil and gas reserves. Contrast with Dubai's diversification. Al Jazeera 2021: new bolívar drops six zeros.
