# Session 7: International market selection, Where? (Neira, 28 Sep 2026) plus Ghemawat, "Distance Still Matters" (HBR 2001)

This unit is the third step of the course's internationalization framework: global trends, benefits and reasons, ready?, **where?**, how?. It answers "which country?" with a staged funnel that starts from roughly 200 countries and ends with one: earliest screening (start from your own business), preliminary screening (macro, PESTEL), second screening (distance, CAGE), in-depth screening (industry attractiveness and barriers with Porter's five forces, then a detailed market analysis), and final selection (the competitive advantage equation: company resources and capabilities matched with country specifics and industry success factors). Cost of entry, resource constraints and the main goals are kept in mind the whole way. The assigned article, Pankaj Ghemawat's "Distance Still Matters", supplies the second-screening tool: the CAGE framework (cultural, administrative, geographic, economic distance), the Frankel and Rose numbers on how much each kind of distance changes trade, the Star TV failure, the China sidebar, the industry sensitivity table and the Tricon Restaurants case where Mexico moves from 16th of 20 to 2nd once distance is counted. The professor's emphasis in class: selecting the country is one of the most critical steps; do the analysis for the right reasons with SMART goals; distance is not only physical; if you cannot get data on one ingredient, swap it for another; and company capabilities must match the market, because strength in one element cannot compensate for weakness in another.

### Slide 6: Goals should be SMART
- **S**pecific, **M**easurable, **A**chievable, **R**elevant, **T**ime-limited, plus "**flexible** to some degree as priorities change". Caption: SMART objectives can be applied anywhere in your life, professionally and personally.
- Class examples for each letter:
  - Specific: "next year you should sell more" is not a goal; without specificity you can never track whether it was achieved.
  - Measurable: "increase customer satisfaction next year" fails unless you define how to measure it (polls, etc.).
  - Achievable: a goal so far from reality demotivates the team; nobody is challenged to reach it.
  - Relevant: "paint all the warehouses red" is specific and measurable but irrelevant to the business.
  - Time-limited: always set a period (6, 12, 18 months); he said he would insist on this all course long; applies to careers too ("12 months after the master's to get into this sector, then switch").
  - Flexible: COVID is the example; every company had budgets and goals and had to change them.
- Class, student question (when should a company not go international / losing track of the SMART goal): his experience is that with many people throwing input the team gets paralysed; the fix was a **weekly** (not monthly) review against the plan. Startups can do a lot in one month, so weekly follow-up avoids wasting time.

### Slide 14: CAGE distance framework, what it does
- Analysis of the **distance between home and potential countries**.
- **Helps identify and prioritize the differences between countries** when developing international strategies.
- Details **how easy or not it is for the company to adapt and operate in the target country: to REPLICATE their business** in the foreign country.
- Class: it gives you a comparison of the issues that are critical to your company across candidate countries. Student question on where to get objective data (for example on cultural differences): sometimes the data is not there; if an ingredient would take too long or you will never get enough data points, **drop that ingredient and find an alternative one**. ### Slide 15: CAGE video
- Picture: Pankaj Ghemawat on video (Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota) explaining CAGE. Content of the clip (played in class): C cultural, A administrative/political, G geographic, E economic. Most interactions, not just trade, are hindered by differences and helped by similarities on these dimensions. **Two countries with the same official language, in the same regional trading arrangement, sharing a border and with similar per capita income can be expected to trade 10 to 15 times as much** as similarly sized countries without those commonalities. The framework gives an imperfect but useful metric: is Canada closer to home than Mexico? **Canada is still the US's largest bilateral trading partner although only about the tenth largest economy**, because of CAGE commonalities.

### Slide 22: Geographic distance
- Definition: **more than just how far two countries are from each other**: the country's **physical size, within-country distances to borders, access to the ocean, topography, and even time zones**.
- **Most affects:** **heavy or bulky products (transport costs)**; **fragile or perishable products (glass, fruit)**; businesses **in which communications are critical (financial services)**.
- Class: also think about moving goods inside the country (from the port by boat, then bus), poor connections, landlocked countries with no ocean border, warehouse and distribution logistics.

### Slide 23: Cultural distance
- Definition: **language, beliefs, religion, values and social norms, the unwritten, unspoken rules of the game, that shape the behavior of individuals and organizations.**
- **Most affects:** products with **high linguistic content (TV)**; products **related to national identity (food)**; **consumer durable goods (Japan: small cars and appliances)**.
- Class: are you selling food? Then you probably need to adapt to local taste. Many companies have failed entering another country on this point (he mentioned spicy sauces as an example).

### Slide 24: CAGE table from the article, "Distance between two countries increases with..."
- **Cultural**: different languages, ethnicities, religions, social norms; lack of connective ethnic or social networks.
- **Administrative and political**: absence of shared monetary or political association; political hostilities; weak legal and financial institutions.
- **Geographic**: lack of common border, waterway access, adequate transportation or communication links; physical remoteness; different climates.
- **Economic**: different consumer incomes; different costs and quality of natural, financial and human resources; different information or knowledge.
- Class: distance is never only physical; it increases with every one of these. Focus on the items that are critical to your product or service (inflation, interest rates, taxes, whatever matters for you).

### Slide 28: Key concept, barriers to entry

## Part 2: Ghemawat, "Distance Still Matters: The Hard Reality of Global Expansion" (HBR, September 2001, reprint R0108K)

### Idea in brief
- Companies routinely overestimate the attractiveness of foreign markets because they are dazzled by size and use tools that count only potential sales. Distance has **four dimensions: cultural, administrative/political, geographic, economic**. Different types of distance affect different industries differently (religion shapes food preferences but not cement). The more two countries differ on these dimensions, the riskier the market; similarities suggest potential (a common currency boosts trade more than 300 percent).

### Star TV: the opening failure
- Launched **1991**: deliver TV to a "media-starved" Asian audience, targeting the **top 5 percent** of Asia's socioeconomic pyramid, a newly rich elite that could pay and was an attractive advertising market. Because English was the second language of most targets, Star would use cheap, readily available **English-language programming** instead of investing in local content, and **satellites** would sidestep geographic distance.
- **Rupert Murdoch's News Corporation bought out Star's founders for USD 825 million between 1993 and 1995**, attracted by leveraging the Twentieth Century Fox film library across Asia.
- Results: fiscal year to 30 June 1999, loss of **USD 141 million pretax on revenues of USD 111 million**; losses in fiscal 1996 to 1999 about **USD 500 million**, not counting joint ventures such as **Phoenix TV in China**; no positive operating profit expected until 2002.
- Two mistakes identified later in the article: (1) **ignoring cultural distance**: assuming TV was culture-insensitive, despite European evidence that mass audiences in countries large enough to support local content prefer local programming; China and India would have needed heavy localisation ("TV is hardly cement"). (2) **Ignoring administrative and political sensitivities**: foreign ownership of broadcasting is politically loaded everywhere (Murdoch himself had to become a US citizen to buy the stations that became Fox). Shortly after the acquisition Murdoch declared satellite TV "an unambiguous threat to totalitarian regimes everywhere"; the Chinese government then **banned reception of foreign satellite TV**. China accounts for nearly **60 percent** of Star's potential customers.

### Country portfolio analysis (CPA) and why it fails
- CPA: the "hoary but still widely used" technique. Plots actual and potential markets on a grid: **per capita income** on one axis, a **product performance measure (often penetration rate)** on the other; **bubble size = total market size** (GDP or absolute consumption). It focuses on national GDP, consumer wealth and propensity to consume, so it places all the emphasis on **potential sales** and **ignores the costs and risks** of doing business in the new market.
- Most of those costs and risks come from distance, and distance is not only geographic.

### Measuring the impact of distance: Frankel and Rose
- Economists use the **gravity theory of trade**: trade rises with economic size and falls with distance. Gravity models explain **up to two thirds** of observed variation in trade between country pairs. Jeffrey Frankel and Andrew Rose (working paper, May 2000) estimated these effects.

| Distance attribute | Change in international trade |
|---|---|
| Income level: GDP per capita, 1 percent increase | +0.7 percent |
| Economic size: GDP, 1 percent increase | +0.8 percent |
| Physical distance, 1 percent increase | -1.1 percent |
| Physical size, 1 percent increase (*) | -0.2 percent |
| Access to ocean (*) | +50 percent |
| Common border | +80 percent |
| Common language | +200 percent |
| Common regional trading bloc | +330 percent |
| Colony-colonizer relationship | +900 percent |
| Common colonizer | +190 percent |
| Common polity | +300 percent |
| Common currency | +340 percent |

- (*) Footnote: the starred estimates exclude the last four variables in the table.
- Headline facts in the text: trade between countries **5,000 miles apart is only 20 percent** of what it would be at **1,000 miles**; a company trades **ten times as much** with a former colony; common currency **+340 percent**; common trading bloc **+330 percent**; shared language makes trade **three times greater**.
- "The death of distance" (IT and global communications shrinking the world) is not just wrong for business, it is dangerous. CPA must be tempered by an evaluation of distance.
- Industries differ: geographic distance matters most for heavy or bulky products and for operations needing coordination of dispersed people; cultural distance matters for consumer goods and media but far less for cement or steel.

### Cultural distance
- Attributes: differences in **religion, race/ethnicity, social norms, language**; lack of connective ethnic or social networks. Language is easy to see; **social norms** (deep, unspoken principles guiding everyday choices) are nearly invisible even to those who follow them.
- Example of a hidden norm: Chinese **tolerance of copyright infringement**. William Alford (*To Steal a Book Is an Elegant Offense*, Stanford, 1995) traces it not to communism but to a Confucian precept favouring replication of past intellectual work ("I transmit rather than create; I believe in and love the Ancients"). In the **1920s** Merriam Webster found the Commercial Press in Shanghai already distributing its own version of Merriam Webster's bilingual dictionary; a Chinese court imposed a small fine for using the seal but did not stop publication.
- How culture usually creates distance: it shapes **choices between substitute products** through feature preferences. **Colour** tastes: the word "red" in Russian also means beautiful. **Consumer durables** are especially sensitive: the **Japanese prefer small cars and appliances**, a norm in countries where space is valued.
- Sometimes products touch **identity** and whole categories are affected: food is sensitive to religion (**Hindus do not eat beef**); in **Japan rice carries enormous cultural baggage** while Americans treat it as a commodity.
- Industries most affected (CAGE table): high linguistic content (TV); products affecting cultural or national identity (foods); products whose features vary by **size (cars), standards (electrical appliances), packaging**; products carrying **country-specific quality associations (wines)**.

### Administrative or political distance
- Attributes: absence of colonial ties; absence of shared monetary or political association; political hostility; government policies; institutional weakness.
- Effects: **colony-colonizer links boost trade by 900 percent** (Britain and the Commonwealth, France and the franc zone of West Africa, Spain and Latin America). **Preferential trading arrangements, common currency and political union each raise trade by more than 300 percent.** The **European Union** is the leading example of deliberately reducing administrative distance. Ties must be friendly: **India and Pakistan** share colonial history, a border and linguistic ties, yet trade is virtually nil.
- Unilateral measures: **policies of individual governments are the most common barriers** to cross-border competition. Sometimes the home country is the problem: US **prohibitions on bribery** and health, safety and environmental rules dampen US firms' international business. More commonly the target government raises barriers: **tariffs, trade quotas, restrictions on FDI, and preferences for domestic competitors (subsidies, favouritism in regulation and procurement).**
- Governments protect a domestic industry when it is:
  - a **large employer** (large voting blocs: Europe's farmers);
  - a **national champion** (symbol of modernity and competitiveness: Boeing versus Airbus could spark a trade war; the more a government has invested in an industry, the more protective it is);
  - **vital to national security** (high-tech such as telecommunications and aerospace; the FBI delayed Deutsche Telekom's acquisition of VoiceStream);
  - a producer of **staples** (food staples, fuel, electricity);
  - a producer of an **"entitlement" good or service** (health care; governments set quality standards and control prices);
  - an **exploiter of natural resources** (physical assets seen as national heritage; foreigners seen as robbers; nationalisation a constant threat to oil and mining multinationals);
  - involving **high sunk-cost commitments** (oil refineries, aluminium smelters, railway lines; irreversibility creates scope for hold-ups once the investment is made).
- **Weak institutional infrastructure** (corruption, social conflict) depresses trade and investment **more than any explicit policy or restriction**; a strong legal system makes a country much more attractive.
- Industries most affected (CAGE table): government involvement is high in staples (electricity), "entitlements" (drugs), large employers (farming), large suppliers to government (mass transportation), national champions (aerospace), vital to national security (telecommunications), exploiters of natural resources (oil, mining), subject to high sunk costs (infrastructure).

### Sidebar: How far away is China, really?
- Survey of nearly **100 multinationals**: **54 percent** said performance in China was "worse than planned" versus **25 percent** "better than planned"; **62 percent** had **overestimated market potential**.
- Culturally China is far from nearly everywhere: many **dialects** hard for foreigners, limited local foreign-language skills; business culture based on personal connections (**guanxi**) versus Westerners' transaction focus; Chinese consumers may be **"home-biased"** (less preference for foreign brands than in India); greater China plays a disproportionate role in China's economic relations.
- Administrative barriers probably matter more: American Chamber of Commerce members flagged **market-access restrictions, high taxes and customs duties** as the biggest barriers to profitability; high state involvement, loss-making state-owned enterprises and technically insolvent state banks. **Transparency International 2000: China 63rd of 90** (a rating of 1 = least perceived corruption). **Standard and Poor's political-risk ranking 5 in 2000, with 6 the worst.**
- Conclusion: China is a big market, but distance matters along many dimensions.

### Geographic distance
- Attributes: physical remoteness; lack of a common border; lack of sea or river access; size of the country; average within-country distances to borders; topography; weak transportation or communication links (man-made infrastructure); differences in climate.
- Effects: transport cost for **low value-to-weight or bulk** products (**steel, cement**); costs for **fragile or perishable** products rise with distance.
- Intangibles too: **cross-border equity flows fall significantly as geographic distance rises**. This cannot be transport cost (capital is not physical); it is explained by the level of **information infrastructure** (crudely measured by telephone traffic and the number of branches of multinational banks).
- Firms that find geography a barrier to trade are expected to switch to **direct investment** in local plant, but research suggests geographic distance also dampens **investment flows**. Keep both information networks and transport infrastructure in mind.
- Industries most affected (CAGE table): low value-to-weight or bulk ratio (cement); fragile or perishable (glass, fruit); communications and connectivity important (financial services); local supervision and operational requirements high (many services).

### Economic distance
- The **wealth or income of consumers is the most important economic attribute** creating distance. Rich countries do relatively more cross-border activity relative to their size, mostly with other rich countries (positive correlation between per capita GDP and trade). Poor countries also trade more with rich countries than with other poor ones.
- Behind this are differences in cost and quality of **financial, human and other resources**. Two strategies:
  - **Replication**: companies relying on economies of **experience, scale and standardization** should focus on countries with **similar economic profiles**, because they must replicate their business model. **Wal-Mart in India would be a very different business from Wal-Mart in the United States; Wal-Mart in Canada is virtually a carbon copy.**
  - **Arbitrage**: competitive advantage from exploiting **cost and price differentials** between markets; industries whose major cost components vary widely across countries (**garments, footwear**, where labour cost matters) target countries with **different** economic profiles.
- For both, **supply chain and distribution channel disparities** are a significant barrier. A study found that **distribution margins within the United States** (domestic transport, wholesaling, retailing) are a bigger barrier to imports into the US, on average, than **international transport costs and tariffs combined**.
- Cross-country complexity puts a premium on responsiveness and agility; replicators struggle to match locally focused competitors. In home appliances, **Maytag** (few geographies) produced far better investor returns than **Electrolux and Whirlpool**, whose spread cost them simplicity and profitability.
- Industries most affected (CAGE table): demand varies with income (cars); economies of standardization or scale important (mobile phones); labour and other factor cost differences salient (garments); distribution or business systems different (insurance); companies need to be responsive and agile (home appliances).

### Industry sensitivity to distance (Ghemawat and Rajiv Mallick)
- Method: regressed trade between every pair of countries in each of **70 industries (SIC)** on each dimension of distance. Results confirm the need to separate the components: **electricity is highly sensitive to administrative and geographic distance but not at all to cultural distance.**

| Dimension (measure) | More sensitive | Less sensitive |
|---|---|---|
| Cultural (linguistic ties) | meat and meat preparations; cereals and cereal preparations; miscellaneous edible products and preparations; tobacco and tobacco products; office machines and automatic data-processing equipment | photographic apparatuses, optical goods, watches; road vehicles; cork and wood; metalworking machinery; electricity current |
| Administrative (preferential trading agreements) | gold, nonmonetary; electricity current; coffee, tea, cocoa, spices; textile fibers; sugar, sugar preparations and honey | gas, natural and manufactured; travel goods, handbags; footwear; sanitary, plumbing, heating and lighting fixtures; furniture and furniture parts |
| Geographic (physical remoteness) | electricity current; gas, natural and manufactured; paper, paperboard; live animals; sugar, sugar preparations and honey | pulp and waste paper; photographic apparatuses, optical goods, watches; telecommunications and sound-recording apparatuses; coffee, tea, cocoa, spices; gold, nonmonetary |
| Economic (wealth differences) | Economic distance **decreases** trade: nonferrous metals; manufactured fertilizers; meat and meat preparations; iron and steel; pulp and waste paper | Economic distance **increases** trade: coffee, tea, cocoa, spices; animal oils and fats; office machines and automatic data-processing equipment; power-generating machinery and equipment; photographic apparatuses, optical goods, watches |

- Note the economic column is different in kind: it does not rank sensitivity but splits industries into those where wealth differences cut trade (replication-type goods) and those where wealth differences raise trade (arbitrage-type goods such as coffee, tea, cocoa, spices).

### Case study in distance: Tricon Restaurants International (TRI)
- Tricon manages **Pizza Hut, Taco Bell and KFC**; **spun off from Pepsico in 1997**. TRI is its international arm, based in **Dallas**.
- At spin-off: restaurants in **27 countries**; **two thirds of revenues and an even higher share of profits from just seven markets**; limited operating cash flow plus Tricon's debt service left TRI with **less than one tenth** of the money that **McDonald's International** had to invest outside the US. In **1998** president **Pete Bassi** decided to rationalise by concentrating equity investment in a limited number of markets.
- **CPA chart ("A Flawed Approach")**: 20 non-US fast-food markets plotted by per capita income (x axis, 0 to 40,000 dollars) against per capita fast-food consumption (y axis, 0 to 400 dollars), bubble size = total consumption. Key: 1 Japan, 2 Korea, 3 Taiwan, 4 Puerto Rico, 5 Malaysia, 6 Australia, 7 Thailand, 8 New Zealand, 9 South Africa, 10 Singapore, 11 Saudi Arabia, 12 Poland, 13 Belgium, 14 France, 15 Germany, 16 Spain, 17 UK, 18 Canada, 19 Mexico, 20 China. Puerto Rico has the highest per capita consumption, Canada next; the big bubbles (biggest opportunities) sit centre-right: **Japan and Germany the largest**. On CPA, **Mexico (total fast-food consumption USD 700 million) ranks 16th of 20**, which would imply disposing of the Mexican investments.
- **Adjusted for distance**: adjusting each country's consumption for geographic distance from Dallas, Mexico's consumption falls less than any other country's and **Mexico jumps to 6th**. Adding the **common land border** and **membership in a trade agreement with the US** pushes Mexico to **2nd, after Canada**. Not all adjustments are positive: adjusting for **common language** (which Mexico lacks) drops Mexico to a **tie for second with the United Kingdom**. Mexico, which had been less than one tenth the size of Japan and Germany, ends up the second largest opportunity. Bassi: "Mexico is one of TRI's top two or three priorities."
- **Company-specific features** change distance too: firms with many **cosmopolitan managers** are less affected by cultural differences. TRI already owned **more than four fifths of its Mexican outlets** and had a **38 percent share** of the local market, well ahead of McDonald's, making Mexico even more attractive.

### Closing
- Factoring industry effects of distance is only a first step; a full analysis considers how the company's own characteristics increase or reduce distance. The CAGE framework is **necessarily subjective** but an important complement to the usual portfolio tools. "Technology may indeed be making the world a smaller place, but it is not eliminating the very real, and often very high, costs of distance."
- Further reading listed with the article: Sebenius, "The Hidden Challenge of Cross-Border Negotiations" (HBR March 2002), on how negotiation styles differ across regions; Porter, "Clusters and the New Economics of Competition" (HBR Nov-Dec 1998), on how geographic, cultural and institutional proximity creates clusters (Silicon Valley, Hollywood) that raise productivity, drive innovation and spawn new businesses.

### In-depth screening and final selection
- Take the two countries left and understand the industry there: attractiveness (Porter's five forces, to be covered in about two weeks) and barriers to entry; the ideal is low capital: home-made product, minimum customisation, shipped, local logistics partner.
- Detailed market analysis: do you need to adapt the product, language, packaging; will local customers resist a foreign product; can you match local prices and still have margin or EBITDA after shipping and distribution.
- Final selection: compare the two countries on macro specifics, industry success factors and attractiveness (including barriers and cost of entry) against your resources, capabilities and goals; find the gap; it is very unlikely a country matches fully; ask how complicated the gap is to correct.
- Then set specific goals for the chosen market (market, product, marketing, packaging, customisation).

## Traps and look-alikes
- **Earliest versus preliminary screening**: earliest starts from your own business model (URBAR: Spanish-speaking countries); preliminary is the macro country view with PESTEL. Both are "macro specifics" but only preliminary uses PESTEL.
- **Second versus in-depth screening**: second = CAGE distance, a country-level comparison; in-depth = industry-level (Porter five forces, barriers to entry, detailed market analysis). CAGE is not an industry tool and Porter is not a distance tool.
- **Country macro specifics versus country industry specifics versus company specifics**: the first three funnel stages are macro, in-depth is industry, and setting country/market specific goals is company specifics.
- **Administrative versus cultural distance**: language, religion, social norms, ethnicity are cultural; colonial ties, currency, trading blocs, political hostility, corruption, legal systems, tariffs are administrative. India and Pakistan are an administrative (hostility) case even though they share a language.
- **Geographic distance versus physical distance**: geographic includes size, internal distances, sea or river access, topography, climate, time zones and transport and communication infrastructure, not just miles.
- **Economic distance versus consumer wealth**: wealth or income is the most important economic attribute, but the dimension also covers cost and quality of natural, financial and human resources, inputs, infrastructure and knowledge.
- **Replication versus arbitrage**: replicators (scale, standardisation, Wal-Mart) want similar economies; arbitrageurs (garments, footwear) want different economies. Economic distance hurts the first and helps the second.
- **Cultural distance "most affects" examples**: TV (linguistic content), food (identity), cars and appliances (size), wines (quality association). Cement is the standard example of a product insensitive to culture but sensitive to geography.
- **Administrative "most affects" examples on the deck versus the article**: deck gives telecommunications (national security), aerospace (national reputation), taxi services (regulation); the article's longer list adds electricity, drugs, farming, mass transportation, oil and mining, infrastructure.
- **Frankel and Rose magnitudes**: colony-colonizer (+900) is far bigger than common currency (+340), trading bloc (+330), polity (+300), language (+200), colonizer shared (+190), border (+80), ocean (+50). Do not confuse "common colonizer" (+190) with "colony-colonizer relationship" (+900).
- **Star TV numbers**: USD 825 million is what News Corp paid; USD 500 million is the 1996 to 1999 loss; USD 141 million is the FY1999 loss; USD 111 million is FY1999 revenue.
- **Tricon rankings**: 16th of 20 (CPA), 6th (geographic distance only), 2nd after Canada (border and trade agreement), tied 2nd with the UK (after common-language adjustment). Mexico's company-specific factor was four fifths ownership and 38 percent share, not the ranking.
- **China sidebar percentages**: 54 percent worse than planned (versus 25 percent better) and 62 percent overestimated potential; 63rd of 90 on corruption (2000); S&P political risk 5 of 6 (6 worst).
- **CPA axes**: per capita income on x, per capita consumption (penetration) on y, bubble = total market size. CPA is not wrong about size; it is incomplete because it ignores distance costs.
- **High versus low barriers to entry**: high barriers make a market less attractive for a new entrant; low barriers more attractive. (Do not mix with the Session 6 view where high barriers protect an incumbent.)
- **Corruption index editions**: the slide shows TI 2020 with 180 countries; the deck's hidden file name says 2017 with 170. Answer 2020 and 180 if asked what the slide shows. In the article, the China figure is the 2000 index with 90 countries.
- **Coface versus Transparency International**: Coface rates business defaulting (country) risk A1 to E; TI scores perceived public-sector corruption 0 to 100.
- **Match or gap**: the final selection does not expect a perfect match; it asks whether the gap can be corrected and how complex that is.
