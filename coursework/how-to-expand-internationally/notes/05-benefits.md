# Session 5: Benefits and reasons to internationalize (Prof. Pablo Neira)

This unit is the first step of Neira's four-step method for expanding abroad (benefits and reasons, ready?, where?, how?). The deck is a catalogue of reasons a company may have for going international, grouped into five main drivers on the opening slide (home market, foreign market, competition, product, economies of scale and scope) plus a sixth bucket that applies to both home and foreign markets. Each reason comes with a company example, most of them drawn from Neira's own career (the port terminal in Guatemala, the Metrocompost plant in Vietnam, General Kinematics, the steel wire rod plant, URBAR, National Geographic). What the professor said matters most: the list is not exhaustive, but before you walk into a board meeting saying "we should expand", you must be able to name which of these reasons you are pursuing and what you are trying to achieve. Taught on 21 September 2026 in the second half of a double session; the first half was a recap of the global trends material from sessions 1-2, summarised at the end of these notes.

## Slide-by-slide

### Slide 1: Title
- "How to Expand Internationally", Prof. Pablo Neira, IE University, Session 5, March 2026. Subtitle: "Benefits and reasons to internationalize."

### Slide 2: Course map
- An arrow with five boxes in order: **Global trends**, **Benefits and reasons to internationalize**, **Ready to internationalize?**, **Where?**, **How?**
- Global trends is shaded green (already covered); this session is the second box.
- In class Neira framed the sequence as four questions: why do I want to go (benefits), is my company ready, which country, and by which entry mode (franchise, agent, subsidiary, and so on, covered later).

### Slide 3: Main drivers
- Five main drivers, numbered on the slide:
  1. **Home market**
  2. **Foreign market**
  3. **Competition**
  4. **Product**
  5. **Economies of scale and scope**
- Note the deck then presents them in a different order: foreign market first, then home market, then "both home/foreign markets" (a sixth group not on this list), then product, competition, and economies of scale and scope. The section numbers on later slides (2. Home market, 3. Both, 4. Product, 5. Competition, 6. Economies) follow that presentation order.

### Slide 4: Foreign market 1, growth
- **Seek opportunities for growth in new markets: increase sales and benefits** ("benefits" here means profits).
- The most basic reason. Neira: a for-profit company wants more money, so look for more places to sell.

### Slide 5: Foreign market 2, resource seeking (a to c)
- **Resource seeking opportunities**: access to capital, raw material, low labor costs and new technologies.
- a) **Be close to supply sources**: minerals (oil, zinc, copper) or agricultural products (rubber, tobacco, sugar). These resources are sometimes **central to the survival** of a company.
- b) **Global sourcing advantages and flexibility** in product sourcing: purchasing from the most appropriate supplier around the world regardless of its location.
- c) **Availability of new technology and capacity**: some companies source overseas because **domestic suppliers lack the capacity** and are not making the investments needed to stay competitive.

### Slide 6: Resource seeking (d and e)
- d) **Superior quality products**: search for quality inputs not available from domestic suppliers.
- e) **Gain access to lower-cost or better value factors of production.**
- Picture: a large call centre full of agents in blue shirts. The point: labour is a factor of production that can be sourced abroad at lower cost (Neira's example in class was Spanish call centres relocated to Latin America).

### Slide 7: Resource seeking (f), strategic assets
- f) **Access to strategic assets.** Grey text on the slide: "PM-Guatemala".
- Picture: a container **port terminal** with ship-to-shore cranes and a container ship at berth.
- The point: sometimes the reason to go abroad is a unique asset (a port concession) you could not obtain at home.

### Slide 8: Resource seeking (g), talent pools
- g) **Access to new talent pools.** International labour can offer unique advantages: increased productivity, **technical skills**, advanced language skills, diverse educational backgrounds and others.
- Pictures: the orange logo of the app **YoVivo!** ("All your photos. All your clouds. All in one place.") and its App Store screenshots (Organize, Enjoy, Encrypt, Share), with the caption "Featured as a Best New App in 31 countries in July 2015".
- The point: the app's founders in London hired their programmers in the Philippines (story in the class section).

### Slide 9: Foreign market 3, specific opportunity
- **To exploit a specific business or market opportunity.** Example: **Metrocompost in Vietnam**.
- Picture: a screenshot of the MetroCompost website ("recycling and environment", "more than 20 years of commitment, since 1990") showing a green composting plant and a project sheet: location **Hai Duong, Vietnam**; year of operation **2011**; treatment capacity **29,500 t/a**; material: organic fraction of municipal solid waste; composting system: static trenches; scope of supply: pretreatment installation, composting in static trenches, refining line, machinery (loaders, trucks).
- The point: a one-off tender can take a company to a country that was never in its strategy.

### Slide 10: Foreign market 4, partners
- **Invest in a potentially rewarding relationship with foreign partners**:
  - Create **joint ventures** with profitable partners.
  - Develop **partnerships** to grow business in a specific country. Grey text: **General Kinematics**.
  - **Build trust with local players.**
- Quote on the slide: "One reason for Netflix to establish an office in France was the company's need to win the trust of the French industry; to build ties with content-makers, filmmakers, producers and sales agents..."

### Slide 11: Foreign market 5, serving global customers
- **Serving global customers.** In several industries the primary driver of globalization has been the need to service global customers who demand **the same product, service and quality at various locations** around the world.
- Pictures (three groups of examples):
  - A supplier map of the **Ford S-Max** (Automotive News Europe) naming dozens of component suppliers (Mazzucconi, ABC Group, ThyssenKrupp, Mitsubishi Electric, Federal-Mogul, Webasto, Omron, Magneti Marelli, ZF, Getrag and others). Component makers follow the carmaker.
  - The **Big Four** auditors (PwC, Deloitte, EY, KPMG) and global banks (Goldman Sachs, HSBC, JPMorgan Chase, Lazard, Deutsche Bank, Merrill Lynch, Morgan Stanley, RBC Royal Bank, State Street).
  - "Top 10 advertising agencies in the world": Ogilvy & Mather, Mullen, Publicis Groupe, BBDO, Dentsu, Grey Global, Havas, Droga5, McCann Worldgroup, Young & Rubicam.
- The point: professional services, banks, ad agencies and auto suppliers internationalize because their clients do.

### Slide 12: Foreign market 6, learning (a)
- **Learning benefits.** a) **Gain ideas** about products, services **and business methods** that arise from being in foreign markets.
- Quote from the **CEO of IKEA Japan**: "One reason for us to enter the Japanese market, apart from hopefully doing very good business, is to expose ourselves to the **toughest competition in the world**. By doing so, we feel that we are expanding the quality issues for IKEA all over the world."
- Picture: the entrance of an IKEA store in Japan ("Welcome to IKEA", opening hours 10:00 to 20:00).

### Slide 13: Learning (b and c)
- b) **Access to new technologies** which may significantly improve operations.
- c) **Integration of knowledge** from different locations and the **creation of new knowledge through interacting with different national environments**.
- Critical requirement for exploiting these learning benefits: the company must possess some form of **global infrastructure for managing knowledge** that permits new experiences, ideas and practices to be **integrated and diffused**.
- Picture: Starbucks ("The best coffee for the best YOU"). The point: Starbucks observes consumer behaviour in each country, sends it to the parent, and diffuses what works to other stores.

### Slide 14: Foreign market 7, entry barriers and tariffs
- **Changes in entry barriers / avoid tariffs.**
- Picture: a The Local/AFP news article dated **3 October 2019**: "Cava, queso, olive oil and mariscos: Trump slaps extra tariffs on Spanish produce", with a photo of olive oil and Manchego cheese.
- The point: when a tariff is raised against your country, producing or routing from inside the barrier (or from another country) is a reason to internationalize.

### Slide 15: Foreign market 8, high demand
- **Countries where there is a high demand for the offering** (grey text: consumer goods/food; healthcare; housing).
- The study guide and class call this the **"pull effect"**: the foreign market pulls you in because demand is there and unsupplied.

### Slide 16: Home market 1 and 2
- Section heading "2. Home market".
- 1. **Local market is small.** Pictures: an aerial photo of a motorway (the Spanish highway network) and Google Maps of **Singapore** and its position in Southeast Asia (the island is tiny next to Malaysia and Indonesia).
- 2. **Slow growth** on the domestic market.
- The point: a market can be small physically (Singapore), small because the need is already met (Spain's highways are built), or small because people do not consume the product.

### Slide 17: Home market 3, saturation
- **When saturation in the home market occurs.** Grey text: "consumer goods, Z" (Z for Zara in Spain, per the study guide).
- Definition on the slide: a **saturated market** is the situation when **demand has been fully satisfied**. The only ways to sell more on a saturated market are:
  - **substitution or replacement** of the existing product;
  - **taking over market share** at the expense of other competitors;
  - **innovation** of a product in such a way as to move to an unsaturated market or to create an entirely new target market.
- Internationalization is the fourth way out: take the same product to a market that is not saturated.

### Slide 18: Home market 4, seasonality
- **To level out seasonal fluctuations in demand.**
- Text on the slide: **IDE Technologies** provides "snowmaking" to ski resorts around the world. It operates in **both hemispheres** (e.g. **Switzerland and South Africa**) to maintain consistent revenue: when it is summer in one part of the world and they cannot operate, they focus on the part where it is winter.
- Pictures: a snow cannon, a Spanish **jamón** (cured ham leg), and a row of **cava** bottles. Ham and cava are Christmas products in Spain; selling abroad fills the rest of the year.

### Slide 19: Home market 5, excess capacity
- **Make use of excess capacity (fill capacity).**
- Pictures: the website of **Celsa Group / Global Steel Wire** (GSW), coils of steel wire rod, and a hot rolling mill.
- The point: a plant with huge fixed costs must run near full capacity; export orders fill the gap when domestic orders fall short.

### Slide 20: Home market 6, 7, 8
- 6. **Excessive regulation** at home.
- 7. **Economic problems or recession.** Grey text: **URBAR** (Neira's industrial company, which went to Argentina after the 2008 Spanish recession).
- 8. **Diversification: risk reduction**:
  - To **reduce dependence on home market sales**.
  - To **offset negative growth in one market** by operating successfully in another.

### Slide 21: Both home and foreign markets
- Section "3. Both home/foreign markets". Three reasons that can arise on either side:
  1. **Changes in regulation.** Grey text: **UBER SPAIN**.
  2. **Tax reasons.**
  3. **Governmental reasons: incentives** to companies to internationalize (the home government pushing you out, or a foreign government pulling you in).
- Distinguish from slide 20: slide 20 is **excessive** regulation that already exists at home; slide 21 is regulation that **changes** after you have started operating, forcing you to move.

### Slide 22: Product 1, extend the life cycle
- **To extend the product's life cycle**: provide products and services which have **reached maturity** at home. Grey text: "fatmilk/cereals/coke".
- Picture: the **product life cycle** chart with four stages on a sales-versus-time curve: **introduction** (blue), **growth** (green), **maturity** (yellow, the peak), **decline** (red).
- The point: a product at maturity or entering decline at home can be at introduction or growth in another country (full-fat milk is declining in developed countries but still in high demand elsewhere).

### Slide 23: Product 2, exclusive product
- **To exploit an exclusive product or technology.**
- Neira: if you have something unique, do not test it in one country first; launch it across the planet.

### Slide 24: Competition 1 to 3
- Section "5. Competition".
- 1. **Competition in the home market is intense or growing.** Some companies find easier markets abroad than locally.
- 2. **Lower degree of competition abroad.** Many markets are still developing and have no strong structure of competitors.
- 3. **First-mover advantage**: businesses that expand into markets where their competitors do not operate often get a first-mover advantage that lets them **build strong brand awareness with consumers before their competitors** arrive.

### Slide 25: Competition 4, follower move
- **As a reaction to competitors' moves.** The **follower move**: entering a market because a major competitor has done so, driven by the belief that the competitor would gain a significant advantage if allowed to operate alone in that market.
- Text box on the slide: "**H&M** opened its first stores in India in **2017** after **Zara** expanded to India in **2010**, where it now has **13 stores**. In **2 out of the 3 years** since Zara opened in India it has made profits. This was a signal for H&M that it could also be successful in India's market. Its aim is to take Zara's spot as the world's number one apparel retailer."
- Pictures: a Zara logo, a Zara store, an H&M store.
- Order to remember: Zara first (2010), H&M the follower (2017). The class recording garbled this; trust the deck.

### Slide 26: Competition 4, offense as defense
- **"Offense as defense"**: a company enters the **home market of a competitor**, usually in **retaliation for an earlier entry into its own domestic market**. The objective is also to **force the competitor to allocate increased resources** to an intensified level of competition at home.
- Pictures: **Kodak vs Fujifilm** (film canisters and logos) and **Michelin** vs **Goodyear** logos.
- The point: when Fuji entered the US, Kodak attacked Japan; Michelin (French) and Goodyear (American) did the same to each other.

### Slide 27: Economies of scale and scope, definitions
- Section "6. Economies of scale & scope". Footer text: "M MNCs" (this driver mainly applies to multinational corporations).
- **Economies of scale**: savings in cost of production by **increasing the scale of production** (increasing the number of units produced). In areas like **sourcing, production, marketing, R&D**.
- **Economies of scope**: savings in cost by **producing multiple products using the same resource or capability**.
  - **Tangible** resources (distribution networks).
  - **Intangible** resources (global brand recognition or management capabilities).

### Slide 28: Develop economies of scale
- 1. Develop economies of scale (in sourcing, production, marketing, R&D):
  - a) **Reducing costs per unit**: costs are spread over a larger sales base, so profit per unit increases.
  - b) **ROI**: large investment projects (R&D, etc.) may **require global markets to justify the capital spent**; recover the cost across the maximum number of countries.

### Slide 29: Economies of scope, operational
- 2. Develop economies of scope: **operational and strategic**.
- a) **Operational**: synergies from **sharing resources** across businesses.
  - Sharing **tangible** resources (research labs, distribution networks) across multiple businesses.
  - Sharing **intangible** resources (brands, corporate reputation and technology) across multiple businesses.
  - **Global brand recognition**: the benefit that derives from having a brand that is recognized throughout the world.
- Picture: a **National Geographic** branded retail store (clothing, bags, merchandise). The point: a brand built in television and magazines can be stretched into retail products and stores in several countries.

### Slide 30: Economies of scope, strategic
- b) **Strategic**: synergies from the ability to **apply common management capabilities** to different businesses.
  - Transferring **functional / organizational capabilities** (marketing, product development) across businesses.
  - Applying core capabilities in **general management** to different businesses.
- Operational scope shares a **resource**; strategic scope transfers a **capability**. That is the distinction to remember.

## What the professor added in class

### Opening brainstorm and the growth reason
- Before showing the list Neira asked the class for reasons. Students offered: access to credit and funding, finding a market to reduce costs, friendlier regulation and taxes, raw materials, labour and energy, building an international brand, accessing technology, finding lower competition, and the reverse case of bringing a product found abroad back to the home market. He said all of them fit somewhere on the list.
- Growth: "this is a for-profit company, the more money you make the better", so the first reason is simply more sales.

### Resource seeking
- Flexibility of sourcing: if you depend on one supplier and its factory burns down, your production stops. Having suppliers in several countries gives flexibility in **cost and quality**, not only security.
- New technology and quality: sometimes no manufacturer in your home country can make a specific part or provide a service at the quality you need, so you have to look abroad.
- Lower cost: until recently the call centres of Spanish phone companies and utilities were moved abroad (you hear the accent is not Spanish); the reason was **lower labour cost**.
- Strategic asset (slide 7): a Spanish port-handling company (PM) that manages about **80 percent of loading and unloading in Spanish ports** was not planning to go to Central America. An agent asked whether they wanted to bid for a port terminal tender; they bid and won. Neira first said Colombia and corrected himself to **Guatemala**. Lesson: sometimes the reason is a unique asset you get to know about through an agent.
- Talent pool (slide 8): in 2013 Neira was external advisor to the YoVivo founders, **Javad (from Iran) and Filippo (from Italy)**, based in London, who had been running Deutsche Bank's IT. The app gathered your photos from phone, cloud and Dropbox, removed duplicates and organised them; it was best new app in **31 countries in July 2015**. They did not program it in London (too expensive, and they could not find programmers of the same quality and speed). They kept a pool of **20 to 25 programmers in the Philippines**, cut the work into pieces, assigned each piece to a group and followed them weekly, firing and replacing as needed. "Sometimes you need special talent that you do not have at home."

### Specific opportunity and partners
- Metrocompost (slide 9): when Neira ran a listed industrial manufacturing company it had a division that built turnkey waste-treatment and waste-to-energy plants. An agent brought a tender in **Vietnam**. "Was it within our strategy to go to Vietnam? Of course not." They bid and won. A waste plant can be simple (manual screening) or sophisticated (X-ray sorting of glass by colour, separating glass from plastic), depending on what the local government wants to spend.
- General Kinematics (slide 10): the same company had a division designing equipment for moving components and parts, medium to small size. **General Kinematics** in the US made similar equipment but much larger. During the **2007-2008 crisis** in Spain they agreed: we sell your equipment in Spain and Europe, you sell ours in the States. Lesson: a partner can even be a competitor with overlapping products if the combination works.
- Netflix in France: when Netflix arrived there was a strongly negative reaction from local players in a very local industry. Netflix opened an office, did marketing, hired and grew local people. "This was the key": **build trust**.

### Serving global customers
- Audit firms: if a client opens a subsidiary in a new country, a big audit firm will accompany it rather than risk losing the client. For a tiny client with a EUR 10,000 audit they will not bother; for a large phone company they will.
- Investment banks (Goldman Sachs, Bank of America) follow clients into new countries; so do advertising agencies.
- Automotive: when **Volkswagen set up a factory in Mexico**, its German component suppliers literally opened new plants around it. "They follow the customer, when it makes sense."

### Learning
- IKEA in Japan: do-it-yourself furniture is widespread in Japan and competition is terrible; IKEA was reluctant. The decision was: go, maybe we are not successful, but we will learn how the Japanese do business.
- Starbucks (slide 13): enter a country to observe how consumers behave, capture it, send it to the parent, and see whether it can be diffused to stores in other countries. Neira said this learning driver is really for large multinationals with presence in many countries, not for a startup or SME.

### Entry barriers and high demand
- Tariffs (slide 14): "this is not about norms, it is about customs duties." When Trump put tariffs on Spanish cheese and olive oil, many Spanish producers shipped the product to **Italy**, put an Italian sticker on it, and exported from there. They do not ship directly from Spain any more.
- High demand (slide 15): the example is **regular (full-fat) milk**. In developed countries consumption of regular milk is falling and semi-skimmed and skimmed are rising, but in other countries demand for regular milk is still very high. Also healthcare products and consumer goods.

### Home market reasons
- Small market (slide 16): students suggested three meanings: your capacity exceeds what the home market buys; the population is small; or people simply do not consume much of the product. Neira added Spain's **highways**: the network is built, so for the 5 or 6 big Spanish construction companies the home market is already small and they went to Latin America and Asia. A student added **Nigeria**: a huge market, but Nigerians prefer **powdered milk**, so dairy companies there export fresh milk and Nigerians import powder. Singapore: physically small.
- Saturation (slide 17): "no matter how much you produce and how much you try to sell, you will not sell more." Milk in Spain: people drink what they drink; a lower price will not make them go from 3 litres a week to 6. Same with **Coke and Coke Zero**: cutting the price per can does not raise consumption. So you must look at your excess capacity and find other countries.
- Seasonality (slide 18): snowmaking companies run in Switzerland and Europe in winter and go to **Chile and Argentina** in summer (the slide says South Africa; both illustrate the opposite hemisphere). Students added: a Kenyan fintech whose biggest market was Tanzania sold enormously in the tourist season (Serengeti, Zanzibar) and nothing in low season, so it had to expand to new markets and sectors; Italians who open **ice cream shops in Germany** for 4 or 5 months of summer and return to Italy. Neira: Spanish **ham** companies concentrate about **70 percent of sales in December and January** (Christmas gifts to clients), and **cava** likewise, so they need other markets for the rest of the year.
- Fill capacity (slide 19): Neira's company produced **steel wire rod**. The investment in a plant is enormous and you only **break even at 85 percent of capacity**. When orders for the next two or three months fell below that, they went to new markets, even ones they had never sold to, sometimes at a lower price with no margin, just to break even. "Sometimes you are simply obliged to look for alternative markets."
- Excessive regulation (slide 20): find countries where the regulation is not so tough.
- Recession (slide 20, URBAR): in 2007-2008 about **85 percent of the company's sales were in Spain**, spread across **15 or 16 sectors**. They had always assumed all sectors could not go bad at the same time; it happened. They looked at **Argentina and Chile** and established in Argentina.
- Diversification: "all your apples in the same basket." Do not wait for the crisis; anticipate and de-risk.

### Both markets
- Changing regulation (slide 21): the deck says Uber Spain; in class Neira told the **Uber in Colombia** story: Uber started, after two years the taxi industry brought a claim, Uber had to stop and leave, and came back years later in a different form. The point is the same: you start under one set of rules and the rules change.
- Tax: many large Spanish companies have set themselves up in **Ireland** (Neira said "Northern Ireland") because of **corporate income tax**. New taxes on your business at home can also push you out.
- Government incentives: in Spain the government body **ICEX** (Neira said "IFEX") helps Spanish companies internationalize. The reverse also exists: a foreign government offering incentives to attract companies.

### Product
- Life cycle (slide 22): a product entering decline at home, where no amount of extra production or cost-cutting sells more, can be revived in another market (milk again).
- Exclusive product (slide 23): companies with a unique new product do not start in the USA and see how it goes; they launch across the planet.

### Competition
- A Turkish student: he invested in **confectionery production in Ethiopia**. In Turkey it would have been impossible to compete with hundreds of small, medium and large producers; in Ethiopia a small-scale operation could compete. This is slide 24 point 2.
- First mover: with a decent product you get a customer advantage from being the first newcomer in a country.
- Follower (slide 25): H&M was reluctant about India because fast fashion there seemed complex. Zara went in (2010), was doing "okay, okay, okay" and then grew well; when H&M saw its competitor succeeding it changed its mind and followed. (The recording muddled who went first; the deck is the reference: Zara 2010, H&M 2017.)
- Offense as defense (slide 26): Kodak (US) and Fuji (Japan): when the Japanese company came to the US market, Kodak reacted by going into Japan. Same story with tyres: Michelin (French) and Goodyear (American) entered each other's home markets, and both are doing well in different countries.

### Economies of scale and scope
- A student defined economies of scale as decreasing the cost per unit by producing more; Neira accepted it.
- Scope: his illustration was a logistics warehouse that stores one type of product and sells spare capacity to store other products.
- ROI: healthcare (pharma) and similar companies with huge investments need customers from everywhere to pay back the investment.
- National Geographic (slide 29): a Spanish **family office** Neira worked with licensed the National Geographic brand for something the owner had never thought of: merchandise (t-shirts, trousers, shoes, climbing glasses) and branded stores. They opened stores in **Madrid (Gran Vía), London and Panama** and could open franchises. National Geographic had to approve every layout, design and colour. The channel has an estimated **250 million TV followers** and the brand is about **80 years old**. This is economies of scope from an **intangible** resource, the global brand.
- Strategic scope (slide 30): if your marketing or commercial department is "magic" with any product you give it, use it to promote more products. Neira is "fully convinced" that **good general management capabilities are fully transferable** across industries: a good general manager moved to another sector needs about six months to adapt and then performs. So a company with two or three such managers can widen its scope of businesses and hand them the new ones.

### Closing
- A student noted that some Indian companies export simply because the weak rupee means euro or dollar prices are worth far more than the local price. Neira: the list is not exhaustive; access to funding and currency reasons also exist.
- **EXAM:** Neira's closing rule: "you want to expand, wonderful, but you need to have clear and solid reasons why." Do not go to a board or your boss saying "we should expand here and there" without knowing what you are trying to achieve. This is only the first step of the method, but it must be done.

## Global trends recap (sessions 1-2 and the first half of sessions 5-6, no deck uploaded)

### Course framing (session 1)
- Neira (economics, Harvard MBA 1991, about 36 years of experience: Unilever, consulting, CEO of a listed industrial manufacturer, family offices, now BlackRock investments in Spain including a tungsten mine) teaches the strategy half; Mikel Aguirre teaches economic, geopolitical and country risk. Fifteen sessions; exam in session 15; Neira's graded work is a report plus class discussion on the Zara case; Aguirre has 4 or 5 cases.
- Tungsten aside: 85 percent of world deposits are in China; the largest mine outside China is in western Spain (Extremadura).

### The Fortune 500 dilemma (mortality rate)
- About half of today's Fortune 500 will be off the list in a few years (Neira said "less than 5 years" in class; the study guide says 7). Almost **90 percent of the 1955 list is gone**. Average lifespan on the list used to be 30 to 35 years. Of the **top 12 companies by market cap in 2000, only Microsoft** is still in the top 12.
- Market caps have gone from hundreds of billions to **trillions**; the three largest companies together are worth about the GDP of Spain (unclear figure on the recording). Tesla's valuation implies far more cars than it sells; expectations drive it.
- Why: **industry disruption**, forces based on new technology, new ideas and new ways of doing business that enter traditional sectors, blur their boundaries and bring new competitors. Not only technology: Airbnb also shifted power away from hotels.
- Disruption is both uncomfortable and destructive (layoffs, new factories) and an opportunity for companies and consumers.
- The **invisible competitor challenge**: new competitors come through another door, are not classified as competitors, and you notice them too late (a student's marketing agency lost clients to ChatGPT in 2022). Ask what a hotel, a taxi, a bank or a dentist is now; **motor vehicles versus mobility**: carmakers used to compete on engine power, now on the dashboard.

### Three primary roots of disruption
- **Technology: the great enabler.** Not new: the locomotive disrupted the stagecoach; steam (1784), electricity (mass production), computing, roughly every 100 years. Two critical impacts today: data has been **opened to everyone** ("data is the new oil"), and companies cannot use it all: **Tesla analyses only about 15 percent** of the data its cars capture.
- **Demographics: the silent force.** Urbanisation: world population 8.3 billion (India overtook China last year); around 2010 urban and rural populations were equal and urban keeps rising. Cities generate **80 to 85 percent of GDP** and attract talent and ideas. Video shown: half of humanity lives in cities, **two thirds by 2050**; cities generate more than 80 percent of world GDP; Europe needs more than EUR 600 billion a year of infrastructure investment; megacities (over 10 million people): only **New York and Tokyo in 1950**; Mexico City, São Paulo and Osaka joined by 1980; over 20 in 2010; **32 today**, **Asia has over half**; **41 megacities by 2030** holding nearly 9 percent of world population; fastest future urbanisation in **Africa**. Also rising **life expectancy** (people born after 2000 may pass 100; population heading to 9 or 10 billion even with flat birth rates) and **immigration**, both forcing companies to serve more people with limited resources.
- **Globalization: the amplifier.** Trade liberalization, the trend of the last 50 years, now carries a **question mark** (Trump, trade wars, tariffs on Spanish products announced within 24 hours of a political spat). New competitors from emerging markets produce decent quality at very low prices. **McKinsey: about 60 percent of cross-border purchase decisions are driven by lower price.**

### What is different now
- Two ideas: **accelerating industry disruption** and a **global growth shift**.
- Acceleration: technology explosion (printing, telescope, steam engine, then in the last two decades new technologies every month; a 2013 McKinsey list of potentially disruptive technologies; self-assembling robots, brain-to-vehicle interfaces, molecular robots, facial recognition (needed to activate a SIM card in China), extended reality (augmented, mixed, virtual; used by Nike, Amazon, IKEA to sell), hotel delivery robots in China, a guide robot in the Plaza Norte mall, police robots in Singapore, delivery robots at Purdue).
- **Combinatory technology**: technologies are now open and easy to combine. **Amazon Go** (2017): computer vision, deep learning and sensor fusion ("Just Walk Out") so you grab goods and leave with no checkout. A student noted it needed over 1,000 people in India reviewing video and was largely wound down; Amazon now sells the technology to supermarkets in Europe. The lesson is the combination, not the success.
- **B2C to C2B**: customers now tell companies what to sell. "Consumer articulation": things we used to pay for are free (international calls via Skype and WhatsApp; the 20-volume encyclopedia Neira sold for EUR 25). **90 percent of shoppers say reviews influence their buying**; most trusted sources are **family and friends (60 percent) and customer reviews (60 percent)**; **only 12 percent** look at what the supplier says. Companies respond with referral marketing (reward fans who share; Revolut-style invite bonuses). Both customers and companies will have more choices.
- **New business models**: from the linear value chain to **horizontal platforms** (a strong technology platform lets Apple go from PCs to iPod, iPhone, Apple TV, Watch and cars; Google from Maps and Mail to healthcare and cars) and then to **ecosystems** (the Uber ecosystem combining cloud, payments, maps). Most are **asset-light**: Alibaba, the world's largest retailer, owns no warehouse; Airbnb owns no hotels; Uber owns no taxis. This lets them diversify and enter new geographies at a speed never seen before.
- **Global growth shift**: cross-border data flows have exploded since 2015; in 2008 devices roughly equalled people; now 8.3 billion people and an estimated **28 billion devices within 5 years**. But data localisation rules are rising (a Kenyan fintech had to build local data centres in Tanzania and Uganda). Technology now connects **people**, not just devices, yet use is mostly domestic: **Facebook about 50 percent cross-border, YouTube about 20 percent, e-commerce 11 to 15 percent**. Ghemawat's HBR article: **distance still matters**, the hard reality of expansion; beliefs, religion, culture and taste still differ, and US firms have failed abroad by assuming what sells at home sells in China.
- Numbers to remember: world population about 8.2 to 8.3 billion; **5.5 billion internet users**; **only 12 percent of internet users buy cross-border**; cross-border e-commerce about **USD 700 billion**, reaching only about **8 percent of the population**. Neira's point: going from 8 to 20 or 25 percent is realistic, so the opportunity is enormous, and not only for Apple or Tesla but for SMEs and startups that pick the right country.
- Growth engines: in the past **BRIC** (Brazil, Russia, India, China); now **India, China, Africa and Southeast Asia**, about 60 percent of world population and **50 percent of expected global urban growth**; Europe, Latin America and North America are flat. A student: Prudential followed exactly this path, from the UK to the US and now headquartered in Asia (China, Indonesia, Vietnam) and Africa.
- Resources: limited or unlimited? Population growth raises consumption; technology helps by **reducing consumption** and by **creating alternative resources** (electric cars reduce fuel use, but in Spain they are only about 10 percent of the market against an expected 30 to 35 percent, real range is 200 to 250 km against claimed 350, and nickel for batteries has its own externalities). "Can innovation make the planet resource-rich?" Neira has no answer but believes each shortage attracts investment in alternatives: a seed of opportunity.

### Impact on businesses and leaders
- Global competition is greater than ever; competitors from Vietnam and China arrive with decent quality at much lower prices. **Chinese car brands took close to 15 percent of the Spanish market in 5 years**, fully equipped, and are moving upmarket. US versus China trade has reshaped the map since 2000. On the demand side, countries never considered as markets now have enough customers to matter.
- "The future is much faster than we think." No company can do everything in-house; some piece of the global value chain is always done better by someone else; be humble enough to use them.
- Incumbents must ask hard questions (a healthcare company's customer may now be the patient on an app, not the hospital). Doing nothing means "continuing our slow decline into obsolescence." **Kodak** had the data on digital and did nothing; **Blockbuster** turned down Netflix; **Nokia** and **Steve Ballmer** laughed at the iPhone ("USD 500 subsidised, no keyboard, not a good email machine; the Motorola Q is USD 99; I like our strategy"). In all three cases the data was available; **the failure was cultural**: they thought they were immune and had internal resistance from the CEO, executive team and board.
- New competitors grow flat for a year or two and then "go to the roof" in years three and four. Big companies now run departments that scan for digital competitors and buy or ally with them; small companies must at least stay alert. In Spain listed large manufacturers are only about 15 percent of GDP; thousands of smaller firms lack those resources.
- Marketing myopia (HBR, raised by a student): define yourself by the **need you satisfy**, not the product (Uber is logistics and transportation, Amazon is a marketplace), so you can disrupt yourself.
- Rules: see disruption as positive; be vigilant; keep an **anticipatory mindset**; try things without 100 percent certainty; **disrupt yourself before somebody else disrupts you**. Famous misjudgements: IBM's president in 1943 ("a world market for about 5 computers"), DEC's president ("no reason for any individual to have a computer"), Bill Gates ("640K should be enough").

## Likely exam angles

- Know that the deck lists **five main drivers**: home market, foreign market, competition, product, economies of scale and scope, and that a sixth group ("both home/foreign markets") holds changes in regulation, tax reasons and government incentives.
- Know that **foreign market** reasons are pull factors: growth, resource seeking, a specific opportunity, partnerships and trust, serving global customers, learning, entry barriers and tariffs, high demand ("pull effect").
- Know that **home market** reasons are push factors: small market, slow growth, saturation, seasonality, excess capacity, excessive regulation, recession, diversification and risk reduction.
- Know the seven sub-points of resource seeking (a to g): close to supply, global sourcing flexibility, new technology and capacity, superior quality, lower-cost factors, strategic assets, talent pools.
- Know which example goes with which reason: PM Guatemala port terminal = strategic asset; YoVivo and Philippine programmers = talent pool; Metrocompost Vietnam = specific opportunity; General Kinematics = partnership; Netflix France = build trust; Ford S-Max suppliers, Big Four, banks, ad agencies = serving global customers; IKEA Japan = learning (toughest competition); Starbucks = knowledge integration; Trump tariffs on cava, cheese, olive oil (3 October 2019) = entry barriers; IDE Technologies snowmaking, ham, cava = seasonality; Celsa Global Steel Wire = fill capacity; Singapore and Spanish highways = small home market; URBAR = recession; Uber Spain = changes in regulation; full-fat milk, cereals, Coke = product life cycle; Zara and H&M India = follower move; Kodak vs Fujifilm and Michelin vs Goodyear = offense as defense; National Geographic stores = economies of scope from a global brand.
- Know the definition of a **saturated market** (demand fully satisfied) and the three ways to sell more in one: substitute or replace the product, take share from competitors, innovate into an unsaturated or new market.
- Know the four stages of the **product life cycle** (introduction, growth, maturity, decline) and that internationalization extends a product that has reached maturity at home.
- Know that IDE Technologies operates in **both hemispheres** (Switzerland and South Africa) to level out seasonal demand.
- Know that **H&M entered India in 2017 after Zara in 2010**; Zara had 13 stores and was profitable in 2 of 3 years; that was the signal for H&M (follower move).
- Know that **offense as defense** means entering a competitor's home market in retaliation to force it to spend resources defending at home.
- Know that **first-mover advantage** is about building brand awareness before competitors arrive.
- Know that the **learning benefit** requires a global infrastructure for managing knowledge so ideas can be integrated and diffused.
- Know the definitions: economies of **scale** = lower unit cost from more units; economies of **scope** = savings from using the same resource or capability for multiple products.
- Know that economies of scale have two sub-benefits: lower cost per unit and justifying the **ROI** of large investments (R&D) across many countries.
- Know that economies of scope split into **operational** (sharing tangible resources such as labs and distribution networks, and intangible resources such as brands, reputation and technology) and **strategic** (transferring functional capabilities such as marketing and product development, and general management capabilities).
- Know that global brand recognition is an **intangible** resource and a distribution network is a **tangible** one.
- Know the steel wire rod rule of thumb: break-even at **85 percent of capacity**; and that URBAR had 85 percent of sales in Spain across 15 or 16 sectors in 2008 and went to Argentina.
- Know the global trends numbers: three drivers (technology the enabler, demographics the silent force, globalization the amplifier); 90 percent of the 1955 Fortune 500 gone; only Microsoft left from the 2000 top 12; Tesla analyses 15 percent of its data; cities generate 80 to 85 percent of GDP; 32 megacities today, 41 by 2030, Asia over half; McKinsey 60 percent buy abroad for lower price; 90 percent of shoppers influenced by reviews, only 12 percent trust the supplier; 12 percent of internet users buy cross-border; cross-border e-commerce about USD 700 billion, about 8 percent of the population; Facebook 50 percent, YouTube 20 percent, e-commerce 11 to 15 percent cross-border; Chinese cars about 15 percent of the Spanish market in 5 years.
- Know that Kodak, Blockbuster and Nokia had the data; their failure was **cultural**, not informational.
- Know Neira's rule: have clear, solid reasons before proposing expansion; this is the first step before "ready", "where" and "how".

## Traps and look-alikes

- **Home market vs foreign market reasons**: the same word can appear on both sides. "Slow growth" and "saturation" are home (push); "high demand" and "growth opportunities" are foreign (pull). Excess capacity is home; serving global customers is foreign.
- **Excessive regulation (home market, slide 20) vs changes in regulation (both markets, slide 21)**: the first is rules that are already too tough at home; the second is rules that change after you start (Uber Spain). Tax reasons and government incentives sit with "changes in regulation" in the "both" group, not under home market.
- **Entry barriers / tariffs (foreign market reason 7) vs changes in regulation**: tariffs are customs duties, Neira stressed, not norms. The deck files tariffs under foreign market, with the Trump 2019 article.
- **Saturation vs small market vs slow growth**: small = the market was never big (Singapore, highways built); slow growth = big but not growing; saturated = demand fully satisfied, so even price cuts do not raise volume (milk, Coke).
- **Seasonality vs excess capacity**: both are about filling the plant, but seasonality is a predictable calendar swing (snowmaking, ham, cava, ice cream) and excess capacity is structural volume above home demand (steel wire rod needing 85 percent utilisation).
- **Recession (home reason 7) vs diversification (home reason 8)**: recession is reacting to a crisis that has already hit (URBAR 2008); diversification is anticipating it to reduce dependence on home sales and offset negative growth in one market with another.
- **Strategic asset (resource seeking f) vs specific business opportunity (foreign reason 3)**: the port terminal is an asset you gain access to; the Metrocompost plant is a one-off project or tender. Both arrived through agents; the deck keeps them in separate reasons.
- **Talent pool (resource seeking g) vs lower-cost factors (resource seeking e)**: Philippine programmers were chosen for quality and speed as much as cost (talent pool); call centres moved purely for labour cost (lower-cost factors).
- **Learning (a) gaining ideas vs (c) knowledge integration**: IKEA Japan illustrates exposing yourself to tough competition to gain ideas; Starbucks illustrates integrating knowledge across locations, which needs a global knowledge infrastructure.
- **Follower move vs offense as defense**: both are reactions to a competitor. Follower = go where the competitor went, so it does not enjoy that market alone (H&M following Zara into India). Offense as defense = go into the competitor's home market in retaliation to make it spend at home (Kodak into Japan, Fuji into the US; Michelin and Goodyear).
- **First-mover advantage vs lower competition abroad**: lower competition is a static condition of the foreign market (Ethiopian confectionery); first-mover advantage is the benefit of arriving before your known rivals and building brand awareness first.
- **Economies of scale vs economies of scope**: scale = same product, more units, lower unit cost (and ROI on big investments); scope = different products, same resource or capability.
- **Operational scope vs strategic scope**: operational shares a **resource** (tangible: labs, distribution networks; intangible: brand, reputation, technology; National Geographic). Strategic transfers a **capability** (functional such as marketing or product development; general management).
- **Tangible vs intangible resources**: distribution networks and research labs are tangible; brands, corporate reputation, technology and management capabilities are intangible.
- **Zara and H&M order**: Zara 2010, H&M 2017. The class recording reversed it; the deck is right.
- **IDE Technologies hemispheres**: the slide says Switzerland and South Africa; Neira said Chile and Argentina in class. Either way the point is both hemispheres; if a question quotes the deck, South Africa.
- **Uber example**: deck says Uber Spain; class story was Uber Colombia. Same lesson: regulation changed after entry.
- **PM port terminal country**: Guatemala (the deck says PM-Guatemala; Neira first said Colombia and corrected himself).
- **Three drivers of disruption**: technology is the **enabler**, demographics the **silent force**, globalization the **amplifier**. Do not swap the labels.
- **Reviews statistics**: 90 percent are influenced by reviews; 60 percent trust family and friends; 60 percent trust customer reviews; only 12 percent trust the supplier. Separately, 12 percent of internet users buy cross-border. Same number, different facts.
