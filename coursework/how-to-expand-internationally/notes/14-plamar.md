# Session 14: Trade finance, Venezuela and the Plamar, C.A. case (IE GE1-015-I)

Prof. Mikel Aguirre, 4 Nov 2026. This unit uses a small real Venezuelan family plastics firm, Plásticos Martínez, C.A. (Plamar), to teach one financing tool, **factoring**, and one way of reading a balance sheet from a developing country. The case (June 2003) asks whether Plamar should stay in Venezuela or expand abroad, which of Brazil, Guatemala or the Dominican Republic to pick, and above all how to pay for it: a plant abroad costs about **USD 300,000** and the company has only about USD 70,000 of cash plus USD 152,000 of receivables. The deck adds the mechanics of factoring and invoice discounting, the off-balance-sheet effect, the cash conversion cycle and three financial scenarios. The professor said his exam questions "come from the cases" and flagged four things for this one: **where the money comes from, what factoring is, why that destination, and how to read the balance sheet** (currency first, year second).

## Part 1: The case, section by section

### Header and opening scene

- Case GE1-015-I, written by Alejandro Almenar, Miguel García, Carlos Martínez and Fabio Martínez under Prof. Joseph Ganitsky at IE Business School. Original 12 June 2003, revised 27 October 2008, translated 19 September 2010. Epigraph: "Better to become than to be born being" (Marco Fidel Suárez).
- June 2003. **Francisco Martínez**, president and founder of Plásticos Martínez, C.A. (**Plamar**), is sipping coffee on Calle Serrano in Madrid with his sons **Francisco Jr.** (business manager) and **Carlos** (former marketing manager, now an MBA candidate at a prestigious business school in Madrid).
- Plamar: plastics manufacturer, set up in Venezuela in **1994**; in just 9 years became leader in the **packaging seals and metal handles** niche. Also in industrial plastic bags and shrink-wrap. Exports to **Guatemala, the Dominican Republic and Bolivia**.
- Venezuela is in one of the worst economic crises in its history with a politically uncertain process. The four questions on the table: set up a subsidiary in a more stable neighbouring country; diversify into new businesses; grow through exports; or expand domestically via acquisitions or mergers.

### The founder

- Francisco Martínez: born **1949 in Linares, Andalusia**; married, three children; studied **Chemical Engineering at the Barcelona Polytechnic University**.
- **1969**: production manager at a PVC factory in Barcelona, with frequent travel to Venezuela to monitor operations. He saw his expertise was valuable there.
- **1976**: moved to Venezuela offering independent advice to local industry.
- **1978**: founded a **plastics recycling firm**; later added **packaging seals** and was soon supplying **over 70% of the market**.
- **1989**: sold his businesses and returned to Spain; set up a **plastic bag factory in Granada**.
- Spain was much more competitive than Venezuela; the company went **bankrupt in 1991**, "the toughest year of his career".
- He returned to Venezuela and, **with funding from an investor**, set up a packaging factory run with his sons.
- Family roles: father, general management; **Francisco Jr.**, administration and operations; **Carlos**, sales and marketing; **Javier** (youngest, last to join), technology (new machinery, process automation).
- Management philosophy: a dynamic structure that learns from mistakes; stability and security for customers and employees (high loyalty, low turnover). Unlike typical small firms, he **delegated decision-making to his sons** for their development. He felt the future of the company was in their hands.

### The origins of Plamar

- Plamar began operating in **1994** making **cleaning products** for consumers (chlorine, bleach, washing-up liquid). Returns were very low, so they went back to plastics.
- First machine: an **incomplete plastic extruder**, repaired with their own technical skills. It had no gearbox; they fitted one **bought at a car breaker's yard**. They worked in the **garage at home**.
- First product: **plastic laminated tubing for low-cost imitation wicker (synthetic rattan) furniture**, using **recycled PVC from toy manufacturers**. Fragmented, unprofitable customer base; idle capacity; high competition; low margins.
- Why they were still cost competitive: depreciation of second-hand machinery with low residual value, low rent, intensive use of labour. This gave margins above the industry average.
- Problem with recycled raw material: lower quality, heavy wear of extruder nozzles, frequent stops, lower productivity. They wanted a less crowded niche using original raw material and their excess capacity.
- They designed special equipment themselves, improving quality and processes and opening other markets.
- **Packaging seals** (as used in the paint industry): a niche sold exclusively to manufacturers of plastic containers for industry. In **two years** they won **90% of the national market**. They had **seven clients**, all manufacturers of **5, 4 and 2.5 gallon containers** (case footnote: one gallon equals 3.17 litres). Success came from competitive prices (intensive family work) and flexibility on delivery times and forms. The only local competitor that had supplied this market **was forced to close**.
- **Metal handles, 1997**: not plastic, but needed by the same customers. Suggested by **Carlos**, who saw handles rejected for quality in customers' warehouses and did his own price and quality inquiries.
- Initially rejected: it needed a considerable machinery investment the company could not afford, and **Vicson**, the leading wire manufacturer, was reluctant to supply raw material.
- Solution: an engineer they knew at **Galtreca**, a Vicson competitor **600 km away**. Partnership: Galtreca taught them the handle process, helped design Plamar's **first handle-making machine** and began supplying wire. Foothold without major investment.
- The first machine was entirely manual: wire cut by another company, plastic grip fitted by hand (Annex 4). High labour cost, low productivity. But customers approved quickly and Plamar took a **50% market share in a year and a half**.
- Continuous improvement: monthly production rose from **50,000 to 300,000 units**. Then they bought **two automatic machines in Brazil**, cutting the wire-handle section from **18 to 6 employees** with a big quality improvement.
- Late 1997: production stable, less top-management attention needed. But handles and seals were **seasonal**: Venezuelans paint their homes at Christmas, so a lot of paint is used **September to December**; nearly **50% of the company's sales** fell in that period.
- To reduce seasonality Francisco entered **plastic bags and polyethylene shrink-wrap**. He bought a **second-hand extruder and bag-sealing machine in Spain**, both needing repairs and a new motor. From the start, customers absorbed almost **50% of the output** of the first machines. Later they bought a **second-hand Italian machine in excellent condition** from an equipment importer who could not sell it because of the Venezuelan market's difficulties.

### Company operations: the four products

| Product | Process | Raw material and supplier | Customers | Competition and share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Plastic packaging seals** | Extrusion, ends welded with a heating element | Flexible PVC from domestic suppliers | Manufacturers of plastic packaging for industry; **7 such companies** in Venezuela | Plamar supplies **90%** of the domestic market |
| **Metal handles for containers** | Automated cutting, bending, assembly | Wire of various diameters from domestic suppliers | Same firms as seals | About **70%** share; one competitor with over 20 years in the industry lost share through technological obsolescence |
| **Industrial plastic bags** | Extrusion, then sealed in a separate machine | Low density polyethylene (LDPE); the **only domestic supplier is PDVSA**, the state oil company | Almost **70% of manufacturing industry** (packing bottles, caps, shoes, clothes, computers) | Much higher: **over 35 plastic bag manufacturers** |
| **Polyethylene shrink-wrap** | Extrusion, then separated into layers | LDPE | Very large: foods, soft drinks, beer | Major competitors, **excess capacity** in the sector |

- Packaging manufacturers (the seals and handles customers) tend to have **over 100 workers**, are **family-owned**, with tight control of costs and investments.
- Bag and shrink-wrap buyers range from small water bottlers to large food corporations. Procurement is complex, involving quality control, production and procurement departments.
- Marketing: **personal direct sales**; the seller does everything from demand forecasting to product development. The personal relationship is key to stable, lasting agreements.

### Financing

- From the outset Plamar was financed **almost entirely from equity**.
- After the Spanish bankruptcy Francisco had no capital. His wife's parents paid the return tickets; the family stayed three months at a former neighbour's home in Venezuela.
- Founded with capital of **less than USD 5,000**, built up over a year of work.
- All funds generated were **reinvested**; **no formal dividends for more than 4 years**. The business was the family's only means of support; the accounts of company and family were sometimes hard to separate.
- Francisco Jr. and Carlos tried to get **bank financing several times without success**: every application was rejected **for want of a guarantee** (collateral).
- Result: the only sources of finance were **the company's suppliers and its cash flow**.

### Internationalisation

- Entering export markets was fairly easy but **without a clear strategy**. Francisco: "We got into the international market more as a result of taking up specific opportunities than having a clear internationalisation strategy."
- First overseas contact: via a **Venezuelan exporter of industrial coatings** to packaging factories in the **Dominican Republic**. Those manufacturers were unhappy with their suppliers: they bought **handles in the US** and **container seals in Argentina**. Samples were sent and a quotation given quickly.
- The speed won the first order but caused problems. Carlos: the delivery was **two weeks late**, unexpected costs were incurred, yet the transaction was profitable and the disagreements were smoothed over.
- Then **Guatemala** (packaging manufacturers, a new client) and **Bolivia** (seals for containers, shipped by **air freight**).

### Venezuela's crisis (2003)

- Chávez had been in power about **5 years**. Venezuela: **fifth largest oil exporter**, strategic crude supplier to the US. **60% of voters opposed Chávez**, but class-struggle rhetoric and government control of the means of production kept him in power.
- Economic policy modelled on China: an authoritarian government dominating the economy with politically passive business people; measures to promote small businesses and foreign investment on the assumption foreign capital would stay out of politics.
- Opposition divided; a **referendum** possible from August.
- The Economist on de-industrialisation: **GDP to shrink 12%** by year end (excluding the general strike effects); **60% of manufacturing businesses** existing when Chávez took power set to close by year end; currency controls strangling business: since February only **USD 100 million** of purchases authorised, normally enough for **3 days' business**.
- Oil: **50% of government revenues, 80% of exports, 25% of GDP**.
- Long protectionism and underinvestment left manufacturing uncompetitive, in contrast with capital-intensive petrochemicals with the highest technology. Private consumption had an excessive weight in GDP. Oil price swings drive stability.
- General strike plus exchange restrictions: economic slowdown of **-29% year-on-year**, **-12% at year end** once strike effects fade. A rebound expected next year but output at its lowest since **1991**. Weak demand would cushion the inflationary effect of bolívar devaluations; devaluations reduce incentives to invest in equipment, postponing the renovation of manufacturing technology; but **exports would be promoted**, giving a trade and current account surplus.

### Plastics in Latin America in the new millennium

- Aggregate plastic consumption is an indicator of industrialisation and economic vitality. Drivers of a national plastics industry: access to raw materials on competitive terms, industrial development, technological skills.
- Biggest consumers of plastic raw materials: **Brazil and Mexico**, then **Argentina and Venezuela**. After the crises in the last two, absolute consumption fell but relative positions did not change. Venezuela is in **last place** (among the big four) despite being the world's fifth largest crude exporter.
- Company size by employees: Venezuela **third, behind Costa Rica and Colombia**, but **El Tablazo** (a PDVSA subsidiary) accounts for much of production. **45% of plastics firms in Venezuela employ fewer than 50 people**.
- Imports of plastics grow much faster than exports; the trade gap widens every year. But Venezuela has a **comparative advantage in Plamar's sector**: **39% of Venezuela's plastics exports are bags and packaging**, and this item is not among the main imports.
- Throughout the Southern Cone there is a shortfall in plastic product manufacturing (trade deficits).

### Discussions over a cup of coffee (each person's position)

Plans were sketched on a napkin for when Carlos returns after finishing his MBA in **July 2003**.

- **Francisco (father), first position: stay and invest in Venezuela.** Handles and seals offer no further domestic growth, but **industrial bags and shrink-wrap** could justify investment to win share. The crisis closed **more than 40% of plastics manufacturers in the last 3 years**, so competitors can be acquired **at a big discount**. Biggest problem: nobody knows whether Venezuela has bottomed out, nor how far the situation could affect them.
- **Francisco Jr.: export more intensively.** Past success is no guarantee. Look for opportunities wherever they are: export more to the markets they already serve and to other countries in the region. Only **metal handles and packaging seals** would be exported, but these give the **highest returns**. International competitors are in **Argentina and the US** and find these small markets unattractive. Plamar's advantage: **knowledge of the export process** (contacts with customs agents and shippers took much time and effort) and highly **concentrated potential customers**. Risk: Venezuela's political and economic instability and **poor international image**.
- **Carlos: set up a production plant in a neighbouring country** with which they already trade. Benefits: reduce **country-specific risk**, gain **access to foreign exchange**, access **regional blocs** for easier regional trade. On his Central America trip last year he contacted **more than 10 potential customers** willing to buy if Plamar were located nearby; they wanted **reliable and flexible deliveries**.
- **Francisco on cost:** the challenge is the investment: about **USD 300,000** for a plant with capacity of **300,000 metal handles and 300,000 container seals a month**.
- **Francisco Jr. on operational risk:** you must first understand how people in that country work, which takes time; who would move there? The team should stick together. But Carlos's idea makes sense as a way to cover the risk at home.
- **Carlos, more pragmatic:** maybe cover the risk by investing in **new businesses in Venezuela** and changing line of business; or invest **in Spain** (Francisco and Francisco Jr. were in Spain at a plastics trade fair looking at equipment to buy). Carlos could stay in Spain and open a **franchise or another business**, so "not all our eggs in one basket".
- **Francisco, closing position: caution.** Perhaps make no investment until there is clarity about Chávez's economic measures; there may be a referendum to end his mandate. Worst case, **divest and return to Spain**, avoiding past mistakes, though that means renouncing their basic knowledge of the business. He put off the decision so all three could reflect.

### Annex 4 (plastic types) and the SPI identification code

- Annex 4 in the text is the picture of the manual handle assembly; the plastics reference lists the **six SPI code types** plus "other":
- **PET** (polyethylene terephthalate): from terephthalic acid and ethylene glycol by polycondensation; textile grade and bottle grade. Uses: soft drink, oil and water bottles, mayonnaise containers, textile fibres, tapes, x-ray film. Gas barrier, transparent, unbreakable, light, non-toxic.
- **HDPE**: thermoplastic from ethylene (from ethane in natural gas). Detergent and bleach packaging, motor oil, dairy, drums, water pipes, supermarket bags. Withstands low temperatures, unbreakable, impermeable, non-toxic.
- **PVC**: from **43% gas and 57% common salt**; needs compounds with additives; rigid or flexible. Water, oil and juice containers, door and window frames, pipes, hoses, blister packs, catheters, blood bags. Fire and weather resistant, non-toxic, impermeable, unbreakable. (Plamar's seals are flexible PVC.)
- **LDPE**: from natural gas; versatile. All types of bags, agricultural film, automatic food packaging, serum bags, cosmetics tubes, irrigation pipes. Non-toxic, flexible, light, impermeable, economical, transparent. (Plamar's bags and shrink-wrap; sole Venezuelan supplier PDVSA.)
- **PP** (polypropylene): polymerisation of propylene; rigid, crystalline, high melting point, chemical resistance, low density. Cigarette, gum and sweet film, syringes, lids, upholstery fibres, battery cases, bumpers, hot water pipes. Temperature resistant, odour barrier.
- **PS** (polystyrene): crystalline PS (glossy) and high-impact PS (with polybutadiene). Dairy and ice-cream pots, trays, disposable razors, plates, cutlery, toys, cassettes, insulators. Fire resistant, transparent, easy to clean.
- **Other**: polycarbonates, polyamides, polyurethanes, acrylics (PMMA): car parts, computer casings, phones, aerospace, furniture, sports goods.

### Appendix I: Country analysis

- **Venezuela**: Caribbean coast makes it a strategic port for US trade; borders Brazil (south), Guyana (east), Colombia (west). Area **912,050 km2**; population about **26 million (2002 estimate, OCEI)**; currency **bolívar**; Caracas about 7 million people and most economic activity; other industrial centres Maracaibo, Valencia, Puerto Ordaz. World's **fifth biggest oil exporter**; also gas, aluminium, steel, hydroelectricity (to Brazil and Colombia), cacao, rice, coffee. Oil is **over 50% of income and 80% of exports**: evidence of an uncompetitive national industry. **2002**: GDP **-8.9%**, fiscal deficit **3.7%**, bolívar devaluation **over 150%** against the dollar, unemployment **18%**, inflation **22.4%** (down from 2001, but at the cost of a **drop in domestic consumption of more than 25%**).
- **Brazil**: post-war import substitution; by the 1970s protectionism had made industry inefficient. **Collor de Melo (1990)** removed non-tariff barriers and cut tariffs, cheapening inputs and raising productivity. Exports limited in the 1990s, grew after the **1999 real devaluation**; **2001 exports +5.7%** but mostly low value added. Chronic underinvestment: poor transport and electricity; disorderly privatisation, overcapacity, unclear tariffs. Interest rates fell from a **45% peak** in the early 1990s but stayed high, so SMEs struggle to finance and get export assistance. **January 2003: Lula (Workers' Party) took office**, balancing a social agenda with orthodox policy and dependent on a **USD 30 billion IMF loan**. Rigid fiscal policy and inflation commitment cut country risk. The real not expected to appreciate enough to hurt exports; rates expected to fall from 2004; exports the most active demand component.
- **Guatemala**: Central America; **25 years of civil war**; GDP growth averaged **3.08% a year 1997-2001**. Agriculture: coffee (**3rd largest coffee exporter in 2001**), cocoa, rice, bananas, cacao. 2001: **inflation 2%**, **GDP per capita USD 3,646**, **currency stable against the US dollar** (its main trading partner), trade deficit down from 5.5% to 4.6% of GDP. **Bilateral free trade agreements with Chile, Panama and the Dominican Republic**; privatisation of energy and telecoms; electrical interconnection with Central America. Reads as an opening, strengthening economy.
- **Dominican Republic**: Caribbean island, tourism destination; agriculture and food (sugar, cocoa, coffee, bananas) **13.6% of GDP**; tourism a key source of foreign exchange. 2001, facing an energy crisis, President **Hipólito Mejía** launched reforms: decentralisation of state industries, **devaluation of the peso**, radical tax reform. Post-2001 indicators deteriorated: **GDP growth below 3%** (less than half the previous five years), **inflation up to 8.6%**, **GDP per capita down to USD 5,294**. Positives: fiscal **surplus of 1.5% of GDP**, higher services surplus curbing the current account deficit.

### Appendix II: The plastics industry by sector

- **Packaging**: light (saves raw material and transport fuel, less pollution), recyclable, an alternative energy source, reusable, durable, versatile (rigid or flexible, thick or thin), hygienic. Most consumed packaging materials: HDPE, LDPE, linear PE, PET, PP, PS, PVC.
- **Healthcare**: syringes, contact lenses, prostheses, blood and serum bags, gloves, dialysis filters, pacemakers.
- **Farming ("Plasticulture")**: output can be **tripled**; mulching, tunnels, greenhouses, irrigation, silage sheeting, reservoir linings.
- **Transport and communications**: about **1,700 of the 5,000 parts** of a car are plastic; each car has some **123 kg of plastics replacing 300 kg** of other materials, saving up to **750 litres of fuel** over its life. Main automotive plastics: PP, then PU and PVC. Airbags.
- **Building**: **PVC is 61%** of plastics used, polyethylene 14%, polyurethane 8%, polystyrene 8%. Virtually all buildings after 1950 contain plastic pipes, windows, roofing, flooring, cable sheathing, insulation.
- **Electricity and electronics**: insulation and protection; the "Information Age" depends on plastics; PVC for cable coating is **38.7%** of the sector's consumption.
- **Other**: toys, sport, appliances, furniture, textiles, footwear. A final chart shows market shares of plastic material consumption (values unclear in the text).

### Slide 5: A financing solution (the cash gap picture)

- Timeline between **seller** and **buyer**, in days: day 0 inventory arrives and the supplier's invoice arrives; **day 30** the seller pays that invoice (end of payables); **day 60** the seller ships and invoices the buyer (inventory period ends, receivables start); **day 120** the buyer pays.
- The red arrow from day 30 to day 120 is the **cash gap** (about 90 days): the seller has paid for inputs but has not yet been paid. The **factor**, drawn underneath, closes the gap with **receivables financing**: it pays the seller against the invoice and collects from the buyer.
- Slide note (in French): this is more about **liquidity than bad debt**; "closer to factoring than to credit insurance".

### Slide 6: Customer benefits of factoring

- **Need of working capital**: factoring is a financing product giving **liquidity up to 90% of the invoice value**; better liquidity means more buying power and purchase advantages with suppliers (creditors).
- **Improvement of balance sheet ratios**: factoring can have an **"off balance sheet" effect** on how receivables are treated in the accounts.

### Slide 7: Factoring and invoice discounting (the three-party diagram)

- Three parties: **Seller/Client** (the company with invoices), **Buyer/Debtor** (its customer), and the **Factor**.
- Factor to client: **financing** (cash against invoices) and **debtor risk cover**. Client to debtor: **notification** (dotted line, may or may not happen). Client keeps **in-house trade receivables management** in this variant.
- Slide notes: **invoice discounting or in-house factoring** includes financing of purchased invoices before maturity and debtor risk cover **up to 100%**; it can be **notified or not**; but it **does not include sales ledger management and dunning**, which the client keeps doing.
- Target clients for this product: **medium to large companies with a well-organised administration**. The factor needs low "ceding risk" because it (Coface) becomes **owner of the receivables**, so it must be sure the client really runs collection properly. Proposed to companies that want to **improve balance sheet ratios and ratings**.

### Slide 8: Factoring (the two-ledger mechanism)

- Month 1: the client sends its **sales ledger** (invoices and credit notes) to the factor; the factor **finances the invoices up to the agreed percentage**.
- Month 2: a second sales ledger. The factor **compares the two ledgers**: payments declared in the ledger are matched against **cash received in the trust account**.
- The trust account is **opened in Coface's name or pledged in favour of Coface**. If the comparison leaves a positive amount, the factor pays it out.

### Slide 9: Invoice discounting

- Same two-ledger comparison, but the specificity is that the factor **also considers and finances intermediate invoices** issued between the two monthly ledgers. Financing is more continuous.

### Slides 10 to 12: Off-balance-sheet effect in three steps

- **Slide 10, initial**: Assets = fixed assets, stock/inventory, **accounts receivable** (large), bank deposit. Equity and liabilities = equity, **need of working capital requirements** (the short-term financing that funds the receivables), **suppliers' credit**. Both columns sum to **Total**.
- **Slide 11, factoring**: the receivables block shrinks and the **bank deposit grows** by the same amount (arrow up). Nothing else changes yet.
- **Slide 12, results**: the cash generated by factoring is used to **repay the short-term financing**, so the "need of working capital" block shrinks on the liabilities side and cash falls back. Both sides fall to a **NEW TOTAL** below the old total. Caption: "Cash generated by factoring replaces short-term financing solution." The balance sheet is smaller with the same equity, so **liquidity and leverage ratios improve**. This is the "3 in 1": sell receivables, get cash, repay short-term debt.

### Slides 13 and 14: From the remittance of invoices to disbursement (the waterfall)

- Bars from left to right: **remittance of invoices** (everything the client sends) → **guaranteed and purchased invoices** (only up to the **debtor's credit limit** set by the credit insurance line; the part above the limit is **not guaranteed** and not bought) → a **reserve** is deducted (**withheld until actual payment** of the invoice, to cover bonuses, credit notes, complaints) → the **fundable** amount → paid out only if the **amount already financed plus this fundable amount stays within the client's funding limit**.
- Slide 14 adds the cap: if the total financed would exceed the **client's maximum funding limit**, the factor pays only the part within the limit; the excess (marked with a cross) is excluded from financing.
- Two different limits to remember: a **per-debtor credit limit** (from credit insurance, caps what is guaranteed) and the **client's overall funding limit** (caps what is disbursed).

### Slides 15 to 17: Usual misconceptions

- **"Factoring is too expensive."** Answer: it is less expensive than internal debtor management: fewer personnel costs through outsourcing debtor management; liquidity lets you take **cash discounts for early payment** to suppliers; an improved rating gives stronger negotiating positions; proven faster payment by debtors; **no losses on receivables sold** to the factor.
- **"Only weak companies use factoring" (bad image transfer).** Answer: today it is seen as a professional financing solution with professional debtor management: outsourcing lets you focus on core competencies; no losses from unpaid receivables strengthens the company's image; reliable forecasts (receivables stop being an uncertain factor); the factor's brand as a service provider; higher collection success and no negative image transferred to the client.
- **"With factoring I lose control of my clients."** Answer: the client-debtor relation is improved and professionalised: professional debtor management; maximum transparency of all debtor transactions, accessible to the client anytime; efficient portfolio management with up-to-date information on debtors' creditworthiness; outsourcing dunning and collections takes pressure off the commercial relationship.

### What factoring is

- **EXAM: Discounting vs factoring.** Discounting is **one by one**: a cheque, bill of exchange or promissory note, discounted at a bank (you can discount one bill with one bank and another with a different bank). The bank never gives 100; it gives 90 or 95, hence "discount". **All discounting is with recourse**: if the debtor does not pay, the bank asks you for the money back, so **it is just a loan**. **Factoring is global**: you sell **all your receivables** to the bank.
- **EXAM: With recourse vs without recourse** (shown on Santander's page). With recourse: at maturity, if the debtor does not pay, the bank asks for its money back; it is a loan, "what banks have been doing for the last 70 years". **Non-recourse factoring**: a true **sale**; the receivables **disappear from your balance sheet**; Santander's text: you are insured against the debtor's insolvency, financed through advance payment of invoices, and the bank manages collection.
- What happens when you sell receivables: **cash goes up**, the **need for working capital goes down**, liquidity and **financial ratios improve**.
- Factoring is useful for **SMEs with good debtors**: "if your debtors are low quality, the bank will not offer you factoring because it's not interested". "Your debtors are better than you" is the whole logic; it works in Saudi Arabia, Dubai, China, Turkey or Spain.
- It is also used by **huge listed corporations**: every **quarter** they sell receivables to show more liquidity, less working capital and better ratios to investors and investment banks (Aramco, Apple, Microsoft, Telefónica, Repsol and utilities given as examples). Receivables on **government contracts** are very attractive to banks: "the administration pays late, but always pays".
- Coface's role: **Coface insures the banks that buy receivables from corporations**. "The most difficult time is always the **last week of every quarter**", when all the corporates sell. Factoring has historically been more popular in the **US and Latin America** than in Europe.
- Banks' main business is "to lend money without risk"; buying receivables on an AA-rated debtor is exactly that.
- Downside: factoring **ties you to one bank**, because that bank buys all your receivables and knows most of your business runs through it (normally a CFO works with 2 or 3 banks).
- **Fintechs** now offer factoring too: he deals weekly with fintechs running **non-recourse lines of USD 100 to 200 million with 20 to 25 employees**, serving large Spanish corporates (Telefónica, Repsol, utilities). Rule of thumb: fintechs target big corporates of their own economy; banks (as in the German banking case) can serve SMEs.
- Student remark on US firms selling tariff receivables: his answer was that any bank is eager to buy receivables whose obligor is the government, and that the motive is improving financial ratios.

## Traps and look-alikes

- **Factoring vs discounting**: factoring = the whole receivables book, can be with or without recourse; discounting = one bill, cheque or promissory note at a time, always with recourse, can be spread over several banks.
- **With recourse vs without recourse**: with recourse the seller repays unpaid invoices (a loan, receivables stay on the balance sheet); without recourse the factor takes the insolvency risk (a sale, receivables leave the balance sheet, the off-balance-sheet effect).
- **Factoring vs invoice discounting (deck sense)**: in the deck's "invoice discounting / in-house factoring" the factor finances and covers debtor risk but the **client keeps sales ledger management and dunning**; full factoring outsources collection; invoice discounting also finances **intermediate invoices** between monthly ledgers.
- **Factoring vs credit insurance**: slide 5 note: factoring is about **liquidity**, credit insurance is about **bad debt**; Coface sells both, and in class it **insures the banks** doing the factoring.
- **Debtor's credit limit vs client's funding limit**: the first caps what is guaranteed and purchased per debtor; the second caps what the factor will disburse to the client in total.
- **Reserve vs not guaranteed**: the reserve is a percentage withheld on guaranteed invoices until payment (for credit notes and deductions); not guaranteed is the part above the debtor's credit limit, which is not purchased at all.
- **97.7 million VEB vs USD 97.7 million**: the balance sheet is in bolívares; in dollars cash is about 70,000. "It can't be dollars."
- **Deck sales 472,944 vs case income 452,944 vs slide 19 "total sales" 331,189,769 VEB (USD 238,457)**: the slide 19 line is domestic finished-product sales only; the case total income includes 133.9 million of exports; slide 20's 472,944 (and COGS 355,722) are 20,000 above the case.
