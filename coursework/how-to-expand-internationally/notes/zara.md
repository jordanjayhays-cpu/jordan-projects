# What Business Is Zara In? (Revised) (Ivey W19157, Prof. Neira's graded case, discussed 5 Oct 2026)

This unit is the one case Prof. Pablo Neira graded in his strategy half of the course. The case (Daniel J. Doiron, Ivey, 2019) describes Inditex and its flagship brand Zara in early 2019: how Amancio Ortega built a vertically integrated, decentralised, store-driven model that reacts to customer demand instead of forecasting it, how that model beats the traditional Gap-style model on margin and inventory, and how new rivals (Uniqlo, Boohoo) are now copying or varying it. In class the professor used it to practise **VRIO** properly (step by step, not just naming a capability), to identify the industry's single biggest risk (the **fashion miss**) and how Zara mitigates it (react, do not predict), and to ask whether two fundamentally different business models can **coexist** in one industry (yes, case by case). The answer to the title question, which the professor stressed: Zara is not in the business of "fast fashion" as such; it is in the business of **fast, accurate response to what customers want, week by week and store by store** (CEO Pablo Isla: "not speed but accuracy"). Numbers from the case are heavily tested, so every statistic is listed below.

## Part 1: The case section by section

### Opening (page 1)

- The framing question: what would 2019 hold for **Industria de Diseño Textil, SA (Inditex)** and its flagship brand **Zara** with its "fast fashion" business model?
- It had taken **years** for old and new competitors to build approaches that could compete with Zara's model, while **online shopping** for fashion was rising.
- Footnote 2 of the case: most would agree "fast fashion" is **only a part** of Zara's core business model, not the entire foundation of its success. This is the clue to the title question.
- The early **counter-intuitive model**, which observers called "a formula for disaster": why invest in a fashion manufacturer and retailer that
  - produced its clothing in the **high-cost labour market of Spain** (versus Asia);
  - spent **very little on advertising**;
  - ostensibly **overspent** on high-end stores in chic retail districts across Europe;
  - carried **substantially less inventory** than competitors;
  - made clothes of **arguably lesser quality**;
  - and charged **15 per cent less** at the till?
- Inditex "took great pride in proving them wrong." By **2014** Zara was **number one fashion retailer in the world** by many measures, and still held that position in **2019**.
- The challenge now: many **upstart and copycat competitors** had entered, and it was "highly likely" they would enjoy a reasonable degree of success. Disruptive business models are inevitably copied.
- Examples of disruptive business models that changed their industries: **Southwest Airlines** (discount airlines), **Walmart** (discount department stores), and perhaps **Google's Waymo** self-driving ride sharing.
- Closing thought of the opening: perhaps it was time for Inditex to **reinvent the industry business model once again**.

### The early years

- **1963**: Amancio Ortega Gaona starts a small **dressmaking firm** in **La Coruña, Spain**, aged **16**. He went on to become the **fourth-richest person in the world** (the case footnote cites a Fortune article calling him the third-richest; the body says fourth).
- Over the next **12 years** he learned that textile manufacturing was **risky and frustrating when you are one step removed from your customers**, especially in the fast-changing **women's fashion** segment.
- **1975**: first Zara store opened in the centre of La Coruña, a town of **246,000 people**.
- Ortega's **overriding principle**: the key to success as a fashion retailer is to **link fashion design to manufacturing and distribution** so that the firm can respond rapidly to the finicky, changing needs of customers. The case calls this "the sole foundation" of Zara and still central to its success.
- Early success led to **nine new stores** in Spain's largest cities over the **next eight years**.
- Second lesson: use the **intelligence of employees throughout the company and trust their judgment**. A **top-down** decision model for product design and distribution would be counterproductive to quick reaction. So decisions on **product design, manufacturing and distribution** were put in the hands of employees across the company (**decentralised decision-making**).
- The combination of **fast manufacturing in response to customer needs** plus **decentralised decisions** let Zara thrive.
- In the **17 years** after 1975 Ortega opened **more than 1,000 new stores**, culminating in an **IPO on 23 May 2001**.
- IPO funds fuelled growth to **7,475 stores in 96 markets across eight brands**, fiscal **2017 revenue of EUR 25.3 billion**, and industry-leading **gross profit margin of 57.4 per cent** (Exhibit 1).
- **CAGR of 10 per cent over 2012 to 2017** (the text says 10 per cent; Exhibit 1 prints 11 per cent for net sales).
- Inditex was "by far the world's largest fashion manufacturing and retail business."

### The global fashion retail industry

- Worth **USD 2.5 trillion in 2017**, employing about **75 million people**. Expected growth **4 to 5 per cent** from 2018 to 2019.
- **Demographics and urbanisation**: **60 per cent** of the global population will live in cities by **2025**, up from **45 per cent in 2011**. Europe **82.2 per cent** urban; North America **88.6 per cent**.
- McKinsey: "By 2020, a quarter of global wealth will be concentrated in just **60 mega-cities**," some larger than countries; **16 of the 20 fastest growing cities** for fashion apparel are in **emerging markets**.
- Global population to grow to **8.2 billion by 2030** from **7 billion in 2014**.
- Growing middle class in **China and India**. **China expected to surpass the United States as the world's top fashion retail market in 2019.**
- East Asia's middle class expected to exceed **3 billion by 2030**, up from **1.5 billion**. Middle-class spending to rise from **USD 21 trillion** to **USD 51 trillion in 2030** (see Exhibit 2).
- **Global women's apparel market**: predicted growth **4.8 per cent a year from 2010 to 2025**, up from **3.3 per cent** in the six years before (Exhibit 3).
- Lu et al.: "Some **80 per cent of top growth cities** for total apparel sales by 2025 will be in **emerging markets**," so growth lags in developed countries. These cities add an extra **USD 100 billion** to world apparel markets. Emerging markets would then represent **55 per cent of mid-market women's apparel sales**.
- **Trends and challenges**:
  - **Responsible sourcing** and supply chain visibility topped the list. The **Tazreen garment factory fire in Bangladesh, November 2012** raised the need for fair trade, safe workplaces, living wages, social commitment and responsible resource use.
  - **Plus-size** categories led growth in developed countries: a **USD 17.5 billion** US market in the 12 months to **April 2014**, up **5 per cent**; **seven of 10 Americans** overweight; US plus-size opportunity estimated at **USD 46 billion**; UK plus-size market to grow at **7.1 per cent CAGR through 2022**.
  - **Digital adoption**: McKinsey (2017) predicted a **10 per cent CAGR for online apparel and footwear through 2020**. In the US, online was **27.4 per cent of apparel sales in 2017**, up from **20.7 per cent in 2015**; women's apparel trailing but expected to reach **24 per cent by 2021**.
  - **Transport costs** are linked to oil; with oil **near or below USD 60 per barrel since 2014**, transport costs were likely to keep falling.
  - **Technology in textile manufacturing**: mass customisation, small-batch manufacturing and time to market were becoming key **risk management** factors, driving a deeper focus across the supply chain.
  - **Fast fashion retailers** (Inditex, H&M, Forever 21, Primark) grew sales **8.2 per cent in aggregate in 2017** against an industry growth rate of **3.5 per cent**.
  - **New fabric innovation** was boosting growth, especially in **sportswear**.
  - **Commodity prices**: cotton was **24.5 per cent of the total fibre market in 2016/2017**, production **23 million metric tons**, price around **76 cents per pound**, likely to stay low given demand uncertainty, US trade wars and synthetics.
  - **Consumers over 50**, the "demographic earthquake" in developed countries: global spending by this group expected to reach **USD 15 trillion by 2020**, up from **USD 8 trillion in 2010**.

### Customer behaviours

- The case says shifting customer behaviour is perhaps the **most significant driver of change** in the industry; it can determine a firm's approach and even the industry business model.
- Zara's target customer (footnote 29): "**young, price conscious and highly sensitive to the latest fashion trends**." Zara plays in the **urban** retail fashion market.
- Two key behaviours:
- **1. Unpredictability and influence**
  - Customers are **fickle**, hard to predict and influence, swayed by **celebrity fashion, friends' choices, or the need to stand out** in a crowded city.
  - This creates the **single largest risk in retail apparel: the "fashion miss."** Misses lead to discounts and markdowns to make room for new stock.
  - Example: in its **Q1 2018** report **H&M** reported **USD 4.3 billion** of what Pam Danziger (Unity Marketing) called "unexciting, uninspired, unsold inventory," most of which would need discounting.
  - Studies: specialty apparel retailers mark down **30 to 40 per cent of inventory by up to 60 per cent** on average.
  - Traditional mitigation: spend heavily on **advertising** for brand awareness and loyalty. It "worked, to a degree." **Gap in 2017: USD 673 million on advertising**, up **12 per cent** year on year, **4.2 per cent of sales** or **10.6 per cent of gross profit**. Advertising "could be the defining driver of profitability" in this industry.
- **2. Frequently changing tastes**
  - Fashion cycles are notoriously short, especially in **urban womenswear**. Retailers must introduce new fashions often and avoid replenishing old items that may need discounting.
  - **H&M speed to sell-out: 12 to 38 days** in the first three quarters of 2018. Looks strong, but H&M tended to **replenish sell-out items rather than replace them with new ones**. Ideally **replenishment rates should be low**, reflecting a strong tie to customer needs. This affects **inventory turnover** and the ability to sell at or near **full price**.
  - Frequent new fashion brings customers back: an average customer visits a typical fashion store **four times a year**; Zara entices customers back **as many as 17 times a year**.
  - The ability to **react quickly** to shifting preferences "could be a defining success factor."

### Inditex and Zara: a unique approach

- Inditex is the public holding company for Zara and **seven other brands**, including **Bershka, Massimo Dutti, Pull&Bear and Stradivarius** (plus Oysho, Zara Home, Uterque in Exhibit 4).
- After **23 years** of diversifying into these brands, Inditex still depended on Zara for the vast majority of sales and profit. In 2017 Zara was **EUR 16.62 billion** of revenue, **2,251 stores in 96 markets**, and **70.1 per cent of EBIT** (footnote 35: Zara EUR 3.024 billion of Inditex's EUR 4.314 billion EBIT).
- Sales grew from **EUR 5.67 billion in 2004** to **EUR 23.34 billion in fiscal 2017** (this is the figure in the body; Exhibit 1 shows 25.3 for 2017 and 23.3 for 2016).
- Growth came from **focused geographic expansion**: **7,475 stores** by end 2017, plus **online sales in 47 markets**. Recent geographic focus was **Asia**: **593 stores in China** alone by end 2014.
- **Vertical integration**: unlike all competitors, Inditex **manufactured the majority of its own clothing**, **over 60 per cent** of its products, in high-cost labour markets (**Spain, Portugal or nearby countries**), relying less on third-world outsourcing. Competitors outsourced the vast majority of production.

### Unique capabilities

- **Time to market**: sketch to product ready for shipment in **10 to 15 days**. This is why Zara was dubbed champion of "fast fashion."
- **Pablo Isla** (CEO and chairman) rejected the label: "I don't identify with the concept of fast fashion. We are not about selling a million striped T-shirts as fast as possible." Success was "based not on speed but on **accuracy**, on understanding exactly what customers want, **week by week, and store by store**."
- The underlying capability: **identify customer needs and translate them back to the design teams in La Coruña**, achieved through employee groups called **"commercials"**, in **three levels**:
  - **Design team commercials**: typically **four people, two product managers and two designers**, responsible for one category (e.g. women's sport clothing). Full decision authority: **designs, ordering fabric, manufacturing quantities and pricing**. They decide what Zara makes and sells. About **12,000 new individual designs a year**.
  - **Regional commercials**: liaise with store managers (and customers) across a geographic footprint. Identify what will sell in their markets by **observing what people wear and want to wear** plus insights and orders from store managers.
  - **Store managers**: select the inventory they believe will sell, by **talking with and observing customers**. Must know store inventory and sales up to the minute. **Order new inventory in each category twice a week.** For many years Zara deliberately had **no store-wide inventory management system**, so managers **counted inventory by hand**, which forced them onto the floor and into contact with customers.
- Store managers do not always get what they ordered: regional commercials sometimes place **new items in stores "to see how they would sell,"** made in **small batches** to avoid big markdowns if unpopular. This drove Zara's **industry-leading fashion failure rates** (i.e. the lowest).
- Commercials introduce new items **weekly** and do not restock old ones: **replenishment rate only 2.8 per cent**.
- Two resulting customer behaviours:
  - customers visit **17 times a year versus 4.1 for brand-loyal shoppers** because there is always something new;
  - **scarcity**: when customers like something they buy now, because it will not be there next time.
- Result: **inventory turnover ratio of 28:48** (Q3 2018, trailing 12 months), industry leading.
- The three levels of commercials are "the heart of Zara's success."
- **Small-batch manufacturing**: a core capability. New items go to market fast **and** in small batches, so the market is tested with very little risk. This "mass customisation" was hard to achieve: heavy investment in **automation** in its plants, management of the whole supply chain to remove **bottlenecks**, a local **dye and finishing plant** near its factories in La Coruña, and most **pre-cutting** done in-house before delivery to **sewing subcontractors**.
- Cost implication: Zara's clothes cost about **20 per cent more to manufacture** than competitors' clothes made in bulk in third-world countries.
- **Distribution**: centrally managed from one large distribution centre in **La Coruña**. **All** Zara clothes, even those made in **Portugal, Morocco or China**, go to Spain for distribution.
- Bloomberg quote: **11 Zara-owned factories** send every item to the distribution centre by **automated underground monorail, 124 miles of track**; subcontractors across **Galicia**, some working for the company since **1975**.
- This enables shipping new inventory to every store **at least twice a week**; orders arrive **two days** after a store places them.
- Cost management through design: clothes designed to be **worn only a few times**, lowering material cost. Customers did not mind because they enjoyed changing fashions often.

### Marketing

- The foundation of Zara's marketing has always been **its stores**.
- Store openings: **0.62 net openings per day** over the five years to 2017 (footnote: 7,475 stores at end 2017 versus 6,340 in 2013, over 1,825 days).
- Store **location, design and layout** taken very seriously: a full-time team of **architects and visual-merchandising experts** at HQ designs every aspect, from decor and light-bulb colour to music and the exact positioning of clothing.
- **Window-front designs** are the pre-eminent form of advertising: a full team of window designers travels to international locations to understand each store's culture and customers, creates a window unique to the store, and ships props and details to be installed under strict guidelines.
- Each store is **completely revamped every four to five years**, with minor tweaks in between.
- Stores have a **high-end look and feel** with **relatively low inventory**, the opposite of competitors who fill stores with stock to maximise revenue per square metre. Zara's aim: a pleasant shopping experience that brings customers back.
- **2,238 Zara stores in 96 markets at end 2017**, driving **EUR 16.6 billion** in sales (note the body says 2,238 here and 2,251 on page 4 and in Exhibit 4). Stores are in the **high-end retail sectors of major urban centres**.
- New York: a **4,366 square metre** store in **SoHo** for an astonishing **USD 280 million** (the former home of **Old Navy**, a Gap brand); in **March 2016** a **2,800 square metre** store in the **World Trade Centre**.
- Advertising: only some new store openings and **twice-yearly sales events**. **0.3 per cent of revenue** on advertising versus an industry standard of **3.5 per cent**. **No advertising line item** in its financial reports because the amount is not material.
- Belle Kwan (Marketing magazine) quote: no glossy spreads, no red discount posters, no B-grade celebrities on billboards; a brand that "made it" without advertising, endorsement or mainstream marketing, with a loyal following across **78 countries**.
- **Pricing**: on average **15 per cent lower** than competitors. Ortega's goal since 1975: good quality fashion at affordable prices. But Zara does not look like a discount store.
- Derek Thompson (The Atlantic): Zara likes to "cozy up to the most famous brands in the world to sing their luxury ambitions even as they profit off a brilliant, cheap, short supply chain that delivers similar fashion at a much lower price." Zara places stores near **Prada and Gucci**, which try to stay as far from Zara as possible.

### Competing industry business model (the traditional model)

- Industry titans such as **Gap** launched most new clothing **twice a year**, spring and fall.
- Preceded by **up to nine months** of **centralised** planning, production and marketing.
- Sequence: new lines revealed on **runways**, vetted by elite designers and executives; **small runs** at third-world manufacturers; shipped to central facilities, vetted again, changed, finalised; then **large orders** at **ultra-low per-unit cost**; shipped to **regional warehouses** near outlets; point-of-sale strategy and store layouts designed centrally; inventory levels set centrally and shipped; then **advertising and selling in full force to push product**.
- Risk: **fashion misses**, traditionally **more than offset by ultra-low offshore manufacturing costs**.
- Industry-standard gross profit margin: about **38.3 per cent in 2017** (versus Inditex 57.4 per cent).
- Growth via **geographic expansion and brand expansion**: Gap from **1,640 stores in 2004** to a peak of **3,721 in 2015**, currently **3,594**. Gap brands: **Gap, Banana Republic, Old Navy, Athletica, Intermix** (Intermix: 30 boutiques in Canada and the US, acquired December 2012). Often several Gap-branded stores in one mall.

### Innovative new fashion competitors

- Zara's model inevitably spawned new competitors offering **variations of the model or totally new approaches**: **Uniqlo** and **Boohoo**.
- **Uniqlo** (Japan): labelled a **technology company, not a fashion company**; sole focus on innovation **around the products, not the fashion**. Kensuke Suwa (director of global marketing): "Between fashion and sports is a new area"; athletes' technically sophisticated uniforms could make clothes that "change clothing itself, instead of just following fashion trends."
  - **3,445 stores** at end of fiscal 2018; sales forecast **USD 18.2 billion** for fiscal 2017; in 2018 the **largest apparel chain in Asia**, **6.5 per cent of apparel sales in Japan**; aims to be number one in the world; near-term goal to expand beyond its **50 stores in the United States**.
- **Boohoo** (UK, online **"ultra-fast fashion"**, founded **2006**): **2018 sales GBP 579.8 million**, gross margin **52.8 per cent** (rivalling Zara); target **16 to 30 year olds**; concept to sale in **just two weeks**; **over 100 new products on its website daily**; growing in Europe and the US across brands **Boohoo, PrettyLittleThing and NastyGal**.

### Next steps

- The competitive landscape and the fast fashion world Zara invented were changing. Perhaps it was time to **reposition how Zara and the other Inditex brands competed**. "Zara had changed the retail fashion industry once. Could it do it again?"

## Part 2: What the professor added in class (session 9, 5 Oct 2026)

### Set-up

- Neira opened by saying the case had originally been scheduled before the session on how to enter a country, but moving it did not matter. Students had submitted written answers; the class discussed them.
- First case question: **perform a VRIO analysis** of Zara's resources and capabilities.
- **EXAM:** the first student answered by naming a capability ("less than two weeks from design to distribution, very low for the industry"). Neira: "fair enough, but **how do you go about doing the analysis?**" The question was never just to list resources; you must go through **valuable, rare, costly (difficult) to imitate, and organised** for each one. Expect exam questions that test the four steps and their outcomes, not just the capability names.
- Three groups each presented one capability. All three were accepted as valuable and rare; the discussion was about I and O.

### Group 1: automated distribution network

- Group 1 had chosen three capabilities: **small-batch manufacturing**, the **three-tier commercial system** and the **automated distribution network**. Neira asked them to present the last one.
- **Valuable: yes.** A highly logistical network lets Zara ship fresh inventory to every store **twice a week**; the **underground monorail of about 124 miles** moves product from factories to the distribution centre.
- **Rare: yes.** Traditional competitors store large offshore production runs in regional warehouses; Zara distributes everything from a **single central hub** in Spain. Nobody else in the industry does that.
- **Costly to imitate: yes.** It can be imitated, but at a huge cost (capital-intensive, built up since 1975).
- **Organised to capture value: yes.** Zara accepts high manufacturing costs, and the efficient distribution network lowers total cost; it also underpins the model that lets Zara spend only **0.3 per cent** on advertising.
- Verdict: all four yes, **sustained competitive advantage**.

### Group 2: decentralised three-tier commercial system

- Described bottom up: the **store manager** is in direct contact with customers, knows the trend and demand, **counts inventory by hand** so knows what is moving, **orders twice a week**; the **regional commercial** liaises with store managers and the design side; the **design team of four (two product managers, two designers)** handles design and pricing. Three layers.
- **Valuable: highly.** It delivers the accuracy that Isla describes.
- **Rare: yes.** Other brands (Boohoo was named) have the **speed** but not the **accuracy** or the exact system; no brand is known to have copied it.
- **Costly to imitate: yes, essentially not imitable.** It was **culturally introduced** by Ortega; a copier would have to "go back to the roots and spend the years they have built." (This matches the deck: costly to imitate comes from time, causal ambiguity and social complexity.)
- **Organised: yes.** Subcontractors who have worked with Zara for **decades**; everything flows through **one warehouse in Spain**; **11 factories**; a store order arrives **within two days**; **15 days** from sketch to delivery. The organisation exists to get the most out of the three layers.
- Verdict: sustained competitive advantage.

### Group 3: vertically integrated supply chain

- The student first offered two things (the supply chain and store-based marketing and positioning); Neira: "**just pick one**." They took the supply chain.
- **Valuable: yes.** A centralised, owned supply chain delivers product very quickly, even though it **raises production cost** because production is not offshore.
- **Rare: yes.** Zara is the only one with its own manufacturing in **Spain, Portugal and Morocco**; the other "fast fashion" competitors source offshore.
- **Costly to imitate: yes.** Historical (built from **1975** with Ortega) and **capital heavy** (tunnels, automation). Neira: "clearly valuable, clearly rare; costly to imitate, **not impossible, but it can be imitated**."
- **Organised:** the group's first answer was "everything is automated and centralised from one owner, all under their control." Neira pushed back twice.
  - **EXAM:** "Organised is not so much that they are organised. **Every company has to be organised.** The question is whether the company is **organised to get the most out of** the capability. You may have an amazing supply chain but only use it for one product line or during one period of the year."
  - "All under their control" is also **not unique**; other companies control everything.
  - The accepted answer: the organisation lets Zara **keep its brand promise** (always the newest trends, always fresh stock) by delivering **twice a week**; because the turnaround time is so short, the firm must be highly organised to run the system efficiently. So O = yes.
- Summary written in the study guide from this discussion: all three capabilities are organised through **one centralised automated system**, hence sustained advantage.

### Customer behaviour and the major industry risk

- Neira: in this industry customers have **huge power**; they are **hard to influence**; their tastes change often; they "taste often and are addicted." "Just think about yourself when you go about buying clothing."
- Case question: what is the major risk of an industry whose key feature is this customer behaviour?
- A student said **overproduction or oversupply**. Neira: oversupply is only a **consequence**. The real risk is the **fashion miss**: not hitting what the fashion will be.
- How the traditional companies (Gap and the like) work: they **try to anticipate**. Design teams meet in **September and October** to decide what will sell in **March**; they spend a lot on **marketing**, manufacture thinking it will sell, ship to stores, and at the end of the season discover oversupply.
- Personal anecdote: at **National Geographic** Neira saw the design team getting together in September and October to anticipate the next season.
- There is **no direct link** between how much you spend on marketing and the buying decision. You may influence customers, but in the store they decide **in two minutes** ("rather than buying this, I'm going to buy that").
- A fashion miss can create **tremendous losses** depending on the year for companies that anticipate, "contrary to Zara."

### How Zara mitigates the risk

- Zara **reacts rather than predicts**: small batches, twice-weekly orders from the stores, designs made after observing what sells.
- Because there is **always new stock**, customers keep coming back.
- Because Zara does **no marketing**, the only way to know what is on sale is to **go to the store**; this makes Zara customers visit many more times than, say, Gap customers (case: 17 versus about 4).
- Neira's broader point: innovation is not only technology or invention; **new ways of doing business** can be the innovation. That is how Zara disrupted this industry.

### Can the model be protected?

- Next case question: can this be patented? Neira: you **cannot patent ways of doing business** ("all the ways of doing jackets"); **patents do not work here**.
- Anecdote: a company once asked him whether he wanted to patent an agreement to "cover the whole space"; the point was that a rival can always do an **alternative pathway, a very similar robot**, and you cannot avoid it (detail unclear in the recording).
- Implication for VRIO: protection comes from **costly imitation** (time, culture, capital), not from legal rights.

### Can the two business models coexist?

- Not in the case, but Neira asked: can the traditional model (Gap) and Zara's model **coexist in one industry**?
- His answer: customers are segmented; there is a **grey area** where some customers buy one way at Gap and another way at Zara. **Fundamentally different business models can coexist** in an industry. Gap is trying to **set the trend** (the traditional, anticipating model); Zara follows what the customer wants.
- **EXAM:** "It goes **case by case**, you cannot generalise." You cannot say that whenever a disruptor enters, the incumbents die. Sometimes yes, in the majority of cases, but it is possible that both companies and both business models coexist.

### Second half of the session (not Zara)

- Neira then went on to the rest of the Session 9 deck (strategic alliances, joint ventures, FDI: representative office, branch, subsidiary). Points he emphasised there: BP's former CEO quote (thinking you can do everything better than anyone else is arrogant and foolish); alliances exist to obtain **complementary** resources and capabilities you lack; always put **duration and exit clauses** in a contract (e.g. two years, but fraud lets you resolve it with three months' notice; "today's ally may be tomorrow's rival"); FDI is the **most risky** and most expensive mode but gives the **most return** because there is no intermediary; a **representative office** is only a commercial office to market products and needs an establishment in the country; a **branch** is an extension of the parent, the **head office is legally responsible**, and a branch can only sell the same product or service as the parent. These are covered in the Session 9 notes; they matter for Zara only as the FDI link in Part 3.

## Part 3: How the case maps to the course frameworks

### Session 5: reasons to internationalise

- **Home market push, saturation**: the deck's example of saturation in consumer goods is **Zara in Spain**. Exhibit 1 proves it: stores in Spain fell from 1,930 (2012) to 1,688 (2017) while stores abroad rose from 4,079 to 5,787. A saturated market means demand is fully satisfied; the only ways to sell more are to substitute the product, take share or move to an unsaturated market.
- **Foreign market pull**: growth is in emerging-market cities (80 per cent of top growth cities, China to pass the US in 2019), so Inditex focused on **Asia (593 stores in China by 2014)**.
- **Competition and reaction to rivals**: the deck says **Zara entered India in 2010 and H&M followed in 2017** (follower behaviour). The class recording reversed the order; **trust the deck**.
- **Economies of scale and scope**: scale in sourcing, production and logistics (one distribution centre serving 7,475 stores); scope because the same distribution, design and store know-how is shared across **eight brands** (Exhibit 4). Inditex's brand portfolio is operational and strategic scope in action.
- **Product life cycle**: Zara's "worn a few times" clothes and 12,000 designs a year are the extreme opposite of extending a product's life abroad; the internationalisation extends the **business model's** life instead.

### Session 6: readiness, generic strategies, Ansoff and VRIO

- **Five forces, buyer power and vertical integration**: the deck lists **vertical integration** under bargaining power with the example that **Zara makes its own clothes unlike Gap and H&M**. Zara is not dependent on outside suppliers for over 60 per cent of production, so supplier power is low. Buyer power in fashion is high (fickle, price-sensitive, informed customers), which is why the fashion miss is the key risk.
- **Generic strategies**: Zara is a **mix of differentiation and cost**. Differentiation: fresh fashion every week, scarcity, high-end stores next to Prada and Gucci, no discount-store look. Cost: prices 15 per cent below competitors, advertising at 0.3 per cent versus 3.5 per cent, low inventory, few markdowns. The result is a gross margin of 57.4 per cent versus the industry's 38.3 per cent even though clothes cost 20 per cent more to make. It is closest to **differentiation across the whole (mass urban) market**, but exam options may describe it as a hybrid.
- **Ansoff**: Inditex's growth is mainly **market development** (existing product, new geographies: 96 markets, online in 47) plus **product development and brand extension** at the group level (Bershka, Massimo Dutti, Pull&Bear, Stradivarius, Oysho, Zara Home, Uterque). Gap's growth is the same two levers (geographic expansion plus multiple brands). Market development is the prime example of internationalisation.
- **RBV and VRIO** (Barney): tangible assets can be bought and give little advantage; intangible, heterogeneous, immobile resources give sustainable advantage. The outcomes: not valuable = disadvantage; valuable but not rare = parity; valuable and rare but imitable = temporary advantage; valuable, rare, costly to imitate but not organised = temporary advantage (unexploited); all four = sustained advantage. Costly to imitate comes from **time, causal ambiguity and social complexity (culture)**; organisation means systems, processes, structure and culture.
- Applied to Zara: the three capabilities the class chose (distribution network, three-tier commercial system, vertically integrated supply chain) all score yes on all four. The hardest to imitate is the **commercial system**, because it is cultural and took decades (social complexity, time). The distribution network and supply chain are imitable at huge cost (capital). Zara's organisation (decentralised decisions, twice-weekly cycle, one hub) is what captures the value.
- **Readiness question**: does the firm have the resources and the cash to go abroad? Inditex has **EUR 6.4 billion net cash** and 4.4 billion cash flow, so funding was never the constraint.

### Session 7: where Zara expanded

- The country selection funnel (PESTEL, then CAGE distance, then Porter and detailed market analysis, then the competitive advantage equation) explains Zara's sequence: first **Spain's largest cities** (nine stores in eight years), then **Europe** (chic districts, culturally and geographically close), then the **US** (SoHo, World Trade Centre, big economic market), then **Asia and China** (highest growth, 593 stores by 2014).
- **CAGE and Zara's model**: geographic distance matters a lot for Zara because **everything ships from La Coruña** and stores must be served twice a week; economic distance matters because garments are labour-cost sensitive, yet Zara chose a replicator approach (the same model everywhere) rather than arbitrage (cheap labour). Cultural distance is handled store by store by window-design teams who study local culture and by regional commercials who observe what locals wear.
- **Competitive advantage equation**: Zara's resources (speed, accuracy, stores) match the industry success factor (react to short fashion cycles) and the country specifics of dense, urban, middle-class markets, which is why the case stresses urbanisation (60 per cent urban by 2025, 60 mega-cities, Emerging 440).

### Session 9: entry mode

- Zara expands through **FDI with its own stores** (greenfield in most markets): the highest-commitment, highest-risk, highest-return mode, with no intermediary taking margin and full control of the store experience, which is Zara's marketing. The store-opening rate of 0.62 per day and the USD 280 million SoHo purchase are the scale of that commitment.
- Zara keeps the **vertical integration** choice at the production end too (own factories in Spain, Portugal, Morocco), whereas the deck's example of **subcontracting** as a contractual entry mode is **Gap** and fast fashion generally (and Boeing). So the contrast is: Gap and H&M outsource production and own stores; Zara owns most production and its stores.
- Within Zara's own model there is a **subcontracting layer** (sewing subcontractors in Galicia since 1975), but pre-cutting, dyeing, finishing and distribution stay in-house.
- Alliances and licensing play almost no role in Zara's story; the capabilities it needs are inside the firm, which is exactly the VRIO logic for why it does not need partners.

### The title question

- "What business is Zara in?" The answer the professor wanted: **not fast fashion** as a speed game (Isla rejects the label and footnote 2 says it is only part of the model), but **fast, accurate response to customer demand, week by week and store by store**. In course terms Zara's competitive advantage is a bundle of socially complex, organisationally embedded capabilities (commercials, small batches, central distribution, store as marketing) that together make its model costly to imitate, which is why it has stayed number one and why the threat now comes from rivals that vary the model (Boohoo online in two weeks, Uniqlo as technology) rather than copy it.

## Traps and look-alikes

- **Speed versus accuracy**: the capability is accuracy (knowing what sells, store by store); speed is the means. Isla explicitly rejects "fast fashion."
- **Fashion miss versus oversupply**: the risk is the miss; oversupply and markdowns are its consequences. Neira corrected a student on exactly this.
- **Replenishment versus replacement**: H&M replenishes items that sell out (high replenishment, looks good on sell-out days of 12 to 38); Zara replaces them with new items (replenishment rate 2.8 per cent). Low replenishment is good in this industry.
- **17 versus 4 (or 4.1)**: 17 is Zara's visits per customer per year; 4 is the typical fashion store; 4.1 is the figure for brand-loyal shoppers in the capabilities section.
- **0.3 versus 3.5 versus 4.2 per cent**: 0.3 is Zara's advertising as a share of revenue; 3.5 is the industry standard; 4.2 is Gap's advertising as a share of sales (10.6 per cent of gross profit).
- **57.4 versus 38.3 versus 52.8 per cent**: Inditex gross margin, industry standard (Gap-based) gross margin, Boohoo gross margin.
- **20 per cent more versus 15 per cent less**: Zara's clothes cost 20 per cent more to make but sell for 15 per cent less; the model still earns the higher margin because of low markdowns, low advertising and low inventory.
- **10 to 15 days versus two weeks versus two days versus twice a week**: 10 to 15 days is sketch to shipment (Zara); two weeks is Boohoo's concept to sale; two days is order to delivery for a Zara store; twice a week is the ordering and shipping frequency.
- **2,251 versus 2,238 versus 7,475**: Zara stores in Exhibit 4 and page 4; Zara stores in the marketing section; total Inditex stores. Both Zara figures appear in the case; Exhibit 4 is the official table.
- **25.3 versus 23.34 versus 16.62 billion euros**: Inditex 2017 net sales (Exhibit 1); the "fiscal 2017" sales figure in the body text (equals Exhibit 1's 2016 figure); Zara's 2017 sales.
- **11 versus 124 versus 593**: 11 Zara-owned factories; 124 miles of monorail; 593 Inditex stores in China by 2014.
- **Vertically integrated versus outsourced**: Zara makes over 60 per cent in-house in Spain, Portugal and nearby; Gap, H&M and the rest outsource the vast majority. In the Session 9 deck Gap illustrates subcontracting, not Zara.
- **Uniqlo versus Boohoo**: Uniqlo is a store-based Japanese technology-led apparel firm (products, not fashion); Boohoo is a UK online ultra-fast fashion retailer. Do not swap their numbers (3,445 stores belongs to Uniqlo; GBP 579.8 million and 52.8 per cent belong to Boohoo).
- **Costly to imitate versus impossible to imitate**: Neira's standard is costly, not impossible. The supply chain and distribution network can be copied at huge cost; the cultural commercial system is the closest to impossible.
- **Organised versus organised to capture value**: the O in VRIO asks whether the firm is set up to extract the most from the resource (Zara runs the whole network at a twice-weekly rhythm), not merely whether it is well run or "all under control."
- **Zara and H&M in India**: deck says Zara 2010 first, H&M 2017 follower; the Session 5 to 6 recording reversed it; trust the deck.
- **Fourth-richest versus third-richest**: the case body says Ortega became the fourth-richest person in the world; the cited Fortune headline says third. If asked, the case text says fourth.
- **Who said what**: Isla (CEO) said "not speed but accuracy"; Belle Kwan (Marketing magazine) wrote the "sans advertising" passage; Derek Thompson (The Atlantic) wrote the "cozy up to the most famous brands" line; Pam Danziger (Unity Marketing) called H&M's stock "unexciting, uninspired, unsold"; Kensuke Suwa is Uniqlo's director of global marketing.
