# Sessions 3 and 4: Managing Economic Risks, The Shifting Wealth of Nations (Prof. Mikel Aguirre, 14 and 16 Sep 2026)

This unit opens Aguirre's half of the course. It has two layers. The first is a practical one: the **International Chamber of Commerce (ICC) contract template** with its eight points, the distinction between a term of payment and a guarantee of payment, the choice of courts and legislation, and the **three transaction risks (transport, currency, credit)**. The second is a tour of the world economy in 2026, taught mostly by talking over Coface charts: the Strait of Hormuz and energy, stagflation and monetary tightening, the second China shock, "reciprocal" tariffs, the four named risks (emerging-market capital flows and original sin, commodity prices, debt, freight costs), Japan, strongman politics, the Asian century, patents and technological wars, unicorns, growth forecasts, tourism, and finally Mankiw's ten principles, PESTEL, GDP and the balance of payments. Aguirre works at **Coface**, a trade credit insurer present in more than 60 countries, has taught at IE since 1998, and his recurring message is that "the allies of today are the enemies of tomorrow", that memory is short, that forecasting is unreliable (The Economist missed COVID and Ukraine), and that the growth and the opportunities have moved to developing economies and Asia. He said his exam is multiple choice and that he "never does complicated questions"; he will not ask dates, and his later sessions (10, 11, 12, 14) are built on Harvard cases.

### Slide 3: International contract template (ICC)
- Source: https://iccwbo.org/ , the **International Chamber of Commerce**. The ICC created the **Incoterms** (FOB, CIF, EXW and so on) and governs the rules of the **letter of credit**. It advises (it cannot force) that every international transaction should have a contract with at least these **8 points**:
  1. **Parties involved**
  2. **Object**
  3. **Transport**
  4. **Terms of payment**
  5. **Guarantees of payment**
  6. **Courts**
  7. **Legislation**
  8. **Risks**
- Aguirre's detail on each point (from class):
  - **Parties involved**: not only seller and buyer. 95 percent of companies in any country are SMEs. A Spanish SME with 40 employees bidding for a rail tender in Bangalore has no subsidiary or branch in India and cannot build a joint venture in time, so it uses a **distributor** already working in India and specialised in rail. So distributors, agents and representatives are parties too.
  - **Object**: what is sold, goods or services (even defence products).
  - **Transport**: relevant if goods are sold. Who pays for it.
  - **Terms of payment**: buyers want to buy but lack liquidity, so you give **credit**: for example 10 percent in advance and the rest at 120 or 180 days. **Cuba: average payment term 360 days** because Cuba has no US dollars; Spanish hotel chains there were paid at 360 days and most closed this year.
  - **Guarantees of payment**: not the same as the term of payment. Analogy: the three-month deposit or a bank bond a landlord asks for on a flat. A guarantee is security that you will be paid; the term is how long you wait. Guarantees are used more and more in international business.
  - **Courts and legislation**: choose the **country of destination**, not the country of origin, because if there is a problem it will be there (a claim filed in Spain means nothing to a buyer in Bangalore, and the other way round). Lawyers study their own law, so your in-house lawyer knows Spanish law, not Indian law. A student's case: companies in West Africa with contracts naming **London courts** and arbitration. Aguirre: English law is very specific and English lawyers very expensive, so London arbitration only makes sense for large claims (GBP 2 million, not GBP 20,000, 50,000 or 80,000). African countries often use French or English courts. An LLC exists to limit shareholders' liability.
  - **Risks**: the ICC names **three**: **transport**, **currency**, **credit** (see "What the professor added").
- Rule of thumb: read what you sign. If you will not read it, "sign as Antonio Banderas", or ask an AI what your obligations are. IE's own contract allows expulsion with three Cs.

### Slide 17: Coface key takeaways (the Middle East war, 2026)
- **What we see**: a (fragile) ceasefire after **six weeks of conflict**; traffic in the **Strait of Hormuz close to zero since 1 March**, no resumption expected; sharp rise in commodity prices (energy and hydrocarbon derivatives); unprecedented release of **400 million barrels** of emergency oil reserves by IEA members; strong rise in tanker freight rates and insurance premia; extreme volatility in financial markets. Conclusion: a lingering "short-lived" conflict, quick resolution fading ("even if TACO"); Coface's central scenario increasingly unlikely.
- **What concerns Coface**: oil and gas (damage to facilities, shutdowns, depleted stocks, sea mines, so oil prices will take time to fall; gas inventories very weak in Asia and Europe); beyond oil and gas (fertiliser outflow disruptions threaten food security; petrochemical and sulphur shortages; aluminium prices rising). Supply-chain disruptions intensify as the shock trickles down manufacturing value chains; social unrest in emerging (lower income) and advanced (Europe) countries.
- **Alternative scenario, a long-lasting energy price shock (+50 percent)**: global GDP growth down about 0.5 point to **+2.1 percent** (Asia hit hardest, Europe down half a point to **+0.5 percent**, US down a quarter point to **+1.8 percent**); global inflation up about **1.5 points**, above target almost everywhere; monetary tightening where balance of payments imbalances are strong. Result: **stagflation** back on the agenda, negative for virtually all sectors, insolvencies rising much faster, especially in Asia.
- Bottom line on the slide: "an upstream shock which will cascade downstream into all manufacturing branches (and services)."

### Slide 30: Foreign currency corporate debt, back to the original sin

### Slide 36: "Reciprocal" tariffs are really based on trade imbalances
- White House "Liberation Day reciprocal tariffs" table: tariff "charged to the US" and the US "discounted" rate: **China 67 / 34, EU 39 / 20, Vietnam 90 / 46, Taiwan 64 / 32, Japan 46 / 24, India 52 / 26, South Korea 50 / 25, Thailand 72 / 36, Switzerland 61 / 31, Cambodia 97 / 49**; UK, Brazil, Australia, Turkey, Colombia, Saudi Arabia and others **10 / 10**. Highest: Lesotho 50, Cambodia 49, Laos 48, Madagascar 47, Vietnam 46.
- Reality: the "tariff charged to the US" is simply the **US bilateral trade deficit divided by that country's exports to the US** (10 percent if there is no deficit); the "discounted" rate is about half. Example for the EU (2024): deficit USD 235 billion / EU exports to the US USD 606 billion = **38.8 percent**, shown as 39, discounted to 20. Had it been done country by country, Spain would have faced just 10 percent (Spain, Netherlands, Malta, Belgium have no deficit with the US; Slovenia, Slovakia, Ireland, Austria over 80 percent).

### Slides 63 to 66: Ten Principles of Economics (Mankiw)
- **How people make decisions**: 1 People face trade-offs; 2 The cost of something is what you give up to get it; 3 Rational people think at the margin; 4 People respond to incentives.
- **How people interact**: 5 Trade can make everyone better off; 6 Markets are usually a good way to organise economic activity; 7 Governments can sometimes improve market outcomes (Adam Smith and the invisible hand).
- **How the economy as a whole works**: 8 A country's standard of living depends on its ability to produce goods and services; 9 Prices rise when the government prints too much money; 10 Society faces a short-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment.

### Slides 67 to 78: The principles one by one
- **1 Trade-offs**: guns versus butter; efficiency versus equity; "when the government tries to cut the economic pie into more equal slices, the pie gets smaller."
- **2 Opportunity cost**: whatever must be given up to obtain some item.
- **3 Marginal thinking**: marginal changes are small incremental adjustments to a plan of action; example, selling the last seats on an airline flight.
- **4 Incentives**: costs rising; example biodiesel; the role of the State.
- **5 Trade makes everyone better off**: trade allows countries to specialise in what they do best and enjoy a greater variety of goods and services.
- **6 Markets**: a market economy allocates resources through the decentralised decisions of many firms and households interacting in markets for goods and services.
- **7 Governments**: to promote efficiency and equity; the State should defend property rights and solve market failures.
- **8 Productivity**: the quantity of goods and services produced from each hour of a worker's time; to raise living standards, raise productivity.
- **9 Inflation**: the general rise in prices is caused by growth in the quantity of money; example **Germany 1921-22** (hyperinflation).
- **10 Inflation versus unemployment**: the short-run relation between the two; example, the measures taken in the international economic crises; **business cycle** = fluctuations in economic activity such as employment and production.
- Class on inflation: body temperature analogy (36.5 is healthy, 38 is flu, 34 is dead): **2 percent inflation is healthy** because it means consumption; **Turkey at 30-40 percent** means you are more expensive without adding anything. Spain has 17 car factories and no Spanish brand; Renault in Valladolid; if inflation is 2 percent in France and 4 percent in Spain, cars made in Spain are 2 percent dearer but not better. Nineteen (he said) countries share the euro, so prices and salaries are comparable.

### Macro rules of thumb from Session 4
- **Inflation**: 2 percent is healthy (consumption); Turkey's 30 to 40 percent is destructive; inflation squeezes corporate margins; higher-inflation countries become more expensive without being better.
- **Interest rates**: raising them is the best way to fight inflation, but it hurts borrowers and the poor; about 80 percent of global loans are variable rate (study guide); a half-point rise makes a working-capital loan dearer.
- **Trade balance and currency**: surplus means demand for your currency, so it appreciates, your government borrows cheaply and inflation is low. A weak currency makes exports cheap but imports (energy, travel) expensive.
- **The dollar puzzle**: the US has the biggest trade deficit yet a strong dollar because China invests massively in the US so that US firms keep manufacturing in China ("you have an Apple computer made in China and you don't care").
- **Original sin**: lenders give developing countries dollars or euros, never their own currency; when US rates rise everyone with dollar debt pays more. Russia's foreign-currency debt is very low, so sanctions bite less.
- **Who pays government debt**: citizens and corporates, through taxes. Exports are tax free everywhere (the importer pays) except Argentina, which taxes both.
- **Every crisis since 1945 was escaped through exports**; protectionism is the opposite and is now very popular.
- **Japan**: debt over 220 percent of GDP, owned domestically, rates near zero for 36 years, first rise in 35 years in 2025.
- **Strong leaders**: easy solutions to complicated problems; Erdogan and Putin; vicious circle.
- **Growth is moving away from Europe**: Asia-Pacific about 78 percent of growth; Europe below 1 percent; China 41 percent optimistic versus France 3 percent; rising middle class means more opportunities.
- **Patents and technology**: China files the most (WIPO 2023), Huawei the most of any company; "without Apple no Huawei, without Mercedes or Audi no BYD"; the Mecca-Medina train paid about 25 percent in foreign patents; productive (R&D, education) versus non-productive investment (real estate: 700,000 flats in Spain in 2007, 70,000 in 2008).

- **Nokia and Lehman**: leaders disappear fast; Lehman defaulted September 2008, Bear Stearns' funds were already in trouble in June 2007.

## Traps and look-alikes

- **Term of payment versus guarantee of payment**: the term is time (120, 180, 360 days); the guarantee is security (deposit, bank bond, letter of credit). Both are separate points of the ICC template.
- **Courts versus legislation**: two separate points; both should be the country of destination. London arbitration is a court choice, not a legislation choice, and is expensive.
- **ICC's three risks (transport, currency, credit) versus the deck's four risks (emerging-market capital flows, commodities, debt, freight)**: the first list is for a single transaction; the second is the global macro list. Political risk is a third category illustrated by Iberdrola and Naturgy.
- **Incoterms versus letters of credit**: both come from the ICC; Incoterms (FOB, CIF, EXW) allocate transport cost and risk; the letter of credit is a payment guarantee.
- **Malacca versus Hormuz**: Malacca carries more oil (23 percent of supply, 31 of seaborne trade); Hormuz is second (21 / 27) but has almost no bypass. Suez is the best known, not the biggest.
- **Share of total supply versus share of maritime trade**: the two bars on slide 18 are different denominators (Hormuz 21 versus 27).
- **East-West pipeline (Saudi, about 3 mb/d) versus Fujairah (UAE, about 0.5)**: together about 3.5, far below the 19 to 20 transiting.
- **Original sin versus currency risk in a contract**: original sin is sovereign and corporate borrowing in hard currency; currency risk in a deal is the exchange rate between invoice and payment.
- **Hard currencies versus the rest**: EUR, USD, JPY, GBP are hard; BRL, RUB, INR, CNY, SAR are not. A strong currency is good for a country in Aguirre's view (Japan, UK), bad for a borrower who took a yen loan.
- **Developed versus developing**: the dividing line is about USD 10,000 income per capita, not "rich versus poor"; China is a developing economy and one of the two biggest lenders.
- **Reciprocal tariff "charged" versus "discounted"**: the charged figure is deficit divided by exports (EU 39), the discounted is roughly half (EU 20); 10 percent is the floor for countries with no US deficit.
- **Who bears the tariff**: about 80 percent domestic; of that, two thirds margins and one third consumer prices (so far).
- **78.7 percent versus 62.4 percent**: 78.7 is the caption for Asia-Pacific 2016-2026; 62.4 is Asia ex Japan in the ten-year chart (plus Japan 1.0 and Oceania 0.9 is about 64).
- **Patents by country versus by company**: China leads countries (69,610), Huawei leads companies (6,494); Samsung is second among companies, the US second among countries.
- **Productive versus non-productive investment**: R&D and education (Korea, Emirates) versus real estate (Spain 2007).
- **Trade surplus and currency**: surplus leads to appreciation, cheaper borrowing and low inflation; the US is the exception (deficit, strong dollar) because of Chinese capital inflows.
- **Japan's 10-year yield move**: from 0.25 to 0.50 at the end of 2022 (yield cap), not a policy rate rise; the first policy rate rise in 35 years was in 2025.
- **GDP components versus balance of payments accounts**: C, I, G, NX versus current, capital, financial. Corporates: balance sheet and P&L; countries: balance of payments and GDP.
- **Mankiw's groups**: principles 1 to 4 are how people decide, 5 to 7 how people interact, 8 to 10 how the economy works. The invisible hand belongs with 6 and 7, not with 5.
- **PESTEL versus PESTLE**: same tool, letters reordered; Environment (weather, pollution, recycling) is not Economy (inflation, rates, costs).
- **Unicorn**: privately held and over USD 1 billion; a listed company or a USD 500 million startup is not one.
- **Time versus The Economist covers**: Time (Trump "Meltdown", 2016) illustrates developed-economy risk; The Economist Brazil trilogy (2009, 2013, 2016) illustrates overreaction to developing economies.
- **1979 versus 1981 versus 1990 versus 2003**: embassy seized 1979, hostages freed January 1981 after 444 days, Kuwait invaded 1990 (freed January 1991), Iraq invaded 2003. He will not ask dates, but the sequence of who was the ally may come up.
