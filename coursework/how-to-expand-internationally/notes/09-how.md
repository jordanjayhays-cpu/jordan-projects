# Session 9: How? Country entry alternatives (Prof. Pablo Neira)

This unit is the last step of Neira's internationalization sequence (global trends, benefits and reasons, ready?, where?, how?). It catalogues the ways a company can enter a chosen country, arranged on a ladder from lowest to highest **resource commitment, risk and return**: indirect export, direct export, contractual agreements and foreign direct investment (FDI). Each rung has sub-types with their own advantages and risks. What the professor stressed most in class: who "owns" the customer under each intermediary (agent versus distributor), doing real due diligence on any intermediary or partner, always writing duration and exit terms into contracts, that licensing and franchising can be combined (his own National Geographic experience), that alliances rest on complementarity and trust (60 percent fail), and that FDI is the most risky and most expensive route but also the one that keeps the whole margin, so it is a realistic option only after you know the market as an exporter. The deck was taught across 28 September (indirect and direct export, contractual agreements) and 5 October 2026 (alliances and FDI).

### Slide 4: 1. Indirect export, a) Importers
- **Indirect export**: the company sells to a buyer (**cross-border intermediaries or middlemen**) which in turn exports the product. These intermediaries normally have deep **expertise in a specific product or service and market**.
- **a) Importers** (also called **export management companies**): they work as the **export department** of the company.
  - Familiar with local conditions; help **find customers**, distribution channels, handle documentation, **clear goods through customs** and provide after-sales service.
  - **Marketing**: may take full or partial responsibility for **promotion of the goods**.
  - Can work **under the name of the company**, so customers are often unaware they are not dealing directly with the company.
  - May receive **exclusive rights**, either to sell in a specific market or for a specific product line.
  - A **low cost** option.

### Slide 5: 1. Indirect export, b) Agents and representatives
- An **agent** is an individual or firm you employ, usually **on commission**, to sell your product to wholesalers, retailers and sometimes end users in the target market.
- Agents **do not buy your products**.
- **Customers "are" of the exporter.** Agents may also handle products that compete with yours.
- A **representative** is a specialized agent who operates within a **specific geographic area and/or goods**.

| Advantages of agents | Disadvantages of agents |
|---|---|
| **Low entry cost** | **Low profitability** of the transactions |
| Requires low staffing | **High dependence** on the intermediary |
| Intermediary overcomes difficulties of entry | Inability to gain international experience |
| Gives **immediate presence** in the target market | |
| Gives you **control** over product/service | |

### Slide 6: 1. Indirect export, c) Distributors
- Distributors **buy your product** from you and then sell it on to the end users.
- They may **handle competing** products.
- Usually they will **want exclusive rights** for a specific territory.
- **Customers "are" of the distributor.**
- Can perform various tasks, not only sale: logistics, customs, distribution.
- Picture: the website of **URBAR**, a Spanish distributor showing the products it distributes (vibrating motors, pumps, hydraulic equipment). This is the professor's own company, used as the example of a distributor.

### Slide 7: 1. Indirect export, d) Trading companies
- Give **full service**: cover the sale process from purchasing, logistics and customs to warehousing and selling.
- They **buy from you on behalf of foreign clients**: **no "loyalty" to the exporter**; they seek the **best terms for their clients**.
- The largest and most extended are in Japan: the **Sogo Shoshas**.
  - Conglomerates that sell everything from metals to grains and textiles.
  - Represent **50% of Japan's exports** and **75% of imports**.

### Slide 10: Correct channel selection is key
- **Exercise due diligence** when selecting the intermediary:
  1. Be sure they **have** the marketing **knowledge**, industry expertise, **financial capacity** and facilities (showrooms, staff, warehousing) required to represent you properly.
  2. Find out if they **pay promptly**.
  3. If they represent products that **compete** head-to-head with yours, find out how they will resolve this potential conflict.
  4. **Always** check their **reputations and references**.

### Slide 11: Set goals for the international trade
1. **Volume**, market share, profit margin requirements.
2. **Financial** and personnel commitments to developing the trade.
   - Capital requirements: the **cash flow associated** (for example providing initial inventories).
   - Cost: **investment** on developing the channel and on maintaining it.
3. **Degree of control**, terms of sale and channel ownership.
4. Coverage: **geography** and market segments.
5. **Duration** / continuity with the intermediaries.

### Slide 17: Contractual agreements, definition and types
- **Long term associations** between a company and another company (**or a government**) in a foreign market.
- Involve the transfer of **knowledge** (technology, process, trademarks and/or human skills), **construction** or **manufacturing** capabilities.
- **Five types**:
  1. Turn-key projects
  2. Subcontracting
  3. Licensing
  4. Franchising
  5. Strategic alliances (JV)

### Slide 18: 1.A Turn-key operations

- Pictures: a motorway under construction and a hydroelectric dam.

- **Subcontracting**: subcontracting a **local manufacturer to produce your goods**.
- Picture: a GAP hoodie (GAP designs, local manufacturers produce).

### Slide 22: 3. Licensing
- A **license is the grant of certain rights** to another company: **patents, trademarks, brand names**, rights to use technology, etc.
- Allows the other business to use your **proprietary technology / intellectual property** for specific purposes in a **specific country or region**.
- Examples of what can be licensed: patents, domain names, industrial design, confidential information, inventions, database rights, works of authorship, service marks, logos, trademarks, design rights, business or trade names, commercial secrets, computer software.
- **Proprietary technology**: all proprietary processes, formulae, inventions, trade secrets, know-how and other proprietary rights used in the development, license, sale, marketing, distribution or maintenance of a product.
- **Intellectual property**: the series of rights that creators and other owners (artists, producers, radio broadcasting organisations, etc.) have over the works and benefits that stem from their creation. Includes literary, scientific and artistic creations. Unlike industrial property, intellectual property **comes into being at the same time as the creation and does not have to be registered**; this is known as **"copyright"**.

### Slide 28: 4. Franchising
- A **form of licensing** in which:
  - The **franchiser** provides a standard package of **products, systems and management services**.
  - The **franchisee** provides **market knowledge, capital and personal involvement in management**, in accordance with the franchising agreement.
- Provides a blending of **skill centralization** (at the franchiser) and **operational decentralization** (at the franchisee).
- Logos: McDonald's, Pizza Hut, Burger King, KFC, Domino's, Arby's, A&W, Wendy's, Es Teler 77.

### Slide 33: Strategic international alliances, definition
- A business relationship established by two or more companies to **cooperate out of mutual need** and to **share/reduce risk** in achieving a common objective, towards achieving competitive strength.
- Allows **expanding your business** in a current or new **market** or gaining **access to resources** that would be difficult to acquire by yourself.
- **Complementarity of partners is crucial.**

### Slides 34 and 35: 3 key elements that distinguish alliances from other interfirm relationships
1. Partner companies **worry about themselves and their partners** because success of the alliance depends on **collaborative efforts**.
2. The relationship involves the **exchange of knowledge** associated with technologies, skills or products.
3. **Trust** and cooperation play a key role in the management of the alliance:
   - a) When knowledge is exchanged there is a risk that it could be **used opportunistically** by one of the partners.
   - b) Reality of strategic alliances: a **contract cannot predict all the potential** competitive and organizational **risks** an alliance creates.
   - c) As a result, firms going into an alliance must be prepared to deal with risk, which is why trust is key.

### Slide 36: Unavoidable issues with alliances
- Alliances involve **uncertainty and ambiguity**: this implies significant coordination **costs**.
- The partner **relationship will evolve** in ways hard to predict. Alliances are formed for strategic reasons and as strategies and competitive environments evolve, so do alliances.
- The partners will eventually go their separate ways: **alliances eventually will end**, so firms must be **realistic** about alliance **longevity** and **termination terms**.
- **"Today's ally may be tomorrow's rival."**

### Slide 37: SIA formation process (steps 1 to 3)
1. **Do I need a SIA?** Based on your strategy, do I require special expertise or market presence where an alliance would make sense?
2. Define **form and objectives** of the SIA: how should it work to achieve the goals.
3. **Partner selection**:
   - **Strategic fit**: **complementarity** of partners is key.
   - **Organizational fit**: you **build relationships between people**, not just a relationship between your organization and a company. Look for:
     - **Good chemistry** between key managers
     - **Similar** corporate **cultures** and values
     - Partners that value **public reputation**
     - **Compatible** control and decision-making **systems**
     - Common **time horizon**

### Slides 38 and 39: SIA formation process, 4. Contract negotiation: key issues
1. Are there clearly understood **agreed-upon objectives**? Each party must understand the other side's reasons for the alliance.
2. What will be **each partner's contribution** and rewards; penalties if poor performance; arbitration if necessary?
3. How will the alliance be **integrated** with the parent firm's strategy?
4. Will there be **cultural compatibility** and organizational fit between the partners?
5. Does the alliance **leverage the complementary strengths** of the partners?
6. What and when will be the **exit strategy**? Alliances eventually will end; be realistic about alliance **longevity**.
7. What will be the **monitoring process**?
- But alliances are difficult to manage: **60% failure**.
- Main reason: **poor selection of alliance partner and lack of trust**.

### Slide 47: Foreign direct investments
- An internationalization strategy in which the firm establishes a **physical presence abroad**.
- Represents **greater resource commitment** and entails **substantial risk** and uncertainty.
- **Advantages**: access foreign markets more effectively; serve customers better; can join a global or regional supply chain; secure sources of supply; gain access to new technology.
- Box: a **realistic option after** you have established yourself as an **exporter** and have become **familiar with your foreign market**.

### Slide 48: FDI types: representative office and branch
- Three chevrons in sequence: **Representative office** -> **Branch (sales office)** -> **Subsidiary**. The side list reads: representative office, sales office, subsidiary fully owned.
- **Representative office**:
  - An office opened in a foreign market as a **first step** for establishing a relationship with potential customers or suppliers.
  - Used **only and exclusively in marketing and promotional** activities.
  - **Cannot** conduct production activities or **buy or sell directly**; it acts only on behalf of the company.
- **Branch (sales office)**:
  - An **extension of the parent company** in another country.
  - Carries out the **same activities** performed by the **head office**.
  - Does **not have a separate legal entity** from the parent company.
  - **Can** perform all of the activities of the parent company, **including buying or selling directly**.

### Slide 49: Subsidiary, and branch versus subsidiary
- **Subsidiary**: a company owned by a firm abroad but which **operates as a local company with respect to regulations, laws and taxes**.

| | Branch | Subsidiary |
|---|---|---|
| **Legal** | Not a separate legal entity from the parent company | Has its own legal entity |
| **Taxation** | Double taxation agreements | In the foreign country where it is formed |
| **Activities** | Same as the parent company | Can be different |
| **Ownership** | 100% by the parent company | > 50% to 100% |
| **Liabilities** | Extend to the parent company | Limited to the subsidiary |

### Slide 50: Two ways to create a subsidiary
- The subsidiary chevron branches into **Green field** and **Cross border mergers and acquisitions (M&A)**.

### Slide 51: Greenfield versus M&A
- **Greenfield**: expanding operations in a foreign market starting **from zero**; requires obtaining everything locally, from assets to employees.

| Greenfield advantages | Greenfield disadvantages |
|---|---|
| No risk of losing technology to a competitor | **Lengthy** process from scratch |
| Tight control of operations | Faces competition **before** it is set up |
| Well perceived: creates jobs in the local market | Time researching beforehand |

- **Merger**: **two businesses are combined into one** firm.
- **Acquisition**: a firm purchases a **% of the target company's stock**, normally to **achieve control** of the local firm.

| M&A advantages | M&A disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **Faster** start: ability to expand rapidly, immediate access to market | Reaction of directors and employees |
| Shorter payback time: investor gets income from day 1 | Risks of the acquired firm passed to the acquiring firm |
| Learn from the acquired company and benefit from its assets | |

## What the professor added in class

### Framing and the ladder (28 Sep)
- The sequence of the course: global trends, benefits and reasons, ready?, where?, and finally how? Once you have reasons, are ready and know where, "how do I enter into that country? There are so many ways."
- The list is not exhaustive; which option fits "goes case by case depending on the country" and the product.
- Up the ladder (indirect export) you need little funding and take little risk, but benefits are lower. Down the ladder (FDI) you need a lot of funding and take more risk, but the benefits can be much higher.
- A student asked whether online sales without physical presence count: yes, that is direct export via the internet (the Netflix discussion: subscribing from Spain to a service that has no local operations).
- Student clarification accepted by the professor: indirect export is sending to the market the same product you sell domestically; direct export involves customizing it a little and handling the sale yourself.

### Importers
- The importer says "I will do everything: marketing, handling of the goods, invoicing, the whole process, as much as you tell me to do." The potential customer, for example in Nigeria, will not detect that there is someone in the middle.
- Very low cost because they work largely on the margin of the goods.
- All middlemen ask for **exclusivity**. Reason: they do not want to develop a market for you for six months and then find you gave the same mandate to someone else. "If I do this effort for you, give me exclusive rights for a period of time."

### Agents and representatives
- An agent can be a single person. The agent tells customers "I work for this company, it has these products, I can put you in contact." Once the contact is made, **the company deals directly with the customer**. The agent finds customers and connects you; the agent cannot close on your behalf without telling you.
- Paid on commission; sometimes a small fixed or retainer fee plus commission. If nothing sells, the agent earns nothing.
- Because you must come in with attractive prices to win those customers, the margins are low: that is the **low profitability** disadvantage.
- Immediate presence: with the right agent, "in one month you will already be talking to potential customers."
- The agent is **not responsible for execution**: no logistics, no distribution. You may still need an importer or distributor alongside the agent, which can conflict with exclusivity someone else has asked for.
- Agents also ask for exclusivity, often renewed on an annual basis.
- Dependence is total: if you chose the wrong agent you discover it after eight months of weak leads, and you are locked in by the exclusivity you granted.

### Distributors
- The difference with the agent: the distributor **buys the product** and sets the prices. "I sell it to you for this; I do not know if you are selling it for 2 euros or 10 euros." Customers are the distributor's.
- Personal experience: his company (URBAR) was distributor for several manufacturers, including a Swedish pump maker, with agreements lasting years. The manufacturer had no idea who the end customers were, what discounts or marketing were used; it only saw orders from the distributor.
- Exclusivity works both ways: if a local customer contacted the manufacturer directly, the manufacturer was obliged to send them back to the distributor.

### Trading companies
- Sogo shoshas: 50% of Japan's exports and 75% of imports. Mitsubishi was valued at about 42 billion dollars (chart from six years ago). "You want to sell something into Japan? Go through one of those trading companies."

### Due diligence and contract terms for intermediaries
- They all arrive saying "I will be your man" with wonderful presentations. You must verify: real knowledge of the market and customers; for distributors, **financial solvency** so they pay rapidly and on time; whether they already carry **competing products**; and always **check references**. Asking for references is itself a test: the reaction of someone with no real history gives them away.
- Student question on minimum sales quotas: "Everything is open." Distributors typically ask for exclusivity for three years and say "give us at least one year, or eighteen months, to develop the business." You can set minimum sales, commission percentages, whatever you negotiate.
- Student question: is it better to pick a distributor already selling similar products, or one with country experience but no similar product? His preference was distributors already in the sector with **complementary** catalogues (not competing ones): a competing line turns the relationship into a question of price, and you cannot position as premium.
- Goals to put in writing: volume, number of units; for agents, a required number of **visits per week or month** plus **monthly reports** listing the customers visited with names and contacts, so you can check the visits are real.
- **Duration and exit**: always set a duration and how the contract can be terminated. A notice period of **60 or 90 days** is reasonable, not ten days. Define how termination works to avoid legal claims later.

### Direct export
- Internet: companies received foreign orders without ever planning to export, then adapted websites and catalogues.
- Branded manufacturers: Lee and Wrangler jeans sold outside the US are made and sold by a **local manufacturer** that receives the designs and the fabrics to use, and also takes care of selling, under the Lee or Wrangler brand.
- Export department: for complex, high-value assets (he mentioned windmills and large infrastructure) an agent or a B2B intermediary cannot handle the complex sales process; the manufacturer itself must meet the potential customers. "There are occasions where you just have to be yourself."

### Contractual agreements in general
- Each side puts capabilities on the table so that combining them gives a good outcome. The transfer of knowledge must be handled carefully so it is not used against you: a partner that sold his products later began producing a similar product under another brand name.

### Turn-key and concessions
- Turn-key example from his company: water treatment plants for municipalities in **Vietnam**. They signed with the municipality, built the plant in containers, assembled it on site and handed it over.
- Concessions: his company holds concessions to **collect waste in several municipalities outside Spain**. The municipality says "I will give you ten years of the concession to treat the waste of my municipality; it will not cost me anything." You organize everything from collection to treatment, run the numbers, make the expensive investment and recover it over the years of the concession. It made sense to seek concessions where they already had a plant.

### Subcontracting
- A non-equity option: no new company, no buying or selling of shares.
- Companies realise more and more that they cannot do everything. Fast fashion companies design (colours, fabrics) and subcontract manufacturing. Car makers are really assembly companies buying from multiple suppliers; airplanes likewise (the Boeing map).

### Licensing
- You license something unique to you: brand names, proprietary technology, intellectual property, domain names, industrial designs, logos, database rights.
- Coca-Cola: the company sends the concentrate and in each country different bottling companies produce under licence (the Olayan slide).
- His own case: a licensing agreement with **National Geographic** (Washington) to produce books, clothing and other products with the National Geographic logo, brand name and colours.
- A student asked whether such merchandising hurts an established brand. Answer: case by case. In their case it **did not work**: the Spanish company was not able to make money, some designs were nice but the **quality was not good**, and National Geographic eventually wanted to **recover the licence** because it was to some extent hurting the brand. National Geographic later ran product corners itself in different countries. "We were the wrong partners, I have to admit."
- Payment problems from experience: the licence carried a **minimum payment**; they overspent on marketing and commercial activity while sales did not follow, so an unpaid 500,000 became one, two, three million.
- Licensing is also a good solution where **local regulation restricts foreign companies** from entering directly.

### Co-marketing and co-branding
- Co-marketing: **GoPro and Red Bull**; **Starbucks and Spotify** (codes giving free or discounted access for a period, and vice versa). Two different products advertised together; the products stay separate.
- Co-branding: a new product combining two brands. Student example accepted: **Meta and Ray-Ban** glasses. Others: **Milka with Oreo**, **Philadelphia with Milka**, **McFlurry** with M&Ms or Kit Kat.
- Student question on who keeps the profit: it depends; often a much larger company knocks on the door of a smaller one and proposes a split such as 70 or 80 percent for itself.

### Franchising
- A student recounted the **McDonald's in India** case from another class: no beef, a complicated market, they needed a local joint venture partner, found one, split the country into two regions, and the relationship with one partner degraded into a public controversy with accusations that the partner was taking money from McDonald's. The professor used it as another illustration of why you check references and reputation before entering.
- Franchising works because it combines skill centralization with **operational decentralization**: somebody local brings the knowledge of the market and the people. It is a fast way to get into a country, with risks on adapting to local culture.
- His company was on **both sides**: the licensing contract with National Geographic Washington gave it the right to open **franchise agreements with local stores** in whichever country it wanted. Products were sold through shops the company owned and through franchised shops in different countries.

### Strategic alliances (5 Oct)
- The BP quote is the basis for alliances: any organization that thinks it does everything best is arrogant and foolish; someone else may know more about something.
- Link to the readiness session: when you analyse resources and capabilities, "even if you have them, do you have enough of them?" If not, an alliance is a way to acquire or obtain them. Complementarity with the partner is crucial; the idea is to reduce risk and go hand in hand towards a mutual goal.
- Uncertainty and ambiguity: you are dealing with another party you do not fully know; the relationship evolves.
- **Always put two things in contracts: the duration and the ways to end it.** Example: "I want to work with you for two years, but the contract can be resolved with three months' notice." (Same rule he gave for agents and distributors.)
- "Today's ally may be tomorrow's rival" comes from personal experience.
- Non-equity alliances: no exchange of equity. As a first step, companies normally start with a non-equity alliance. "This is what we did" (General Kinematics with URBAR).

### FDI (5 Oct, recording ends part way through)
- Advantage of FDI: speed of action once you know the country. But his advice: rather than going straight to FDI, start with an agent or a distributor to get a flavour of how your products do in the country. Sometimes that is impossible (for some products or services only FDI works), but normally try a lighter alternative first.
- FDI is **the most risky alternative and the one that takes the most money**, and also **the one that gives you the most money**, because there is no intermediary: the whole margin is yours.
- Representative office: nothing more than a commercial office to market your products, one or two people. It does not have to be a physical shop, but you must legally create an establishment in the country; in most countries this is very easy to set up.
- Branch: just an extension of the current company. Whatever the branch does in the new country, not only the branch but the **head office is directly legally responsible**. A branch can only sell exactly the same product or service as the parent company. (The recording cuts off here; the subsidiary, greenfield and M&A slides were not captured on the transcript.)

## Likely exam angles
- Know that the four entry levels in order of rising resource commitment, risk and return are indirect export, direct export, contractual agreements, FDI.
- Know that the criteria for choosing an entry mode are resource commitment (and barriers), risk, return/benefits, and time frame or speed required.
- Know that indirect export means selling to a cross-border intermediary (middleman) that exports for you; the four types are importer (export management company), agent/representative, distributor and trading company.
- Know that an agent works on commission, does not buy the goods, and the customers belong to the exporter; a distributor buys the goods, usually wants territorial exclusivity, and the customers belong to the distributor; a representative is a specialized agent limited to a specific geographic area and/or goods.
- Know that agent advantages are low entry cost, low staffing, immediate presence, intermediary overcomes entry difficulties, control over the product; disadvantages are low profitability, high dependence, no international experience gained.
- Know that trading companies give full service but buy on behalf of foreign clients, so they have no loyalty to the exporter; Japan's sogo shoshas handle 50% of exports and 75% of imports; Mitsubishi was the largest at 42.5 billion dollars in 2019.
- Know the four due diligence checks on an intermediary (knowledge, expertise, financial capacity and facilities; prompt payment; handling of competing products; reputation and references) and the five goal areas for the trade (volume/share/margin; financial and personnel commitments; degree of control, terms of sale and channel ownership; coverage; duration/continuity).
- Know that direct export includes internet, wholesaler (not a sale to the final customer), branded manufacturers (Lee and Wrangler) and an export department for B2B high-technology or high-value products with complex sales.
- Know that contractual agreements are long-term, usually non-equity, associations involving transfer of knowledge, construction or manufacturing capabilities; the five types are turn-key projects, subcontracting, licensing, franchising and strategic alliances (JV).
- Know that a turn-key project is the complete construction of a plant or infrastructure abroad, and a concession is a right granted (for example by a municipality) to run a service like waste collection.
- Know that licensing grants rights (patents, trademarks, brand names, technology) for specific purposes in a specific country or region; it needs little capital, works where import restrictions block other entry modes, and is key where foreign ownership is sensitive; risks are wrong partner, quality problems, payment problems, loss of marketing control.
- Know that co-marketing is joint promotion of existing products (Starbucks and Spotify, Red Bull and GoPro) while co-branding creates a new combined product (Apple Watch Nike+, McFlurry Kit Kat, Meta Ray-Ban).
- Know that franchising is a form of licensing; the franchiser provides products, systems and management services, the franchisee provides market knowledge, capital and personal involvement; it is the fastest expansion method of all, needs low investment and enjoys friendly regulation; risks are local taste adaptation and control.
- Know the National Geographic chain: the Society in Washington licenses the Spanish company, which franchises store operators; licence terms were worldwide brand rights with prior approval, an annual fee and a percentage of sales.
- Know that alliances are distinguished by three elements (mutual dependence on collaborative effort, exchange of knowledge, trust because contracts cannot foresee all risks), that complementarity is crucial, and that about 60% fail, mainly because of poor partner selection and lack of trust.
- Know the SIA formation steps: do I need one, define form and objectives, partner selection (strategic fit = complementarity; organizational fit = chemistry, similar cultures, reputation, compatible systems, common time horizon), contract negotiation (seven questions including exit strategy and monitoring).
- Know that an equity JV creates a separate jointly owned company (the classic alliance), while a non-equity alliance involves no equity and no new company, is limited in scope or duration, and is easier to form and dissolve.
- Know that FDI means a physical presence abroad, is a realistic option after establishing yourself as an exporter, and its forms are representative office, branch (sales office) and subsidiary.
- Know that a representative office does marketing and promotion only and cannot buy, sell or produce; a branch can do everything the parent does including buying and selling, but is not a separate legal entity. Know the five branch versus subsidiary rows: legal entity, taxation (double taxation agreements versus taxed where formed), activities (same versus can differ), ownership (100% versus more than 50% to 100%), liabilities (extend to parent versus limited to the subsidiary).
- Know that greenfield starts from zero (no technology leakage, tight control, creates local jobs, but lengthy, faces competition before set-up, needs research), while M&A is faster with income from day one and learning from the acquired firm, but brings employee and director reactions and inherits the acquired firm's risks; a merger combines two businesses into one firm and an acquisition buys a percentage of the target's stock, normally to gain control.

## Traps and look-alikes
- **Agent vs distributor**: the agent never owns the goods and the customers are the exporter's; the distributor buys the goods, sets resale prices and the customers are the distributor's. Both may carry competing lines and both want exclusivity.
- **Agent vs representative**: a representative is a type of agent, restricted to a specific territory and/or product range.
- **Importer (export management company) vs trading company**: the importer acts as your export department and can work under your name, loyal to you; the trading company buys on behalf of its foreign clients and has no loyalty to the exporter.
- **Indirect vs direct export**: indirect means an intermediary exports for you; direct means you sell across the border yourself (internet, wholesaler, branded manufacturer, own export department). Selling to a wholesaler is direct export even though it is not a sale to the final customer.
- **Branded manufacturers vs subcontracting**: in direct export, a branded manufacturer makes and sells via a foreign retail chain under your brand (Lee, Wrangler); in subcontracting (a contractual agreement), a local manufacturer produces your goods for you (GAP, Boeing suppliers).
- **Turn-key vs concession**: turn-key builds a plant or infrastructure and hands it over; a concession grants the right to operate a service for a period (waste collection for ten years).
- **Licensing vs franchising**: franchising is a form of licensing but adds a full package of products, systems and management services from the franchiser, with the franchisee contributing capital and management; franchising is the fastest expansion mode.
- **Co-marketing vs co-branding**: co-marketing is joint promotion of two existing separate products; co-branding creates a new product that combines both brands.
- **Proprietary technology vs intellectual property**: proprietary technology covers processes, formulae, trade secrets and know-how; intellectual property is the creator's rights over works (copyright), which arise at creation without registration, unlike industrial property.
- **Equity JV vs non-equity alliance**: the JV creates a new jointly owned company; a non-equity alliance creates no company and exchanges no equity, so it is easier to form and dissolve.
- **Strategic fit vs organizational fit**: strategic fit is complementarity of what the partners bring; organizational fit is about people, culture, chemistry, decision systems and time horizon.
- **Representative office vs branch**: the representative office can only market and promote, never buy or sell; the branch can buy and sell and do everything the parent does. Neither is a separate legal entity.
- **Branch vs subsidiary**: branch = same legal entity as parent, same activities, 100% owned, liabilities reach the parent, double taxation treaties; subsidiary = own legal entity, activities can differ, more than 50% to 100% owned, liability limited, taxed where formed.
- **Greenfield vs M&A**: greenfield is slow but keeps technology and control and is welcomed locally; M&A is fast with immediate income but inherits the target's risks and people problems.
- **Merger vs acquisition**: a merger combines two firms into one; an acquisition buys a stake (percentage of stock) to achieve control.
- **Three criteria vs four**: the ladder slides list resource commitment, risk and return; the final "which entry strategy to choose" slide adds a fourth, time frame or speed required.
- **60% figure**: it is the alliance failure rate, not the sogo shosha share (50% of Japan's exports, 75% of imports).
