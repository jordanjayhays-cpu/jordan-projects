### S&P ratings and investment grade (State Bank of India recap)
- Opened with the **State Bank of India** from the previous session. A bank has **four ratings**: local currency long term, local currency short term, foreign currency long term, foreign currency short term.
- Rule of thumb: when you tell a partner your bank's rating, you give **one** number, the **local currency, long-term** rating. Do not get into short term, long term, local, foreign; "it's going to get lost."
- **State Bank of India is BBB.** Is that good or bad? "Fine, average, not great, not bad." Is it investment grade? **Yes**, because it is above BBB-.
- **EXAM:** **Everything BBB- and above is investment grade. Below BBB- (BB, B, B-, and so on) is speculative grade.** "If someone asks you, I want an investment-grade bank on your side, it has to be triple B minus or above."
- Why S&P: it is "completely for free," you just log in, it is well known, it rates corporates, countries and banks. Moody's and others exist but he uses S&P. The S&P site has videos explaining letter grades from **AAA to D**.
- Lookups he showed: **Germany AAA** ("the best rating ever, there's nothing above AAA"), **Spain AA-**, **India BBB** (same as State Bank of India), Japan also shown.
- Example use: you present to a tender in India, Indonesia or China and the beneficiary wants a bond or guarantee from an investment-grade bank. It "takes seconds" to check and you may discover the bank you worked with for two decades is speculative grade.
- Critique: in **2008** S&P was heavily criticized because it rated **Lehman Brothers AAA** and Lehman defaulted.
- Banks are not forever: in every country represented in the room (Saudi Arabia, US, Spain, India) "plenty of banks have disappeared in the last decade."

### Basel I, II, III and IV
- **Basel** is a city in Switzerland and also the name of the **international banking regulation** that applies to banks everywhere (Saudi Arabia, China, US, India), not only Europe. "You cannot reject Basel."
- **EXAM:** We are now in **Basel IV**; the slides show Basel I, II and III. **Basel I** set the minimum capital ratio; **Basel II** strengthened the minimum capital requirement and tied it to borrower quality; **Basel III** added **liquidity buffers** (short-term assets so a bank can repay in a liquidity squeeze).
- **EXAM:** Minimum capital ratio **8 percent**: if you borrow USD 10,000, the bank must keep **USD 800** "just for security." Banks dislike this because the more they keep, the less they can lend.
- Under Basel II the amount retained **depends on the quality of the borrower**: a low-equity borrower like Porsche forces the bank to retain more; a better borrower, less. Banks "like to lend money, not to keep their money in the vaults."
- Why Germany resisted (student answers he accepted): it went against the Mittelstand and Hausbank model; it forced short-term, ratings-based decisions over long-term relationships ("as a person, not as a number").
- Context of fear: dot-com bubble in 2000 and 9/11. Bank runs are dangerous precisely because most deposits are lent out. In the subprime crisis bank capital ratios were very low, so when borrower quality collapsed banks could not refund depositors.
- Macro link: raising minimum reserves is another way to **fight inflation**, because banks then have less appetite to lend, especially to riskier borrowers.
- Banks "don't like risk": negative working capital, unreliable clients, unreliable borrowers. Good borrowers are cheaper for them because they need to hold less capital.
- Know the **solvency and rating of the bank you work with**: "it would be very funny after one year in IE Business School you are working with a bank that is speculative grade."

### Relationship banking versus capital-market banking (case question 2)
- Most German firms finance through **banks**, not capital markets (the Anglo-Saxon model).
- Student answers he endorsed: relationship banking, long acquaintance, and a bank person sitting on the board, so the bank monitors internally rather than relying on paperwork.
- **Bank credit is flexible**: you do not sell part of your company. If you raise capital instead, someone buys part of your company. "The bank doesn't want your company; it wants to lend, charge fees and get repaid."
- **Bank credit is cheaper than equity**: the return shareholders demand is always higher than what the bank charges.
- German firms were used to bank support "regardless of the GDP levels": "it doesn't matter that your financials are not good enough, we support you, because we know you, your parents, your grandparents." Ironically, being family-owned and private made German banks more comfortable, not less.
- **The German way: analyze the debtor, not the applicant.** In Spain, the US and most countries the bank analyzes **you** (the person who walks in): no history, no capital, no loan. German banks focus more on **your project and your client**. Example: a startup with a contract from **Vestas** (Danish wind-turbine giant) gets funded in Germany; in Spain "plenty of startups died on arrival." This is a chicken-and-egg problem for Spanish entrepreneurship. President Obama praised the German corporate model and many countries want to copy it. Germany is the third-biggest exporter, "so it looks like it's working for them."
- German SMEs are "big, international and resilient," in 2003 and in 2026.

## Part 2: The case, section by section

### Opening quotes and framing (2001-2003)
- **Mario Ohoven**, German Association of Small and Medium-Sized Businesses, May 2002: big banks want nothing more to do with the Mittelstand; credit costs already rose in anticipation of Basel II; "**20-30 percent** of Mittelstand firms will not survive."
- **Karl Lichtblau**, IW Economics Institute, June 2002: to raise their equity base Mittelstand firms need "much greater openness," a complete change of thinking.
- **HVB Group**, December 2000: "We are committed to relationships"; a growth-oriented Mittelstand firm wants a partner that is both universal and investment bank.
- **Basel II**: after vetting over **250 position papers**, the **BIS** announced the revised Capital Accord in **June 2001**, then scheduled for **2005**. The **Basel Committee on Banking Supervision** was founded in **1975** by the central banks of the **G-10** (US, UK, Germany, France, Japan, Canada, Italy, plus Switzerland, Sweden, Belgium, Netherlands). The BIS itself was founded in **1930** in Basel to handle post-Versailles reparations. The EU would write Basel II into its supervision law.
- German reaction: industrial and banking associations warned of "grave" consequences for the **Mittelstand**. In **July 2001** the EU and Germany agreed to phase out state guarantees for Landesbanken. In **late October 2001** Chancellor **Gerhard Schröder**, running for reelection, said "In its current format Basel II is not acceptable for Germany" and threatened an EU veto. Economics minister **Werner Müller**: "The German system of financing the Mittelstand must not be influenced."
- Fear: Basel II would undermine the **Hausbank** relationship, a bank accompanying a firm "from cradle to grave" with secure long- and short-term loans. Basel II became "a cipher" for losing the most important source of outside financing.
- Bankers disagreed: **Bernd Fahrholz** (Dresdner Bank CEO): "With Basel II we are fundamentally on the right track."
- Basel II was a **wedge issue** in a larger fight: **Anglo-Saxon banking** (equity, shareholder value, transparency, external ratings, arm's-length capital markets) versus **relationship banking** (patient bank credit, privacy, strong ownership control).
- Economy slowing (Exhibits 1a-1c); record bankruptcies among Mittelstand firms; bad loans; talk of "banking crisis" and "credit crunch"; comparisons with Japanese banks.
- **Mid-March 2003**: Schröder met finance and bank leaders; Deutsche Bank chairman **Josef Ackermann** reportedly floated a state-funded **"bad bank"** for nonperforming loans. ECB, Bundesbank and bankers denied a system-wide crisis but the 2003 outlook was "not good."
- The four questions: is something inherently wrong with German banks; why alter a relationship system that worked for a century; is Basel II incompatible with relationship banking and will it raise Mittelstand capital costs; should reforms be delayed or accelerated.

### Germany at the Crossroads
- **82 million** people, **third-largest economy** in the world, worst business environment since World War II.
- Postwar: a **"social market economy"** balancing market freedom and social security; "social partnership" of parties, employers and unions; collective bargaining, arbitration and works councils replaced strikes; the **"economic miracle"** absorbed eastern refugees and millions of "guest workers."
- 1980s: talk of a "fading miracle," doubts about **Standort Deutschland** (industrial location) and **Finanzplatz Deutschland** (financial center).
- **1989** fall of the Berlin Wall; immediate reunification; Kohl promised brief pain and no tax rises; against Bundesbank advice the East German mark was converted at **1:1** for wages; fast privatization closed plants; unemployment over **20 percent** in some eastern areas; huge public borrowing for infrastructure, subsidies and welfare.
- Western Germany became one of the most expensive, overregulated locations: high wages, benefits, subsidies, taxes, energy costs, rents (Exhibits 2a-2b); inflexible labor law; unions hostile to longer hours; rising health premiums; rising unemployment.
- Fiscal: once the model for the EMU, Germany's deficit hit **3.7 percent** in 2002, above the **3 percent** Stability Pact limit, inviting a formal EU reprimand; pension reform urgent; aging population.
- **ECB** price-stability mandate slowed growth (ironically a Bundesbank design); low inflation raised fears of Japanese-style deflation; the **euro's 2002 appreciation** hurt exports.
- Education frayed; one report placed Germany near the bottom; too few trained in services, management and IT.
- Germany as the **"sick man of Europe."**

### Mittelstand
- Quiet reason Germany remained a leading exporter; mechanical engineering firms averaged **154 employees**; constellations of suppliers around carmakers; successes in fashion (**Hugo Boss, Jil Sander**) and software (**Mental Images**, effects for the Matrix films); **"hidden champions"** (Exhibit 3).
- Germany led the EU in 2001 in high-tech and medium-high-tech manufacturing employment; **nine of Europe's top 10** high-tech manufacturing regions were German.
- Official definition: **no more than 499 employees** and revenues **up to EUR 50 million**: **3.6 million** companies, **68 percent** of workers (about 20 million), **80 percent** of apprentices, **53 percent** of gross value, **47 percent** of taxable revenues, **46 percent** of gross investment; in manufacturing **42 percent** of jobs and **36.1 percent** of industrial sales. (Endnote: the EU definition differs, 50-250 employees, EUR 7-40 million revenues or EUR 5-27 million assets, and must be independent.)
- Qualitative meaning: independent, unlisted, family firms regardless of size. **J.M. Voith** had **23,000** employees yet its CFO called it Mittelstand because the family owned **93 percent**.
- Traits: flexible, adaptable, "shy" entrepreneurship, dense subcontracting networks, **"diversified quality production"** (high-quality niche goods, high skill, continuous improvement, R&D), strong identity around family, product or region; hands-on owners; local workforce like an extended family.
- Attitude: **Tessen von Heydebreck** (Deutsche Bank board): a "bastion of long-term thinking. They thought in generations." Conservative growth, family ownership, flexible bank loans.
- **Porsche**: former chairman Peter Schutz: "Porsche belongs to the families ... share prices are not what the company is about." In 2001 the **Piech family owned 57 percent**. Porsche was **removed from the Deutsche Börse mid-cap index for refusing quarterly reports**. Its CFO stressed building reserves and expensing costs fast to protect future profits: "In the U.S., corporate management doesn't have the kind of flexibility we have."
- Banks crucial for smaller firms without capital-market access; **strong inverse relationship between firm size and bank credit as a share of liabilities** (Exhibit 4).
- 1993 recession: **Landesbank Baden-Württemberg** offered over **DM 300 million** of liquidity loans to several hundred Mittelstand firms (auto, electronics, mechanical engineering).
- Successful exporters used more than one bank, but **40 percent** of German firms had an "extraordinarily stable" relationship with **just one**.
- Shareholder value seen as antithetical: Porsche in the late 1980s was saved by "patient" financing from **Landesgirokasse Stuttgart** and came back as a model of lean production. In a poll of about **300** firms, **57 percent** of executives felt shareholder value carried a hidden social risk.
- Banks also gave consulting: contacts, joint ventures, export and technology education; Deutsche Bank set up over **260** educational centers in branches in the 1990s.
- **Martin Kannegiesser** (Herbert Kannegiesser GmbH): bank credit "will remain the most flexible instrument"; long trust, profitability "sometimes right, sometimes less right," always covered by collateral; no time to optimize financial instruments; "If it is weakened, then the financing of a great portion of the Mittelstand will be in danger."
- Hidden problems: **one-third** of Mittelstand firms operated at a loss in 2001; about **500,000** family firms faced succession within five years; a weakening home base; difficulty financing moves abroad.
- **"Achilles' heel": thin equity.** Per **KfW** (the leading Mittelstand lender) average equity was **30 percent** in 2000, but firms under **EUR 10 million** revenues averaged **below 20 percent**, among Europe's lowest (the Bundesbank estimated 9-20 percent). US small businesses ran a **near-equal balance of equity and debt**, like large firms.
- New equity meant loss of control and more transparency. Kannegiesser: "We as owner-entrepreneurs will have to acquire the perspective of investors ... Whether that is good for the German economy, is an open question."

### Basel II: background and mechanics
- The furor after June 2001 came as Germany posted near **0 percent** real GDP growth, with the next two quarters negative.
- **Basel I** (1988, effective end of **1992**) followed the **1980s international debt crisis**: a level playing field of prudent regulation; **minimum capital ratio of 8 percent** of **risk-weighted assets**; riskier loan books need more capital; works like a minimum reserve requirement; enforced by national central banks and regulators.
- By the 1990s (Asian crisis) Basel I looked inadequate: it ignored **credit derivatives** and **securitization** (footnote: pooling similar assets such as mortgages and selling slices as securities; derivatives are contracts on the risks and returns of underlying assets without transferring them; together they make risk tradable); it used one blunt measure, **probability of default**; it diverged from market risk; it failed to stop **regulatory arbitrage** because national risk assessments varied.
- Reform goal: calculate each bank's actual risk profile in a differentiated but standardized way aligned with best-practice risk management, so markets help discipline lending; better risk assessment could even lower required capital. Trade-off: prescriptive rules (harmonized but rigid and costly) versus principles (streamlined but leaving too much leeway).
- **Three pillars of Basel II**: (1) **minimum capital adequacy**, still **8 percent**, but with a reformed, more precise risk measurement in the **denominator** using a "menu" of approaches and a "thicker" rulebook, which seemed to make the denominator larger or at least more complicated; (2) stronger **supervisory review**, with BIS supervisors consulting extensively with banks, based on rating-agency assessments and formal bank-internal risk-weighting models; (3) **market discipline through disclosure**, an unprecedented level of transparency.
- Footnote on risk types: **credit risk** (loss through default, the Basel I standard), **market risk** (adverse price moves on trading), and the most controversial, **operational risk** (losses from inadequate internal systems, fraud or external events).
- Allianz's chief economist: "For the first time [German] banks will have to put a market value on a company and properly assess its creditworthiness."

### Basel II controversy
- The Mittelstand read Basel II as a coming **credit crunch**: higher risk weights for low-equity firms meant downgrades and a higher cost of capital. **KfW** feared worse conditions and expected "Mittelstand-friendly modifications." The **BDI** (Association of German Industry) said healthy SMEs would be penalized for belonging to an industry branch rated less creditworthy.
- **Long-term loans (five years or more)** got a higher credit weight. BDI: long-term investment credit matters more in Germany than in Anglo-Saxon countries, so German firms would be disadvantaged and "short termism" would arise. The German Chambers of Commerce called it "unacceptable." Baden-Württemberg's economics minister: no additional charge for long-term credit; the long-term Hausbank relationship "systematically promotes stability."
- European cooperative and savings bank associations: Basel II was "shaped for the biggest and most highly sophisticated banks"; risks "overcalibrated." The **DSGV** (German Savings Bank Association) said the twice-yearly disclosure required nearly **1,000 details**, "information overkill"; small banks (also in the US) could not afford it.
- Non-German critics: London "dissidents" said it was too complex; the **Institute of International Finance** (over **300** global banks) warned the cost would deter banks and regulators from applying it; the director of Wharton's **Lauder Institute** said it was "on the wrong track," confused risk types, had measurement problems, and might "hardwire" politicized risk weights that only a long political process could change. European industry associations argued SMEs as a diversified group pose **less** credit risk than large companies. Final criticism: **pro-cyclicality**.

### Ratings controversy
- Fear that formal internal or external ratings would "constrict a vital artery of bank loans," dry up credit in bad times, and reduce decisions to ratios and computers; one expert called standardized ratings "an attack on relationship banking."
- **Hermann Remsperger** (Bundesbank board): the main challenge was supervising internal bank ratings; risk of greater "pro-cyclicality of credit movements," since in bad times ratings force banks to cut loan volume to keep their risk-weighted capital equation.
- The **Federal Supervisory Office for Financial Services** agreed with Basel's thrust but said external ratings were designed for publicly held firms with separated ownership and control, not owner-managed firms; close alignment of ownership and control plus long-term relationships assured "prudent behavior" without more equity, a structure German supervisors wanted to encourage.
- Power of **Moody's, Standard & Poor's and Fitch** would grow. Von Heydebreck warned of conflicts of interest (agencies also sell other services) and too few agencies. Alternative agencies such as **Creditreform** built cheaper SME ratings, not yet widely accepted. Most German banks would rely on **bank-internal ratings**.
- Shorter horizons: a downgrade makes credit dearer just when it is needed most. **Alois Gigler** (waste-recycling owner) could not get a loan of several hundred thousand euros to bridge delivery and payment because **HVB's** formal internal rating deemed it too risky, with many forms and staff turnover; his local **savings bank**, where he dealt with the same two people for over **30 years** and which had funded his start-up, helped. "Big banks lack the same stability of relationships." Shows tension between commercial and savings banks.
- Ratings as opportunity: move from a historically oriented relationship loan to a future-oriented business model; Bochum finance institute director: see Basel II "as an occasion to tackle the structural weaknesses"; think clearly about cost of capital; a leading lender (IKB's chairman) said ratings build a controlling system as an "early warning system," "soft facts" stay in the decision, and ratings bring "a new quality into the partnership."
- **Axel Wieandt** (Deutsche Bank): ratings make the market transparent and let banks package Mittelstand loans into tradable securities, stimulating private equity and venture capital. **Oliver, Wyman & Co.**: Basel II will expose "uneconomic pricing of bank loans" and drive "liquification of credit and growth of bond financing."
- **KfW securitization**: KfW bought Mittelstand loans from banks, bundled them and issued **collateralized loan obligations (CLOs)** backed by its **AAA** rating; risk left bank balance sheets and the pool of capital for the Mittelstand grew. But this needs formal ratings for both firms and banks, which most savings banks and cooperatives lacked.

### The July 2002 concessions
- Pressure from Mittelstand firms across Europe and the German and Italian governments made the Basel Committee alter its risk weights in **July 2002**:
  - Bank loans **under EUR 1 million** treated as **retail credits** to private clients, needing less equity coverage than corporate loans.
  - Companies with turnover between **EUR 1 million and EUR 50 million** could be given a **lower risk weighting** than an external rating would assess.
  - Companies with up to **EUR 500 million** turnover or total assets would **not** pay a surcharge for **long-term credits**.
  - Deadline extended to **January 2007**.
- DSGV, "relatively satisfied": the danger of systematic discrimination against Mittelstand financing "appears to have been averted." The Committee also "caved in" to American banks on certain **credit card portfolios**. Some asked whether the compromises undermined Basel II.
- Even so, Basel II would reshape bank-client relationships, internal operations and capital positions; large commercial banks already had sophisticated controls, the bulk of German banks had to change extensively.

### Reforming Finanzplatz Deutschland
- Basel II was one piece of a broad reform drive to build capital markets (Exhibit 5), often led by large commercial banks: upgrade the German Stock Exchange, promote IPOs, make **Frankfurt** a rival to London.
- A series of **Financial Market Promotion Acts** deregulated services, raised transparency and shareholder protection. **1994**: Federal Bank Supervisory Office for Securities, the equivalent of the US **SEC**. **May 2002**: banking, securities and insurance supervision fused into **BAFin** (Federal Financial Supervisory Office). Further legislation harmonized German and EU law toward a single **"passport"** for a unified European financial market.

### Three pillars of German banking
- The most important feature: a **"three-pillar" structure**: (1) **public sector** (Landesbanken and Sparkassen), (2) **credit cooperatives** (Genossenschaften), (3) **private commercial banks** (Exhibit 6). Outlines formed by the late 19th century (Exhibit 7).
- The largest four private banks did **not** dominate (Exhibits 8-9). Germany had one of Europe's most **fragmented** systems; assets split roughly equally between public and private banks. Switzerland and Austria had similar pillars; in public ownership Germany most resembled **Spain**; the UK and US had little or none.
- All three groups became **universal banks** (deposit taking and lending plus issuing, underwriting, brokerage). The US **prohibited universal banking 1933-1999**. Competition and deregulation pushed German banks to upgrade investment banking.
- **Allfinanz** strategy: comprehensive financial and consulting services; cheap credit lured clients into higher-margin services (**bundling, cross-selling**). Loan margins averaged a "razor-thin" **1 percent** versus **2-3.5 percent** in Europe and **4 percent** in the US; one banker called such loans "subterranean." Germany was among the most **"overbanked"** (Exhibit 10) and least profitable markets (Exhibits 11a-11b).
- **Merrill Lynch**: "The German banking market has long been considered a **graveyard for shareholder value**," because public-sector banks are driven by **stakeholder** rather than shareholder value. **Deutsche Bank Research**: distortions misallocated capital and produced "too little equity, too much bank debt."
- **Savings banks and Landesbanken**: Sparkassen were "the friendly face" of banking; Josef Plöckl (Schrobenhausen): not a profit institution but one "for the inhabitants of the town." Critics: a nepotistic "personal financial henchman" for local officials' prestige projects. **12 Landesbanken** in 2001 served roughly **700** savings banks, ranked among the largest banks, were partly owned by savings banks and states, acted as Hausbank to state governments, wholesalers and clearinghouses for savings banks, corporate lenders, and lenders to international firms and governments.
- **Public guarantees** (introduced **1931** as deposit insurance for small savers): **Anstaltslast** (maintenance obligation: the guarantor ensures the bank can function and offsets deficits if reserves are depleted; not a running subsidy) and **Gewährträgerhaftung** (guarantor liability: unlimited liability of the public owner for all liabilities beyond assets). With them Landesbanken were rated **AAA or AA+** by S&P and could lend up to **50 basis points** cheaper than commercial banks; without them S&P predicted a **single A or lower**.
- **July 2001** EU-Germany compromise: guarantees phased out after **2005** (Exhibit 5: in phases till 2015). The EU Competition Commission called them an illegal subsidy; the DSGV said they let savings banks compete with commercial banks, increasing competition. The decision struck at German federalist industrial policy and a two-centuries-old social insurance objective.
- **Credit cooperatives** ("people's banks"): local, private, self-help. The central cooperative bank **DZ Bank** became the **sixth-largest** bank by 2001, a "partner of the Mittelstand"; it backed **SAP** from the start when other banks ignored it and underwrote SAP's **1988 IPO**. Cooperatives had one of the densest branch networks (Exhibit 10).
- **Private commercial banks**: Deutsche Bank, Dresdner Bank, Commerzbank and **HVB** (formed by a 1999 merger) led large-scale corporate finance since the mid-19th century, with recurring criticism that they neglected much of the economy or formed a power bloc. Despite near destruction by American occupation authorities, they led underwriting and lending to large firms; long-term bank financing rose dramatically (Exhibit 12) and only recently declined in favor of other lenders and equity (Exhibit 13).
- 1990s redefinition: streamline branches, upgrade investment banking, compete internationally, improve profitability. **Deutsche Bank**: **1989** acquisition of **Morgan Grenfell** (London), many European banks, **1999 Bankers Trust** (US), **2002 Scudder Investments**; listed on the **NYSE in September 2001**; by 2002 called itself global not German, about **half** its staff abroad; first **foreign (Swiss) head** in its history; rumors it might leave Frankfurt. Dresdner and Commerzbank followed with less success; WestLB and HVB also hired investment bankers; all suffered organizational, pay and cultural difficulties. **The Banker** (1996): ventures abroad were a poor fix for an overbanked home market, high admin costs and "crumbling" margins.
- Mergers: **March 2000** Deutsche-Dresdner talks collapsed over investment banking; Dresdner-Commerzbank talks also collapsed; the same week **HVB took over Bank Austria** and renamed itself **HVB Group** ("Bank of Regions," central and eastern Europe). **April 2001 Allianz** made a friendly bid for **Dresdner**, extending Allfinanz to **bancassurance** (retail and investment banking, asset management, insurance); it gained a fund manager and a retail network; the deal gave **Munich Re a 26 percent** share of HVB.

### Deutschland AG: German corporate governance
- Large firms' century-old bank ties created the image of **Deutschland AG**. Banks held **five levers** over big business:
  1. **Lending relationships** that could guide investment projects.
  2. **Equity stakes** (Exhibit 14), modest in size but concealing influence; banks and insurers sat "like spiders" in a web of **cross-shareholdings** and insider negotiations (Exhibit 15); the network loosened in the late 1990s but ties stayed strong.
  3. **Mediating securities issuance and trading**, especially in crises, restructurings and M&A; banks had the **exclusive right to issue new corporate securities** under the Stock Exchange Act; bank shareholding signaled solidity to outside investors; universal banking let investment banking losses be offset by lending and vice versa, spreading risk (relevant to Basel II's pro-cyclicality).
  4. A **seat on the supervisory board**. Since **1884** German joint-stock companies have a **two-tier board**: **Aufsichtsrat** (supervisory) and **Vorstand** (executive). The leading bank often chaired the supervisory board, which appoints or fires executives (with cause; usual **five-year** terms), monitors performance, approves pay and dividends, reviews statements, calls special audits, and must approve major investments and strategy; by law it cannot intervene in day-to-day affairs.
  5. **Proxy voting rights (Auftragsstimmrecht)**: most shares were **bearer shares**, so banks voted custodial shares under regularly renewed authorization; the more dispersed the ownership, the greater the bank's power. Most criticism focused here.
- Defenders: long-term relationships overcame outsiders' information disadvantages. A mid-1990s study: banks voted about **90 percent** of votes at the largest **24** dispersed-ownership companies and **always rubber-stamped management**. **Rolf-E. Breuer** (Deutsche Bank) called the delegation expedient and "wrongly criticized."
- **Codetermination**: worker seats on supervisory boards. Three models (footnote): **mining and steel**, over **1,000** employees, full parity with a neutral tie-breaker; firms over **500** employees, **one-third** worker seats; the **1976** law, over **2,000** employees, "quasi-parity," workers elect half, shareholders elect half and the chairman with a tie-breaking vote. Economic impact ambiguous: higher productivity, but gains partly went to labor, diluting shareholder value; a director said boards dare not question managers in front of works councilors, so control diminished. Mittelstand firms were affected more by works councils and collective bargaining than by codetermination.
- **2001 tax reform**: corporate tax cut to a uniform **25 percent**; **capital gains tax eliminated** (from January 2002) on sales of shareholding packages, designed to unravel cross-shareholdings.
- Defense of the bank role: efficient use of capital and information drove 19th-century growth and the postwar miracle; patient capital plus close monitoring allowed long-term investment; equity plus debt stakes reduced agency risk; face-to-face discipline rather than arm's length.
- Critics: a cozy Hausbank relationship shielded CEOs from market pressure and hostile takeovers. **Vodafone's 2000 hostile bid for Mannesmann** prompted the headline "goodbye to Deutschland AG." Others: the market for control was more robust than it looked; most takeovers were friendly transfers of controlling minority blocs; behind-the-scenes hostility was similar to Britain's; banks sometimes backed predators; less transparent than the UK or US.
- Scandals of the mid-1990s questioned bank monitoring: supervisory boards had up to **21** members and met **twice a year** for a few hours. The near collapse of **Bankgesellschaft Berlin** in **2001** on bad real-estate loans, rescued by a massive state capital injection under the guarantee, questioned lending practices, controls, public monitoring, auditors and state-bank ties to officials.
- Lack of disclosure of board memberships and pay; doubts about auditor independence; a postwar Deutsche Bank chairman quipped it was easier to hold a greased pig by the tail than hold a supervisory board member liable.
- **February 2002**: voluntary **German Corporate Governance Code** (Cromme Commission). Breuer: it would end the "behind-the-scenes arrangements (**Kungelei**) of Deutschland AG." Bayer's supervisory chairman: "Our system is just as good and just as bad as the Anglo-Saxon board system. Enron occurred in spite of the American system."

### Deutsche Börse: a missing pillar?
- Bank intermediation allegedly crippled a shareholder-value capital market; the exchange was small and historically volatile; bank strength traced back to the scandals and bubble that burst in **1873**.
- **2000 market capitalization to GDP: Germany 66.8 percent**, versus US **154.9**, UK **184**, Sweden **147.6**, France **108.7**, Switzerland **330**; closer to Italy **70.3** and Japan **69** (Exhibit 16). Few joint-stock companies were listed (Exhibits 17-18).
- Large firms bypassed banks for bonds, often via foreign subsidiaries, but in 2000 corporate bond issues were only **6 percent** of medium- and long-term bank loan volume. The Bundesbank: most Mittelstand firms had little chance of bond finance, and fixed-interest bonds lack bank-credit flexibility.
- **1995** partial privatization of **Deutsche Telekom** and the **1997 Neuer Markt** (Germany's NASDAQ) raised hopes; investment-fund ownership (many American) rose (Exhibit 14), concentrating on the **DAX 30**; about half of DAX 30 firms introduced stock options.
- Of **540** listed companies only about **150** were actively traded. Free float varied: **Bayer 94.5 percent**, **Beiersdorf (Nivea) 18.7 percent**. Household names fully private: **Robert Bosch** (foundation), **Aldi**, **Melitta**; family-controlled: **Bertelsmann, Porsche, BMW**. About **87 percent** of listed firms in 1991 had a blockholder of **25 percent or more** (Exhibit 19). Dispersed ownership is the international exception; Germany is typical. One study found dispersed ownership hurt corporate reputation in Germany.
- Post-March 2000 downturn: Deutsche Telekom shares slid; the Neuer Markt peaked at **338** listings, lost almost **95 percent** of its value in scandals, and in **September 2002** Deutsche Börse announced its closure. A banker: "Retail investing had been wiped out for a whole generation, at least 15 or 20 years." Smaller firms would rely on bank credit.

### Downturn 2002
- Slow growth, unemployment, bankruptcies and a bank earnings crisis overshadowed the Basel debate. Mittelstand owners said commercial banks rationed credit in favor of global investment banking, acting "as if Basel II were already in force." **April 2002**: Schröder promised a **special Mittelstand bank** after the election.
- **Commerzbank's** CEO denied refusing to lend and wanted to be the "number one bank" for the Mittelstand, cross-selling investment banking products. **Deutsche Bank** stayed committed "only if it could generate a fair risk-adjusted return for shareholders."
- Analysts expected less lending to shore up capital ratios; Commerzbank fought rumors of a **solvency crisis in summer 2002**.
- End of 2002: all major commercial banks except **Deutsche Bank** posted losses; **Commerzbank and HVB** were in the red for the **first time in their postwar history**; they sold assets, unwound shareholdings, spun off units and cut costs; the Dresdner merger became "a nightmare for Allianz"; public banks were hit too. Vicious circle: falling share prices and market capitalization drained reserves, forcing higher loan-risk provisions and less lending, causing more bankruptcies.
- **November 2002**: Breuer (commercial bank association president) proposed that private banks **take over savings banks**; the three groups were "a stuck brake." Commercial banks would gain deposits and retail arms; savings banks would gain investment-banking advice, controls and ratings systems. The DSGV president: "problems need to be solved where they were caused" (investment banking losses); mergers would limit competition. Schröder: "changing the system of savings banks is out of the question." Consolidation among savings banks and Landesbanken likely but resisted locally; cooperatives needed consolidation.
- **February 2003**: courts ruled Breuer breached client confidentiality by publicly doubting **Kirch Media Group's** finances before its bankruptcy; a Düsseldorf court proceeded against **Ackermann** for "breach of trust" over the generous **Mannesmann** severance after the Vodafone takeover (with others, including the former IG Metall head). Both appealed. The Financial Times saw "wider resonance" over Germany's adoption of Anglo-American practices.

### Banking on Germany? (closing)
- Was the move to Anglo-Saxon capital markets desirable, what would be gained and lost, and did Basel II, the EU decision on public banks and capital-market reforms end relationship banking?
- **Hannes Rehm** (Norddeutsche Landesbank): banks will no longer "accompany customers through good and bad times or ... open the umbrella when the rain gusts"; because of Basel II they must orient to other norms; "inevitable."
- **Bernd Rudolph**: "The classic bank credit is in retreat." One executive hoped to "intelligently web together" target-oriented investment banking with relationship-oriented commercial banking; but could banks have it both ways?
- **US regulators balked** at applying Basel II except to a few banks, citing cost and complexity. The European Commission expected draft legislation in June; the Basel Committee issued its revised accord and quantitative impact study in **April and May 2003**.
- Human ending: **Norbert Neuenhofer**, third generation running the **177-year-old** printing firm **B. Kühlen KG**, filed for bankruptcy; Doris Kretschmann, whose husband lost his management job there at 49: "I'm afraid of what comes next."

## Likely exam angles
- Know that **investment grade is BBB- and above** on S&P; anything below (BB+, BB, B, CCC) is speculative grade; the scale runs AAA to D.
- Know that when quoting a bank's rating you use the **local currency, long-term** rating; a bank has four ratings (local/foreign, long/short).
- Know that State Bank of India is **BBB**, India **BBB**, Spain **AA-**, Germany **AAA**; Lehman Brothers was rated AAA in 2008 before defaulting.
- Know that **public banks carry their sovereign's rating** (CaixaBank, Sabadell, Bankinter below BBB- in 2012; Santander and BBVA kept investment grade).
- Know that the three big agencies (S&P, Moody's, Fitch) are **US-based** and S&P is free to consult.
- Know that **Basel I (1988, in force end 1992)** set the **8 percent** minimum capital to risk-weighted assets after the 1980s debt crisis; **Basel II (June 2001)** kept 8 percent but tied risk weights to borrower quality and added three pillars (minimum capital, supervisory review, disclosure); **Basel III** added liquidity buffers; **Basel IV** is current.
- Know that Basel II added **market risk** and the controversial **operational risk** to credit risk.
- Know the **July 2002 concessions**: loans under EUR 1 million as retail, lower weights for EUR 1-50 million turnover, no long-term surcharge up to EUR 500 million, deadline moved to January 2007.
- Know that Schröder said Basel II was "not acceptable for Germany" in **October 2001**, and that opposition came mainly from **corporates (BDI, chambers, Mittelstand)**, not banks (Dresdner's Fahrholz: "on the right track").
- Know the Mittelstand definitions: German official up to **499 employees and EUR 50 million**; EU SME up to **250 employees and EUR 50 million**; 3.6 million firms, 68 percent of workers, 80 percent of apprentices.
- Know that the Mittelstand's "Achilles' heel" is **equity under 20 percent** for firms under EUR 10 million (average 30 percent), versus about 50-50 equity and debt in US small firms.
- Know the Mittelstand traits: family-owned, low equity, strong identity, high R&D, export-oriented, little attention to cost of capital; **Porsche** (Piech family 57 percent, kicked off the mid-cap index over quarterly reports; CFO template showed negative working capital of about USD 6.5 and 5.3 billion in 2010).
- Know the **three pillars of German banking**: public (Sparkassen plus Landesbanken, 36 percent of assets), cooperatives (8.7 percent, DZ Bank sixth largest, backed SAP's 1988 IPO), private commercial (28.4 percent, Big Four 16 percent). Aguirre counted four by separating Landesbanken from savings banks.
- Know that the state guarantees **Anstaltslast** and **Gewährträgerhaftung** gave Landesbanken AAA/AA+ ratings and 50 basis points cheaper loans, and the EU deal of **July 2001** phases them out after 2005.
- Know that Germany was "overbanked" (2,915 credit cooperatives alone in 1990, almost 3,000 banks as Aguirre put it), with loan margins of 1 percent versus 2-3.5 percent in Europe and 4 percent in the US, ROA 0.21 versus 1.77 percent in the US (2001), and ROE falling from 19.34 (1998) to 4.38 percent (2002).
- Know the **five levers** of Deutschland AG (lending, equity stakes, securities issuance, supervisory board seats, proxy voting) and the **two-tier board since 1884**; banks voted about 90 percent at the largest 24 firms.
- Know the capital market numbers: market cap **66.8 percent** of GDP (US 154.9, UK 184, Switzerland 330); bonds 6 percent of bank loans; 540 listed, about 150 traded; Neuer Markt 338 listings, down 95 percent, closed September 2002.
- Know the 2002 downturn facts: all big commercial banks except Deutsche lost money; Commerzbank and HVB first postwar losses; Ackermann's "bad bank" idea in March 2003; Breuer convicted over Kirch; Ackermann tried over Mannesmann severance.
- Know bank credit is **flexible and cheaper than equity** (no dilution, shareholder returns exceed bank interest), and the umbrella saying: banks lend when it is sunny and take it back when it rains.
- Know that reunification was done at **1:1**, the 2002 deficit was **3.7 percent** against a **3 percent** limit, and Germany was "the sick man of Europe."

## Traps and look-alikes
- **BBB versus BBB-**: both are investment grade; the cut-off is BBB-. BB+ is the top of speculative grade. Do not say "above BBB" is the rule.
- **Local currency long term versus the other three ratings**: quote local-currency long term unless specifically asked about foreign-currency debt.
- **Basel I versus Basel II versus Basel III**: I = 8 percent ratio (1988); II = same 8 percent but risk-sensitive denominator plus supervision and disclosure (2001 proposal, 2007 deadline after concessions); III = liquidity buffers; IV = today.
- **8 percent minimum capital versus loan margin of 1 percent**: the 8 percent is capital against risk-weighted assets; the 1 percent is the German bank lending margin (Europe 2-3.5, US 4).
- **Three pillars of Basel II versus three pillars of German banking**: Basel pillars are capital, supervision, disclosure; banking pillars are public, cooperative, private commercial. Exhibit 6 adds a fourth column of specialized banks (71 institutions, 26.9 percent of assets) that is not a universal-bank pillar.
- **Sparkassen versus Landesbanken**: Sparkassen are municipal savings banks (about 700); Landesbanken (12 in 2001) are regional state banks at the apex that serve the Sparkassen and the state. Both are in the public pillar and both had the state guarantees.
- **Anstaltslast versus Gewährträgerhaftung**: maintenance obligation (keep the bank functioning, not a running subsidy) versus guarantor liability (unlimited liability for all liabilities beyond assets).
- **Credit risk versus market risk versus operational risk**: default losses (Basel I); adverse price moves on trading; losses from failed systems, fraud or external events (most controversial, new in Basel II).
- **Securitization versus credit derivatives**: pooling assets and selling slices (KfW's AAA-backed CLOs) versus contracts on the risk of assets without transferring them.
- **German Mittelstand definition versus EU SME definition**: 499 employees versus 250 employees, both EUR 50 million turnover; the EU adds an independence requirement.
- **Relationship (Hausbank) banking versus Anglo-Saxon capital-market banking**: patient credit, privacy, control, internal monitoring by a board seat versus equity, shareholder value, transparency, external ratings, arm's length.
- **Who opposed Basel II**: German corporates and their associations and the savings/cooperative banks (information overkill), not the big commercial banks (Fahrholz, Deutsche's Wieandt in favor).
- **Aufsichtsrat versus Vorstand**: supervisory board (appoints, monitors, approves strategy, cannot run daily business) versus executive board (manages).
- **Codetermination thresholds**: over 1,000 employees in mining and steel (full parity), over 500 (one-third), over 2,000 (1976 quasi-parity).
- **Blockholding thresholds (Exhibit 19)**: 25 percent veto and Cartel Office reporting; 50 percent control; 75 percent can dismiss the supervisory board.
- **Negative working capital in a supermarket versus a carmaker**: normal for retail (cash in, suppliers at 180 days); a red flag for an automobile manufacturer like Porsche.
- **Bank credit versus equity**: credit does not dilute ownership and is cheaper; equity costs more because shareholders demand a higher return.
- **Deutsche Bank acquisitions**: Morgan Grenfell 1989 (London), Bankers Trust 1999 (US), Scudder 2002; NYSE listing September 2001. Allianz bought Dresdner (2001), not Commerzbank; HVB took Bank Austria (2000); Munich Re held 26 percent of HVB.
- **Breuer versus Ackermann cases**: Breuer, breach of client confidentiality over Kirch; Ackermann, breach of trust over Mannesmann severance.
