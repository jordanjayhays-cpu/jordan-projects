# Session 6: Ready to internationalize? (Prof. Pablo Neira, deck dated October 2026, taught 2 Oct 2026 as session 8)

This unit is the third box in the course roadmap (global trends, benefits and reasons, **ready to internationalize?**, where, how). It asks one question: before a company picks a country or an entry mode, is it actually ready to go abroad? The deck answers with a **strategic analysis in the home country** built from three pieces: (1) **external analysis** of the industry with **Porter's Five Forces**, (2) **internal analysis** of how the company competes, using **Porter's generic strategies** and the **Ansoff matrix**, and (3) **resources and capabilities analysis** using the **Resource Based View (RBV)** and the **VRIO** test. In class Neira said these are not the only frameworks, but the three areas (industry, how you compete, resources and capabilities) are the ones you must cover. His closing emphasis: look inside; most successful companies win because of resources and capabilities that give a **sustained** competitive advantage, and a temporary advantage is "no good." He also stressed being realistic about funding and the 6 to 18 month ramp-up before a new market makes money.

### Slide 12: What determines the level of profit in an industry? Who is Michael Porter?
- **Michael Porter** (born 1947) is a management guru and professor at **Harvard Business School**.
- Engineer, **PhD in Economics and MBA, both from Harvard**; bestselling author; founder of the consulting firm **Monitor**.
- He redefined how business people think about competition by bringing the language and concepts of **economics into corporate strategy**.
- He began by simplifying the notion of competitive advantage, then built a framework for how to achieve it.
- His approach rests on the overall economics and structure of complex systems rather than individual parts.
- The test for each force: **"Is it sufficiently strong to reduce or eliminate industry profits?"** (highlighted on the slide).
- The diagram on the right shows the five forces around **industry competitors** (rivalry among existing firms) in the centre, lettered: **a Substitutes** (threat of substitutes, from the right), **b Potential entrants** (threat of new entrants, from the left), **c Industry competitors** (centre), **d Buyers** (bargaining power of buyers, from below), **e Suppliers** (bargaining power of suppliers, from above).

### Slide 13: Porter's Five Forces Competition Framework (diagram)
- Same picture, full size: suppliers above, buyers below, potential entrants left, substitutes right, rivalry in the middle. The point: profit in an industry is squeezed from five directions, not only by direct rivals.

### Slide 14: 5 Porter's forces summary and how to apply the model
- This slide lists the **determinants** of each force (image only, transcribed here):
- **Supplier power:** buyers' price sensitivity, relative bargaining power (see buyer power for detail, the logic mirrors it).
- **Threat of entry:** capital requirements, economies of scale, absolute cost advantages, product differentiation, access to distribution, legal barriers, retaliation.
- **Industry rivalry:** concentration, diversity of competitors, product differentiation, excess capacity and exit barriers, cost conditions.
- **Substitute competition:** buyers' propensity to substitute, relative prices and performance of substitutes.
- **Buyer power:** two parts. **Price sensitivity:** cost of product relative to total cost, product differentiation, competition between buyers. **Bargaining power:** size and concentration of buyers relative to producers, buyers' switching costs, buyers' information, buyers' ability to backward integrate.
- **HOW TO APPLY THE MODEL (three steps):** 1. **Identification of the industry.** 2. **Identification of the players** in the industry. 3. **Sequential analysis of each force.**

### Slide 15: (a) Substitutes: products with equivalent function
- Definition of a substitute: a product with an **equivalent function**.
- Two drivers, each with high and low substitution examples:

| Driver | Logic | High substitution | Low substitution |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Number of close substitutes** | The more substitutes, the more competitive the industry and the lower the prices, because the customer can switch to another product when prices rise | Newspapers, travel agency, urban transportation | Gasoline, cigarettes |
| **Buyers' propensity to substitute** | Driven by similar performance and price, similar occasion for use, same geographic market | Audi Q5 vs Land Rover, pizza vs burger, Amazon vs El Corte Inglés | Speed train vs bus, orange juice vs cola, cement in Madrid and Paris |

- What the pairs illustrate: Audi Q5 and Land Rover have similar performance and price (easy to switch); pizza and burger share the occasion for use; Amazon and El Corte Inglés compete in the same market. Cement in Madrid and in Paris is low substitution because they are not in the same geographic market; a high-speed train and a bus differ in performance and price.

### Slide 16: (b) Potential entrants: new firms and firms diversifying from other industries
- Seven **barriers to entry**, each with a definition and an example:

| Barrier | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Capital requirement** | Initial investment needed to enter the industry | Boeing and Airbus (high investment); smartphone apps (low cost of developing) |
| **Economies of scale** | Large indivisible investments that must be amortized over a large volume of output | New development cost of the A380, estimated at **USD 18 billion**, requires **at least 400 units** to break even |
| **Absolute cost advantage** | Established firms may have advantages over new entrants: ownership of low-cost sources and economies of learning | Aramco (low price of raw reserves); Intel's experience in microprocessors (learning economies) |
| **Product differentiation** | Brand recognition and customer loyalty for established firms | Cosmetics (high product differentiation); low-cost carriers (limited differentiation) |
| **Access to channel distribution** | Mainly for consumer goods, which depend on the retailer; the internet has diminished this barrier | Mercadona (leader in distribution in Spain) limits the brands that appear on its shelf space |
| **Government and legal barriers** | Some economists claim these are the only truly effective barriers to entry | Traffic rights between countries limit new airlines' entry and the frequencies offered |
| **Retaliation** | The entrant's expectation of the established firms' reaction | Airlines frequently react with price-cutting on former routes when another airline starts operating |

### Slide 17: (c) Rivalry between existing competitors: major determinant of profitability
- Rivalry is called the **major determinant of profitability**. Four factors:

| Factor | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Seller concentration** | Number and size of firms competing in a market; market share of the **largest 4** is the indicator. In a monopoly the firm can exercise high price influence; in a duopoly firms tend to have similar prices and compete on advertising and innovation | **Gillette** dominates razor blades; **Coca-Cola and Pepsi** are a duopoly with similar prices and a focus on marketing |
| **Product differentiation** | If there is no differentiation the product becomes a **commodity** and firms compete on price | **Restaurants** differ significantly (type of food, dishes, atmosphere, location); **petrol stations** cannot differentiate their core product (they compete on location, convenience) |
| **Excess capacity and exit barriers** | Imbalance between demand and capacity pushes firms to cut prices to stimulate demand; exit barriers are the costs of reducing capacity (job protection, infrastructure) | **Seaside hotels** cut prices in winter to stimulate demand and improve occupancy |
| **Scale economies and fixed vs variable costs** | High fixed cost relative to variable cost causes price competition; more scale economies push companies to grow and compete on price | Scale efficiency in the **automotive** industry is core (low margin, high volume) |

### Slide 18: (d, e) Bargaining power and price sensitivity: competition in the market for inputs and outputs
- **Buyers' power** has two components.
- **Price sensitivity** depends on: 1. importance of the product relative to cost; 2. competition between buyers; 3. differentiation of the product. Examples: automakers as buyers of car components (components are a big share of their cost, so they are price sensitive); El Corte Inglés buying T-shirts.
- **Bargaining power** depends on: 1. **size and concentration of buyers vs suppliers** (few buyers making big purchases means a high cost of losing one); 2. **buyers' information** (more informed buyers find lower prices more easily); 3. **vertical integration** (do it yourself vs depend on the supplier). Examples: 1. a customer has low bargaining power negotiating with electrical contractors; 2. travel search engines (Booking.com, KAYAK) let buyers compare fares easily; 3. unlike Gap and H&M, which buy their clothes from suppliers, **Zara makes most of its own**.
- **Suppliers' power** (price sensitivity and bargaining power again): the analysis of relative power between producers and their suppliers is **precisely analogous** to the producer-buyer analysis. The only difference is that now the firms are the buyers and the producers of inputs are the suppliers.

### Slide 26: 2. Internal analysis (second tool added)
- Same framing: how is the company competing, is the strategy solid and proven? Tools: 1) Porter strategies, 2) **Ansoff matrix**.

### Slide 27: Ansoff matrix, why
- What **growth strategy** is the company **currently** using in its local market?
- Is it **sustainable and transferable** to foreign markets? Should the company **change** the growth strategy?
- The matrix **helps businesses decide their market growth strategy**: entering into new foreign markets?

### Slide 28: Ansoff matrix, the two levers
- **Only two approaches** to any growth strategy, varying: **1. what is sold; 2. to whom it is sold.**
- Boxed: **Selling new or existing products in new or existing markets.**
- Footer note: it is all about choice; business strategy is not either or (companies can combine).

### Slide 29: Ansoff matrix (picture)
- Axes: **Products** (current, new) across; **Market** (current, new) down.
- Bottom left: **Market penetration** (current product, current market). Bottom right: **Product development** (new product, current market). Top left: **Market development** (current product, new market). Top right: **Diversification** (new product, new market).

### Slide 30: Market penetration and product development
- **MARKET PENETRATION:** selling an existing product in an existing market.
- **Only possible in a growing market which is not yet saturated.**
- Seeks **four** main objectives: a) maintain or increase the market share of current products; b) secure dominance of growth markets; c) restructure a mature market by **driving out competitors**, typically with an aggressive pricing strategy; d) increase usage by existing customers.
- Footer note: open new locations; less risky because you know the customer; organic growth.
- Pictures: **Aldi** store and **Domino's** pizza (chains that grow by opening more outlets).
- **PRODUCT DEVELOPMENT:** developing **new products to sell in existing markets**. Usually employed with **branded goods**, so that the qualities of the new product are linked to the customer's confidence in the established brand.
- Pictures: the Coca-Cola range (Coca-Cola, light, Diet Coke, zero) and the McDonald's **McSalad** ad.

### Slide 31: Diversification
- **DIVERSIFICATION:** selling **new products to new markets**. This can lead into **entering new countries**.
- One of the main reasons for diversification: **efficiency gains** from applying the organization's existing resources or capabilities to new markets and products or services, that is **economies of scope**.
- Footer examples: **Alphabet, Samsung**. Picture: Apple **iPod** range.

### Slide 32: Market development
- **MARKET DEVELOPMENT:** offering an **existing product in a new market**.
- Three forms: **new segments** (from discount to premium); **new users** (new channel: e-commerce); **new geographies**, the **prime example is internationalization**.
- Pictures: **Starbucks** in China (store and Chinese-language drinks such as Fortune Chestnut Molten Mocha, Ice Cream Plus) and **Kellogg's** Corn Flakes in Chinese packaging: existing products taken to a new country.

### Slide 33: 3. Resource analysis
- Tool: **Resource Based View**.
- Central question: **does the company have resources and capabilities to go international?**
- Sub-questions: what **uniqueness** can we bring into the new markets? Does the company have **sufficient** resources for international markets? If not, will the company be **able to acquire** these in order to execute its international business plan?

### Slide 34: Resource Based View (RBV)
- Firms should **look inside** for the sources of competitive advantage.
- **Resources are the main reason** that explains why companies achieve higher organizational performance.
- Boxed question: **what resources of the company generate competitive advantage?**

### Slide 35: RBV 1st step, identify the firm's resources and capabilities
- **Resources** are the productive assets owned by the firm. Two types:
- **1. Tangible:** financial resources and physical assets. Understand their potential to generate profit.
- **2. Intangible:** trademarks, patents, copyrights, access to information, know-how, organizational culture.
- On their own, **resources are non-productive**. To perform a task, resources must **work together** (that is what a capability is).
- Example: **Disney** has tangible resources (theme parks, hotels, movie studios) and an intangible one: the enduring affection of millions of people across generations for Disney and its characters.
- Table of **capabilities by functional area** (second half of the slide):
- Corporate functions: financial control; strategic management of multiple businesses; strategic innovation; multidivisional coordination; acquisition management; international management.
- Management information: comprehensive integrated network linked to managerial decision making.
- Research and development: research; innovative new product development; fast-cycle new product development.
- Operations: efficiency in volume manufacturing; continuous improvement in manufacturing; flexibility and speed of response.
- Product design: design capability.
- Marketing: brand management; promoting reputation for quality; responsiveness to market trends.
- Sales and distribution: effective sales promotions and execution; efficiency and speed of order processing; speed of distribution; quality and efficiency of customer service.

### Slide 36: RBV 1st step, questions to find resources and capabilities
- Which activities **lower the cost** of production without decreasing perceived customer value?
- Which activities increase **product or service differentiation** and perceived customer value?
- Do you have access to **scarce raw materials** or hard-to-get distribution channels?
- Do you have a **special relationship** with key players, organizations or suppliers in your sector?
- Do you have an **integrated order and distribution platform** powered by unique software that joins stakeholders?
- Do you have **employees** with unique skills and capabilities?
- Do you have **brand** reputation for quality, innovation, customer service?
- Do you perform any tasks **better than your competitors** do? (Benchmarking is useful here.)

### Slide 37: RBV 2nd step, check if they meet critical requirements
- Two types of resources:
- **Tangible assets: can be bought in markets, so little advantage.** Physical things like land, buildings, equipment and capital.
- **Intangible assets: the main source of sustainable competitive advantage.** Brand reputation, trademarks, intellectual property.
- Resources must be:
- **Heterogeneous:** competitive advantage comes from a **different bundle** of resources.
- **Immobile:** resources such as brand image, process knowledge or intellectual property usually **do not move from company to company and cannot be replicated, at least in the short term.**
- The RBV chain diagram: Resource-based view relies on resources (tangible, intangible), that must be heterogeneous and immobile, and have VRIO attributes to become VRIO resources, that provide competitive advantage.

### Slide 38: VRIO framework
- Resources must have **4 attributes** to become a source of **sustainable** competitive advantage: **V**aluable, **R**are, **I**mperfectly imitable, company **O**rganized to capture the value.

### Slide 39: RBV 3rd step, evaluate resources and capabilities (VRIO framework by J. Barney)
- A flowchart of four yes/no questions, each "no" exits to an outcome:
- **Valuable?** Is the resource valuable to exploit opportunities? Does it increase perceived customer value? Does it increase differentiation or decrease the cost of production? **No: competitive disadvantage.**
- **Rare?** Can the resource be acquired by only one or few firms? Is it unique or owned by several firms? **No: competitive parity.**
- **Costly to imitate?** Hard to imitate because: developed over a long period of time; **causal ambiguity** (written "casual ambiguity" on the slide); **social complexity**, based on company culture. **No: temporary competitive advantage.**
- **Organized to capture value?** Resources by themselves do not confer any advantage if the company is not organized to capture the value from them: management systems, processes, policies, organizational structure and culture. **No: temporary competitive advantage.**
- All four yes: **sustained competitive advantage.**

| Valuable | Rare | Costly to imitate | Organized | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No | | | | Competitive disadvantage |
| Yes | No | | | Competitive parity |
| Yes | Yes | No | | Temporary competitive advantage |
| Yes | Yes | Yes | No | Temporary competitive advantage |
| Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes | Sustained competitive advantage |

## What the professor added in class (2 Oct 2026)

### Framing and the three areas
- It sounds nice in a boardroom to say "we want to go global" and start debating when and where, without knowing whether the company is ready. Readiness comes first.
- There are many ways to evaluate readiness; the frameworks in the deck are the ones he uses when running or advising companies, not the only ones. But **three areas** must be covered: what industry am I in and how profitable or captive is it; how am I competing (my strategy); what are my resources and capabilities.
- The concepts may feel like high school material, but companies still skip them.
- **EXAM:** the three analyses are external (industry, Porter Five Forces), internal (how you compete: Porter strategies and growth strategy with Ansoff), and resources and capabilities (RBV, VRIO).

### External analysis
- He adds a sixth angle Porter did not have: **disruption or disruptors**. Include it when analysing the industry.
- Sometimes the analysis concludes the industry is not attractive (too many competitors, too many potential entrants) and the company must **readapt or redirect its strategy at home before** going abroad. Do your homework first.
- The worst thing is to find the benefits and reasons and then jump at the 195 to 200 countries. Maybe you are not ready: not enough skills, not the right people, or simply **not enough funding**.
- Porter video shown in class: you fight for profits not only with direct competitors but with customers and suppliers who have bargaining power, new entrants, and substitutes that cap profitability and growth. Five Forces is a holistic way to see the structural drivers of profitability, what is causing profitability, and which trends will change the game.
- **Define the industry boundaries first.** If you are Iberia, what is "the airline industry" you are analysing? Companies often start the analysis without setting this clearly (this is step 1 of "how to apply the model").
- Student discussion on airlines and India: existing competitors can lobby to block new airports within 100 to 200 km to keep entrants out; government and political barriers shape the number of flights per route. Neira: this is why country choice (Session 7) needs an industry analysis for that specific country.
- Student example, **Southwest Airlines**: low cost plus a differentiated model (short-haul only, no food, first come first served seating, no business class) gives a 20-minute turnaround versus about an hour for others. Neira: they found their niche.
- Customizing the offer (for example menus) is also a way to shape the forces when entering; once you are established, the threat from entry is lower.

### Internal analysis: Porter generic strategies
- Two major ways to compete, or a combination: cost leadership or differentiation, crossed with whole market or a specific segment.
- Cost leadership, whole market: class named **Walmart** (always lower cost, all customers) and **Amazon** (sells to rich, medium and low income alike). The slide uses Amazon.
- Differentiation, whole market: **Apple**. iPhones are expensive, yet the whole market, not only wealthy families, pays the premium.
- Cost focus: **Lidl, Aldi**. You go in expecting the lowest prices; they do not target the medium-to-high-end segment. Contrast in Madrid: **Sánchez Romero** supermarkets (one in Goya), premium brands, wealthy customers, perhaps 20, 30 or 40 percent more for the same goods.
- Differentiation focus: targeting only the rich top of the wealth pyramid with something unique. Cars: **Ferrari, Porsche, Tesla** were named.
- Real companies are often in a grey zone: Apple has very expensive models and cheaper ones; car makers have high-end and small cars. The matrix is a simplification. Start by understanding the industry, then place the company.

### Internal analysis: Ansoff matrix
- The growth strategy question: what is it, and is it **transferable or copyable** into the market you want to enter? Does it make sense there, or do you need to change it?
- Only two levers: change **what** you sell or **to whom** you sell. Companies are often in a grey area between cells.
- **Market penetration:** same products, same customers, grow by presence. Examples: a Kenyan fintech growing from Nairobi into Western, Northern and coastal Kenya (student); supermarkets opening more locations in Madrid, then second-tier cities; fast food chains (**Domino's, McDonald's**) opening more stores.
- **Product development:** new product to the existing customer base. Student offered Apple credit cards and iPods. Neira's examples: **Coke** adding new flavours and types; **McDonald's** adding salads, coffee, donuts: same stores, same customers, new products. Loyalty makes customers try the new product.
- **Diversification:** new product and new market at once; the most complicated cell because two unknowns are being tested at the same time (will the product succeed, and will customers who never bought from you buy it). **Apple** launching new products in countries where it had no presence; **Netflix** entering streaming in new countries where people did not know it (student example, endorsed).
- **Market development:** current portfolio into a new market, the typical cell for international expansion, new geographies. Student example **Uber**: in the US it is private drivers with their own cars; in Spain it was sued and had to operate under the **VTC licence** model, owning cars and paying drivers as contractors. Neira: same product, new market, with adaptation.

### Funding and the ramp-up (student question: do companies expect to lose money at first?)
- **EXAM:** a rigorous plan assumes a **ramp-up period of 6, 8 or 18 months, sometimes a year or two** depending on the product, during which the venture loses money. You must have enough funding and capabilities to hold through it.
- Be realistic; do not lie to yourself or play tricks in the spreadsheet. "On a spreadsheet you can write whatever you want." Spanish saying quoted: reality is what it is. If the plan is not funded, go back and raise money first.
- The temptation when presenting to the board or shareholders is to show profits rising from month one. Resist it.
- His own example: a mine in Spain (name unclear in the recording) restarting once permits arrive; shareholders were told **18 months** before it begins to produce, needing capex (equipment, roads) plus **working capital** for salaries and monthly payments during those 18 months. Shareholders judged it still worth funding.

### Resources and capabilities
- Resources are tangible and intangible; capabilities relate more to people and functional areas (strong technical team, strong marketing team, strong finance team). Use the questions on slide 36 to dig deeper.
- VRIO walked through: valuable? If everybody has the same resource it is not rare, so you are in **competitive parity**. Then costly to imitate?
- **Starbucks** example: the first store in Seattle was successful; the founder could have stopped there but opened more, saw it get more profitable and expanded to thousands of stores worldwide. Store managers send weekly feedback to headquarters; anything unique that works in one store is analysed and disseminated to the others (an organization set up to capture value).
- Debate: what matters more, inside or outside? Conclusion: in most successful cases it is the **skills, resources and capabilities that make the company unique** and give a **sustainable** advantage. The industry helps, but the inside view is what you can play with.
- **EXAM:** a **temporary advantage is "no good."** You need something that is very complicated for competitors to copy in the long term. In the very long run everything can be replicated (someone said even a 30-year-old Japanese company could be copied), but that is a rare situation.
- Closing recap: look at three areas; do the industry analysis with Five Forces or any model; do the internal analysis (how you compete, growth strategy); know your resources and capabilities and whether they give a sustained competitive advantage.

- Know the generic strategies grid and the slide examples: cost leadership Amazon, differentiation Apple, cost focus Lidl, differentiation focus Ferrari; the axes are competitive advantage (costs or differentiation) and market scope (entire or a segment).
- Know Ansoff's two levers (what is sold, to whom) and the four cells: penetration (existing, existing), product development (new product, existing market), market development (existing product, new market), diversification (new, new).
- Know that market penetration is only possible in a growing, unsaturated market, has four objectives, and is the least risky; diversification is the most complex and is justified by economies of scope (Alphabet, Samsung).
- Know that market development has three forms (new segments, new users or channels, new geographies) and that internationalization is its prime example.
- Know RBV: look inside; resources are the main reason for higher performance; tangible assets can be bought so they give little advantage; intangible assets are the main source of sustainable advantage; resources must be heterogeneous and immobile.
- Know VRIO by J. Barney and the outcomes: not valuable = competitive disadvantage; not rare = competitive parity; not costly to imitate = temporary advantage; not organized = temporary advantage; all four = sustained advantage.
- Know the three sources of imitation difficulty: time, causal ambiguity, social complexity (culture), and that organization means systems, processes, policies, structure and culture.
- Know the Disney example (theme parks, hotels, studios plus enduring affection for its characters) and that resources on their own are non-productive and must work together.
- Know Neira's class rule: plan for a 6 to 18 month loss-making ramp-up, fund capex and working capital, and do not trust optimistic spreadsheets.

## Traps and look-alikes
- **Strategy vs goal:** "be number 1" or "grow" is a goal; strategy says how you will compete and be unique.
- **Strategy vs action:** "merge" or "outsource" is one action, not a strategy.
- **Cost advantage vs differentiation advantage:** cost = similar product at lower cost; differentiation = price premium from a unique product. Both can be aimed at the whole market or a segment.
- **Cost leadership vs cost focus:** same cost advantage; leadership targets the entire market (Amazon), focus targets a segment or niche (Lidl).
- **Differentiation vs differentiation focus:** Apple serves the whole market with a unique product; Ferrari serves a niche.
- **Slide examples vs class examples:** the slide says Amazon and Lidl; in class Neira also said Walmart, Aldi, Porsche and Tesla. If a question quotes the deck, answer Amazon, Apple, Lidl, Ferrari.
- **Market development vs diversification:** both are new markets; market development keeps the existing product (internationalization is the prime example), diversification also changes the product and is the riskiest cell.
- **Market penetration vs market development:** penetration = existing product, existing market (more stores in the same market, more usage); development = existing product, new market (new segment, new channel, new country). A student's "new region of Kenya" example was treated as penetration in class because the customer type and country stayed the same; a new country is market development.
- **Product development vs diversification:** both involve a new product; product development sells it to existing customers, leaning on brand trust (Coke flavours, McSalad); diversification sells it to new customers.
- **Economies of scale vs economies of scope:** scale is an entry barrier from spreading large fixed investments over volume (A380); scope is the efficiency reason for diversification, applying existing resources to new products and markets.
- **Entry barrier vs exit barrier:** entry barriers (seven on slide 16) deter new firms; exit barriers (job protection, infrastructure) keep excess capacity in the industry and intensify rivalry.
- **Price sensitivity vs bargaining power:** both are halves of buyer power; sensitivity is about how much the buyer cares about price, bargaining power is about whether the buyer can force a lower price (size, information, integration).
- **Buyer power vs supplier power:** same determinants, mirror image; with suppliers the firm is the buyer.
- **High vs low substitution:** high when products have similar performance and price, the same occasion for use and the same geographic market; cement in Madrid vs Paris is low because the markets differ.
- **Tangible vs intangible resources:** tangible (land, buildings, equipment, capital, financial resources) can be bought, so little advantage; intangible (brand, trademarks, IP, know-how, culture) are the sustainable source.
- **Resources vs capabilities:** resources are assets owned; capabilities are what the firm does with them, organised by functional area; resources alone are non-productive.
- **Heterogeneous vs immobile:** heterogeneous means firms hold different bundles; immobile means the resources cannot move between firms or be replicated in the short term.
- **Competitive parity vs temporary advantage vs sustained advantage (VRIO):** parity = valuable but not rare; temporary = valuable and rare but either imitable or not organized to capture value; sustained = all four attributes.
- **Imperfectly imitable vs costly to imitate:** the same VRIO attribute, worded differently on slides 38 and 39.
- **Five Forces as in the deck vs in class:** the deck has five forces; Neira adds disruption as an extra angle in class, not a sixth force on the slide.
