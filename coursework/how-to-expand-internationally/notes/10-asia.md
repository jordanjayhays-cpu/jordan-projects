# Session 10. The Asian financial crisis (deck + HBS 9-708-001 "Chronology of the Asian Financial Crisis")

This unit uses the 1997-98 Asian crisis as the worked example of how a country defaults and why the standard policy response (raise interest rates, defend a dollar peg, take an IMF loan with austerity conditions) can make a debt crisis worse. The deck (Prof. Mikel Aguirre, 7 Oct 2026) lists the causes, walks through Korea, Thailand, Indonesia and Malaysia under the high-interest-rate "medicine", compares the Asian Tigers of the 1990s with the BRICs, and ends with how sovereign debt "treatments" hit companies. The HBS case is a dated diary from 5 February 1997 (Somprasong Land misses a USD 3.1 million payment) to 25 November 1998 (Camdessus says the worst is over), with five exhibits of exchange rates and stock indices. In class the professor said what matters most is: a company and a country default for the same reason (short-term debt bigger than short-term liquidity); the trigger of the Asian crisis was a loss of confidence after a wave of bad news and non-performing loans; the finance ministries answered with two measures, higher interest rates and dollar pegs, and both backfired; the single lesson is "do not raise interest rates when there is already a huge debt"; and only one country in IMF history has rejected IMF aid, Malaysia in 1997. He flagged the IMF condition of higher interest rates explicitly "for the multiple choice final exam".

### Slide 3. What caused the crisis? (Government)

- **Fixed exchange rates** (currencies pegged to the US dollar).
- **Current account deficits**.
- **Lack of effective regulatory and control measures** over the economy.

### Slide 4. What caused the crisis? (Companies / Market)

- A **numerous financial service sector** (too many banks and finance companies).
- **Dependence on short-term foreign funds**.
- **Currency speculation**.
- **Overpriced assets** (property and shares).

### Slide 5. What caused the crisis? (External reasons)

- **Incapacity of governments to resolve their problems** as a result of political uncertainty.
- **Perception of the region as a unit**, not as individual countries (contagion: a problem in Thailand was read as a problem in all of Asia).
- **Currency speculation** (listed again, now as an external force).
- The **measures for financial institutions that were offered by the IMF** (the conditions attached to IMF money).

### Slide 6. Asian Financial Crisis: high interest rates, an economic medicine?

- Section title. The next five slides test whether raising interest rates cured or worsened the crisis, country by country.

### Slide 7. Korea

- **November 1997: IMF bailout.** The IMF demanded that Korean banks and the government lend at extremely high interest rates. The **short-term interbank rate was raised to 30%**.
- Chain of effects of the high-rate policy:
  - More **corporate bankruptcies**, because companies could not keep up with their debt.
  - Confidence was further ruined.
  - Which accelerated **capital outflows**.
  - Which accelerated the **devaluation of the currency**.
- Result: things got worse during the **first six months** of the IMF "rescue".
- Finally the IMF declared a return to "the Korean way". After the IMF changed its demands (**lowering the interest rates**), Korea recovered dramatically.

### Slide 8. Thailand

- The interest rate hike was **starving local businesses of cash** and **strangling economic growth**.
- For companies already hit by the declines in the currency and in share values, the rate hike became a **third burden "that broke their backs"**.
- The **Thai stock market dropped 75%**.
- Huge numbers of workers returned to their villages in the countryside, and **600,000 foreign workers were sent back** to their home countries.

### Slide 9. Indonesia

- High interest rates made it difficult for Indonesian businesses to pay their debts; many failed, which further decreased investor confidence in the economy and the currency.
- Further depreciation of the currency caused the country's (dollar) debt to keep rising, so more defaults.
- Inflation caused widespread unrest and political turmoil: riots and violence. **Suharto out**.
- Indonesia's recovery was **extremely slow**.

### Slide 10. Malaysia

- Despite being badly hit, Malaysia was the **only Asian country that rejected the IMF bailout**.
- **Bank Negara Malaysia** (the central bank) required commercial banks and finance companies to **peg their deposit interest rates (maturities up to 12 months) to the deposit rate of two leading domestic banks**.
- Malaysia was **less affected** by the crisis than Thailand, Indonesia or the Philippines.
- Speaker note (same note repeated on slide 11): the main problem was unhealthy domestic economies (bad private debt) and low confidence; people sold the currency; central banks tried to bail out their currencies but ran out of money; they needed IMF money, but the conditions the IMF imposed (including raising interest rates) made the situation worse.

### Slide 11. General overview

- The IMF advised countries to **increase interest rates** to attract investment and instil confidence.
- The money that came was **short-term, speculative, "hot" money**: money growth with no growth in export production.
- After the IMF bailout policies, the devalued currency encouraged **private-sector borrowing** and made the private sector more vulnerable to the outside world.
- Because of distrust of Asian currencies, the **outflow of money could not be prevented**.
- The **debt of the private sector became bigger and bigger**.
- High interest rate policies and **restricting government expenditure** deepened the recession.
- Conclusion: the high interest rates did not fix the crisis but made it worse, and in the end the **IMF changed its policies** towards the Asian countries.
- Speaker note: same as slide 10.

### Slide 16. Growth model

- Asian Tigers: growth model started with **import substitution**.
- But the aim was an **outward-looking export model** after industrialisation.

### Slide 17. Global position

- The Four Asian Tigers managed their massive growth mainly through **export-driven economies** that catered to richer, industrialised nations, and focused on keeping a **trade surplus** with those partners.
- They rose to global prominence as **NICs (newly industrialised countries) in the 1970s and 1980s**.
- The BRICs are the key emerging economies expected to **lead global growth in the next 10 years**.

### Slide 19. Type of regime

- Asian Tigers: Hong Kong, a British colony until 1997; Singapore, a republic; South Korea, a liberal democracy; Taiwan, moving from an authoritarian regime towards democracy.
- BRICs: Brazil, a democratic republic; Russia, a democratic federation (considered "not free" by Freedom House); India, the largest democracy; China, a communist "People's Republic".

### Slide 20. Interdependence with the US

- Asian Tigers: Western countries needed to transfer their labour-intensive industries; the Tigers are relatively small countries, so they have to **depend on the US**.
- BRICs: big countries, but with a large amount of trade with the US (especially China); they **challenge the US at the same time**; they also host labour-intensive industries.

### Slide 21. Impact of the (2008) crisis: BRICs

- BRICs' contribution to the global economy since 2007: **45%**.
- However, they are **too heterogeneous** to make any generalised comment.
- FDI outflow limited in China.
- Brazil, Russia, India: stock markets fell. Became fine from 2009.
- Decline in short-term credit.
- Currency appreciation (except China).

### Slide 22. Impact of the (2008) crisis: Asian Tigers

- Singapore and Taiwan: relatively unscathed.
- South Korea: high levels of **NPLs** (non-performing loans) and a stock market crash.
- Hong Kong: intense **speculative attacks** against its stock market and currency.

### Slide 23. Any sovereign debt "treatment" is likely to impact companies

- Three models of how a government handles its debt problem, and the knock-on for companies:
  - **Argentina-Russia treatment: unilateral default.** Effect on companies: credit crunch + increasing financing costs + recession shock.
  - **Turkey-Brazil treatment: long-lasting deep fiscal adjustment.** Effect: late payments by the public sector + anaemic growth.
  - **India-Lebanon treatment: banks are "encouraged" to keep (government) debt exposure.** Effect: crowding-out (banks hold sovereign paper instead of lending to companies).

## Part 2. The case: Chronology of the Asian Financial Crisis (HBS 9-708-001)

### Front matter and introduction

- HBS case 9-708-001, rev. 23 April 2009, by Laura Alfaro, Rafael Di Tella and research associate Renee Kim. Compiled from published sources; covers the beginning of 1997 to the fall of 1998.
- In the 1990s the **"Asian miracle"** was the model of successful growth: Korea, Malaysia, Thailand and others attracted investors and capital from around the world.
- Developing Asia's **average real GDP growth was above 9% during 1992-95**, "probably unparalleled at any time by any group of economies of comparable size" (endnote 1: Martin Feldstein, "Refocusing the IMF", Foreign Affairs, March/April 1998).
- In **July 1997 Thailand became the first Asian "tiger" to abandon its fixed exchange rate** in response to speculative attacks; no one expected a **domino-like effect** across the region.
- Investors fled at a record pace, forcing central banks to spend billions in failed defences of their currencies and then to seek **IMF bailouts**.
- **Jeffrey Sachs** (Columbia): "In a matter of a few months, the Asian economies went from being the darlings of the investment community to being virtual pariahs." (endnote 2: Sachs, "The Wrong Medicine for Asia", New York Times, 3 Nov 1997)

### 1997, February to June: Thailand's warning signs

- **February 5.** **Somprasong Land Plc**, a Thailand-based property developer, misses a **USD 3.1 million interest payment** on its foreign debt, the **first Thai firm to do so**.
- **March 3.** To boost confidence, the **Bank of Thailand** orders financial institutions to raise provisions for substandard loans and cash-strapped companies to boost their capital standards. Trading of banking and financial shares is suspended that day; shares tumble the next day (Exhibit 1).
- **March 11.** Thailand's government proposes a **100 billion baht bailout** for troubled finance companies' bad debts from the weak property sector. IMF managing director **Michel Camdessus** praises the move: "They are doing exactly what you must do to avoid recurrence of a Mexico-like crisis... I don't see any reason for this crisis to develop further." (The crisis then developed.)
- **May 14.** The Bank of Thailand adopts **selective capital controls** to defend the baht: local banks ordered not to sell baht to foreigners in the swap market, and short-sellers denied access to the domestic market.
  - Headlines: "Thais Win Currency Battle, What About the War?" (Reuters); "Thai Currency Tactics Calm SE Asian Markets" (Financial Times).
  - **Amnuay Viravan**, Thailand's finance minister: "We need more established measures to deal with any future attacks on the baht, something like what they have in Singapore which stops its people from speculating in its own currency. We have a free and open market but we do not open our market to let people bully us."
- **June 19.** Amnuay Viravan, "a proponent of defending the currency at all costs", **resigns**. Replaced by **Thanong Bidaya**, head of the Thai Military Bank. A few days later **16 finance firms** are ordered to shut down or consolidate, including the prestigious **Finance One**.

### 1997, July to September: the pegs break

- **July 2.** Thailand lets the **baht float freely for the first time since 1984**, after speculative attacks forced the central bank to spend billions of its reserves defending it. The baht **immediately falls 15%**.
  - **Mark Mobius**, president of Templeton Emerging Market Fund: "We felt it was time for them to devalue and face the reality of what their currency was really worth... now that they've done it, it helps, I think, to clear the deck."
  - The Herald (Glasgow): "The bank finally did the right thing when it had its back to the wall."
  - Reuters: "The risk is you will see some similar weaknesses of the regional currencies such as the (Indonesian) rupiah and the (Malaysian) ringgit and the peso."
- **July 14.** **Malaysia** joins Thailand and abandons its peg, letting the **ringgit float**.
- **July 15.** **Kia Motors**, Korea's third-largest car maker, is placed under bankruptcy protection. It is the **fourth of Korea's top 30 conglomerates** to go under or narrowly avoid bankruptcy that year, starting with **Hanbo Steel** (collapsed in January under **USD 6 billion** of debt).
- **July 20.** The IMF agrees to give the **Philippines USD 1 billion** in emergency loans after it fails to fend off speculative attacks on the peso.
- **July 24.** Malaysia's prime minister **Mahathir Mohamad** accuses **George Soros**, American billionaire financier, of causing the ringgit's depreciation, as speculative trading drags the ringgit to a **38-month low** against the dollar (Exhibit 2).
- **August 11.** The IMF and other Asian countries approve a **USD 16 billion loan package for Thailand** in exchange for a tough reform plan: major restructuring of the financial sector and a tighter fiscal budget. (Endnote 10: a few days later **China pledges USD 1 billion**, bringing the Thai package to **USD 17 billion**.)
- **August 14.** **Indonesia** lets the **rupiah float**, abandoning its managed trading band, after unsuccessfully spending **USD 1 billion** to defend it (Exhibit 3).
- **September 20.** At the **IMF/World Bank annual conference in Hong Kong**, Mahathir declares that "currency trading is unnecessary, unproductive, and immoral. It should be stopped." Soros then brands him "a menace to his own country."
  - Headlines: "Mahathir comments rattle regional markets" (Agence France-Presse); "Damage Control, Can Mahathir Get Malaysia Back on Course?" (BusinessWeek).
  - **Anwar Ibrahim**, Malaysia's deputy prime minister: "I think Soros's remarks are incorrect because the prime minister's criticism against currency speculation and abuse of freedom in the currency trading does not mean that Dr. Mahathir is totally against currency trading."
  - **Robert Rubin**, US Treasury Secretary: "Currency trading is an inherent and very important function in a modern and global economy, and it is integral to global trade."

### 1997, October to November: contagion to Indonesia, Hong Kong, Japan and Korea

- **October 8.** **Indonesia seeks IMF and World Bank help** after the rupiah falls **more than 30% in two months**, despite central bank interventions.
- **October 17.** **Taiwan floats the new Taiwan dollar**.
- **October 17.** Malaysia announces tough fiscal tightening: reducing the current account deficit and **cutting public spending by 2%** (endnote 17 points to the companion case "Malaysia: Capital and Control", HBS 702-040).
- **October 23-28.** The **Hong Kong stock market loses 25%** of its value amid fears over interest rates and pressure on the Hong Kong dollar, dragging other Asian markets and the **Dow Jones** down. The **Korean won begins to tumble**.
- **October 24.** Malaysia's deputy prime minister says Malaysia **will not seek IMF help**, nor foresees needing it.
- **October 31.** The IMF announces a loan package for **Indonesia of USD 23 billion**, which eventually reaches **USD 40 billion** through pledges from other countries. In return Indonesia **closes 16 insolvent banks** and promises other reforms.
  - Headlines: "Reform, Not Aid, Seen Key in Indonesia IMF Package" and "Asia Stocks Rally on Indonesia's IMF Package" (Reuters).
  - Financial Times: "The IMF plan may, of course, itself weaken further Mr. Suharto's political position. But if the U.S. administration and the IMF are to avert their worst fears of financial and political instability in Asia, they know that more governments must be prepared to take some painful medicine."
- **November 3.** Japan's **Sanyo Securities**, a top-10 brokerage, files for bankruptcy, the **first Japanese securities company to do so since World War II**.
- **November 19.** Korea's finance minister **Kang Kyong-shik** is dismissed, blamed for accelerating the financial problems. New minister **Lim Chang-Ryul** says "There is no need for financial help from the IMF" and announces measures such as **opening the bond market to foreigners**.
- **November 21.** Two days later Korea "swallows its pride" and announces it will seek an **IMF bailout worth USD 20 billion**, as the stock market keeps plunging and the central bank cannot stop the won's slide (Exhibit 4).
  - Reuters: "Southeast Asia is pretty much a tea party compared to Korea."
  - Philadelphia Inquirer: "Investors are urged to Remain Calm despite Financial Crisis in Asia. Pacific Rim Leaders Say the Turmoil can be Contained. The Issue Tops the Agenda at the APEC Summit."
  - The Economist: "If Asia's governments fail to cure their sick banks, their economies' crisis, and its implications for the rest of the world, could grow much worse."
- **November 24.** **Yamaichi Securities**, another top-10 Japanese brokerage, collapses.

### 1997, December: Korea's rescue and the year-end panic

- **December 3.** Korea, the **world's 11th largest economy**, announces a **USD 55 billion bailout package led by the IMF** in exchange for a tough restructuring programme. Concerns over Korea spill over, dragging the **Thai baht to a record low**. The previous day the **Malaysian ringgit fell to its lowest level since 1973**.
- **December 5.** Malaysia's deputy prime minister **Anwar Ibrahim** announces a severe austerity programme. The 1998 growth forecast is cut to **4%-5% from 7%**. Measures: (1) slash federal expenditure by **at least 18%**; (2) restore foreign currency reserves to at least **four months of retained imports**; (3) cut the current account deficit to **3% of GNP from the previous 4% target**; (4) **freeze bailout loans** for failing corporations; (5) tighter credit policies. The **Kuala Lumpur Stock Exchange surges more than 5%**.
- **December 8.** In an unprecedented move, Thailand permanently shuts **56 of its 58 ailing finance companies**, welcomed by the IMF and investors.
- **December 16.** Korea removes the **daily foreign exchange band of plus or minus 10%**. BusinessWeek's editorial urges "Dear IMF: Don't Make Asia's Flu Worse", arguing that "in a deflationary economic environment... typical IMF policy prescriptions can do more harm than good."
- **December 17.** Japan announces a surprise **2 trillion yen tax cut**; markets react positively.
- **December 22.** **Moody's downgrades Indonesia, Korea and Thailand to junk-bond status**. Malaysia's rating is cut too but **stays investment grade**. Korean and Malaysian markets tumble; the **Jakarta exchange rises 1.9%** because the downgrade was expected.
- **December 23.** The **World Bank announces USD 3 billion** in emergency loans to Korea, bringing the Korean package to **USD 58 billion**.
  - **Kim Dae Jung**, newly elected president of Korea, the first from an opposition party: "We don't know whether we'll default tomorrow or the day after tomorrow... Our warehouse is empty. It's a matter of surviving a day, not a month. I can't believe how the government has been so negligent."
- **December 23.** Chinese premier **Zhu Rongji** says there will be **no devaluation of the yuan** despite the turmoil (endnote 29: the last major revaluation of the yuan was in 1994).
- **December 24.** The IMF and other economies agree to **speed up disbursement of USD 10 billion** to Korea, since the initial announcement failed to calm the market or stop the won's fall.
- **December 29.** The rupiah suffers as reports put Indonesia's total foreign debt at around **USD 200 billion**; the government says it is closer to **USD 130 billion**.
- **December 30.** Major international banks agree to **roll over Korea's short-term debt**.

### 1998, January to February: Indonesia's panic, Korea's rollover, Thailand's renegotiation

- (The case prints January 8 before January 5.)
- **January 8.** The ringgit hits a **new all-time low** after a **Bank for International Settlements** report that Malaysia's **short-term debt reached 56% of total borrowings from foreign lenders**, far above earlier estimates of **30%**, plus concerns over other regional currencies.
- **January 5.** Thai prime minister **Chuan Leekpai** says Thailand will **renegotiate** its IMF conditions; a **budget surplus of 1% of 1998 GDP** cannot be met.
- **January 12.** **Peregrine Investments Holdings**, Asia's largest private investment bank (Hong Kong), files for liquidation, citing exposure to loans in Indonesia. The **Hang Seng plunges 774 points, or 8.7%**.
- **January 15.** **Suharto signs a new IMF agreement** that eliminates Indonesia's monopolies and state subsidies, amid doubts about his commitment. It does little to restore confidence. Wall Street Journal: "Indonesia Reaches IMF Pact to Speed Reforms, Tougher Accord Expected to Curb Suharto System of 'Crony Capitalism'."
- **January 23.** The **rupiah plunges for five consecutive days, losing more than a third of its value**. Trigger: indications that Suharto could pick **B.J. Habibie** as vice-president (and possible successor). Habibie is known for unorthodox economic views and large-scale capital-intensive industrial projects (aerospace), and lacks the army's support.
  - Headlines: "Indonesia Faces Debt, Currency Nightmare" (Reuters); "Asian Crisis Worsens as Indonesia Panics" (Evening Standard); "Soaring Ambition: B.J. Habibie, best known for his bold aerospace plans, may be set to take the co-pilot seat as Indonesia's vice-president; Investors are taking flight" (Far Eastern Economic Review).
- **January 28.** Korea agrees with its global creditors to roll over **USD 24 billion of short-term debt into government-guaranteed loans**. The New York Times, in hindsight: "Banks were slow to see warning signs in Asia", having stepped up lending even as trouble emerged.
- **January 30.** Thailand removes the **two-tier currency trading system** in place since May 1997 (which had restricted foreigners' holdings of baht), making the baht truly free-floating.
- **February 17.** Suharto fires **Soedradjad Djiwandono**, the independent-minded central bank governor and key IMF negotiator, over a disagreement about a **currency board** and other reforms. Most Asian currencies close slightly higher on speculation that Indonesia may not adopt the board.
- **February 18.** **Standard & Poor's raises Korea's rating** (still junk), removes it from CreditWatch and upgrades its outlook to "stable", citing reform efforts.
- **February 20.** Japan's ruling party announces its **fourth economic package since October**; the yen slips. The package is criticised for lacking bigger tax cuts or public spending. Other **G-7 leaders urge Japan** to speed up stimulus (Exhibit 5), complaining that the world's second-biggest economy is undermining the international effort by failing to stimulate itself and clean up its banks.
  - New York Times: "U.S. Sees New Villain in Asia Crisis: Japan's Government." Fortune: "The Risks Are Rising: Unless Political Leaders Move Fast, the Current Crisis Will Worsen and Shock World Markets Even Harder. The Key: a Bold Plan to Restore Confidence in the Region's Shaky Banking System."
- **February 23.** Indonesia **backs down from the currency board** plan under pressure from the IMF and world leaders, who argued it was unsustainable given insufficient reserves. Camdessus had written to Suharto in early February saying the plan would make the **IMF withdraw funding**.
- **February 24.** The IMF **eases Thailand's conditions**: a **budget deficit of 2% of GDP** allowed (for 1997, as printed) and **interest rates reduced**, a "tacit acknowledgement" that the economy was worse than predicted. The baht appreciates; the stock market closes down **4.76 points to 517.06** on news the economy could **contract by as much as 3.5%** that year.

### 1998, March to May: Indonesia unravels, Korea stabilises

- **March 10.** Suharto is **re-elected to a seventh five-year term**.
- **March 19.** Zhu Rongji reiterates **no yuan devaluation** and a commitment to defend the **Hong Kong currency board** at all costs: "...it will have a bearing not only on the development of China but also on the prosperity and stability of Asia."
- **March 23.** Indonesia's central bank raises key interest rates, **one as high as 45%**, and abandons a proposed **5% tax on foreign currency purchases**. The IMF praises it as part of the strategy to control inflation and strengthen the rupiah.
- **March 31.** Korea signs an agreement with foreign creditors to roll over **97%, or USD 22 billion**, of its short-term debt, removing a major threat to its banks' solvency.
- **April 1.** Japan unveils its **"Big Bang" deregulation** of financial markets: most restraints on FX trading lifted; fixed commissions on large equity transactions deregulated.
- **April 8.** Indonesia signs its **third letter of intent** with the IMF since the crisis began: a government-backed plan to restructure **USD 68 billion of foreign corporate debt**, faster banking reform, and **maintaining high interest rates**.
- **May 6.** **Riots and looting in Medan** (northern Indonesia) as price increases, including a **fuel price hike of up to 71%**, take effect under the IMF agreement.
- **May 13-14.** Riots spread through **Jakarta**, reportedly **over a thousand injuries and deaths in two days**. Armed troops with tanks patrol; Suharto faces calls to resign.
- **May 21.** **Suharto steps down after 32 years** and names vice-president **B.J. Habibie** his successor.
- **May 27.** Korea's unions launch a **two-day nationwide strike** over unemployment and layoffs; **unemployment 6.7% versus 2.6% a year earlier**, with about **8,000 people losing their jobs daily**.
- **May 29.** Habibie agrees to **hold elections in 1999** after a political overhaul.

### 1998, June to July: Japan in recession, Malaysia turns against the IMF

- **June 13.** Japan officially acknowledges its **first recession in 23 years**: the economy contracted **0.7%** in the fiscal year (ending "March 3" as printed). Fears for Korea's recovery send Japan's leading index down **8.1%, its biggest one-day fall ever**; the **Nikkei 225 falls below 15,000** for the first time since 13 January, and the **dollar soars to an eight-year high above 145 yen**.
  - Washington Times: "Wave of Recession in Asia Washes over Japan's Economy, Downturn Steeper than Analysts Feared." Dow Jones: "Japan's Inaction on Yen Indicates It Has Run out of Policy Options."
- **June 17.** The **US Treasury and the Federal Reserve intervene** to prop up the yen, spending about **USD 2 billion** buying yen, amid concerns over Japanese banks' bad loans. **President Clinton** and other leaders urge Japan to fix its banks.
- **June 24.** Mahathir appoints **Daim Zainuddin** (former finance minister and loyal ally) to the Cabinet with special authority over economic recovery, seen as undermining finance minister **Anwar Ibrahim**, who supports high interest rates; Mahathir and Daim favour easing. Ringgit and stocks slip. The next day Mahathir attacks the IMF prescription of high rates: **"We thought the IMF stance was good, so we adopted that. But we found out it has been very damaging to our economy."**
- **June 25.** Indonesia and the IMF announce their **fourth agreement**: up to **USD 6 billion extra funding**, and Indonesia is allowed to **keep fuel and food subsidies** after the May riots.
- **July 23.** Malaysia unveils a national economic recovery plan (stabilise the ringgit, restore confidence, financial stability), including **temporarily lifting limits on foreign equity ownership in new manufacturing projects until the end of December 1999**. Markets shrug: the ringgit is unchanged and the stock index slips **1.5%**.
- **July 28.** The IMF **eases monetary and fiscal austerity for Korea** as the economy sinks into its deepest recession since 1980: Korea may **lower interest rates** and run a **budget deficit of 4% of GDP**. The IMF revises its forecast to a **GDP decline of 4% in 1998**, versus **1% forecast three months earlier**.

### 1998, August to November: global spillover and the end of the acute phase

- **August 4.** The **Dow Jones plunges nearly 300 points**, its third-biggest point loss, on Asia's weak recovery and weaker corporate earnings.
- **August 11.** The yen slumps to **another eight-year low**, hitting regional stock indices and raising fears of **another round of Asian devaluations**.

## Part 3. What the professor added in class

### Opening: ICISA and trade credit insurance

- **ICISA = International Credit Insurance and Surety Association.** Credit insurance insures the seller against the risk that the debtor (buyer, borrower) does not pay. The relevant line for this course is **trade credit insurance**.
- ICISA's website lists members by region: Africa, Asia, Europe, Global, Latin America, Middle East, North America. Members include **Allianz** (German), **Atradius**, **AXA**, Axis, and the company the professor works with (name unclear on the recording, sounds like "Microfast"); the list is alphabetical.
- What these companies do: they **analyse the credit of any corporate and insure the potential non-payment of the debtor, or the guarantee**. They exist worldwide; if a debtor does not pay, you collect from the insurer. He asked the class to look at the ICISA webpage.

### Why a company defaults (the warm-up before countries)

- News that morning: the airline **Volotea** had defaulted. His consumer example: you buy a EUR 40 ticket to London; the CFO cancels the flight because it would lose money; you lose not just the EUR 40 but the GBP 300 hotel and the GBP 300 concert tickets you had booked. Companies default every day in India, Germany, the US, everywhere.
- **Reputation**: about **70% of a company's value in the 21st century is its image**. A defaulter is "non-reliable"; if you close and reopen under a new name, suppliers still know you are the same person.
- A student said "cash flow". The professor's precise definition: a company defaults when **short-term liabilities are bigger than short-term assets**, which is **negative working capital**: you cannot pay your short-term debts with your short-term liquidity. The **tenor** matters. It is not about the size of the debt (he has a 30-year mortgage on a Madrid apartment and is not in default); it is about **debt that is due in the short term versus liquidity**.
- What can the CFO do?
  - **Refinance short-term liabilities into long-term liabilities.** But put yourself in the bank's shoes (he played "an Indian bank"): if you cannot repay what is due in 2026, why give you another year? The bank will **charge more** and will ask for **collateral / guarantees**: machinery, buildings, the brand. Many times, when companies default, the banks end up taking over the company through the collateral. **Banks do not like risk, and now less than ever**; they want to lend money, not take risk.
  - **Increase capital.** But if the shareholders refuse to put in EUR 1,000 more because "this is going down", the bank will not trust the company either. If a company has already defaulted it is because shareholders do not want to put in more money and banks will not lend.
  - **Sell long-term assets.** Example: **BBVA** (second-biggest Spanish bank, owner of **Garanti**, the biggest bank in Turkey) has a huge building at **Nuevos Ministerios** in central Madrid, empty for 10 years, "worth" EUR 12 to 15 million. That value exists only on paper until it is sold. Warning: when everyone says real estate is booming (US, Saudi Arabia, China today), you are in the middle of a bubble.
- **The haircut.** Unique to default: instead of the debt growing (as in gangster films), the defaulter asks creditors to **forgive part of the debt**. This is called **the loss**, and it is usually **10 to 15% of the borrower's total liabilities**. Example: you are owed USD 10,000, you forgive 15% and receive USD 8,500. Creditors accept because the alternative is **liquidation**.
- **Order of collection in a liquidation:**
  1. **The government** (taxes and social security), always first. Reason: taxes are the first thing a company stops paying, because nothing happens for 2 to 5 months, whereas unpaid electricity or water gets cut off. Practical tip: a company that is not paying its taxes or its employee-related taxes has a liquidity problem.
  2. **The banks.**
  3. Varies by country, but **usually the employees**.
  - **Suppliers** come last; by the time a supplier arrives to collect its USD 10,000 there is nothing left.
- Companies disappear; **countries do not disappear**, but countries default for exactly the same reason: **short-term debt bigger than liquidity**. Individuals can default too (Michael Jackson defaulted before he died). How do you create an economic crisis today? **Lenders decide the risk is too high** and stop lending; living above your means is only possible because someone lends to you.

### The 2026 backdrop he kept returning to

- The **US is paying more than 5% on the 10-year bond**; everyone is talking about yields on Japanese, US and French debt, which are supposed to be risk-free. He thinks a European country or the US defaulting is very unlikely, but **China and Japan, the biggest holders of US debt, are selling it for the first time in 20 years**.
- **Interest rates were near zero from 2008 to 2022** (16 years): Mario Draghi's "whatever it takes", quantitative easing, plenty of liquidity. Today 4 to 5% feels like a huge danger. Rates are rising again at the same time as there is a huge debt: "we are replicating the situation of 1997 on a global scale, not just in Asia."
- **About 80% of loans and credits worldwide (personal and corporate) are at variable interest rates**, because they are long term, so rate rises hit existing debt. Fixed versus variable is a bet on your rate expectations. Swapping variable to fixed is possible, but the bank will ask for collateral (your home).
- Governments are "kicking the ball": the best fix is to **reduce government expenditure**, but instead they raise rates to attract money and raise taxes, shifting the problem to future generations.
- **AI as the current bubble**: he analyses AI deals daily; **about 60% of AI investment is financed with debt, in US dollars**, while the dollar appreciates and rates rise. Bank syndications are lending to AI infrastructure. The open question is **monetisation**: a PDF e-book can be sold to a million people at zero marginal cost, but every ChatGPT answer is a personal, expensive computation, so subscriptions (he said "USD 20") will get much more expensive and advertising will come. Difference from the 2000 dot-com bubble: back then company values were almost nothing; today Nvidia and the hyperscalers are worth billions. Still, "there is a bubble there."
- A student noted rules being changed (S&P 500 inclusion rules, the SpaceX IPO lock-up for pension funds); he agreed the system bends to accommodate the situation, and that without the financial system there are no international operations.
- **Bubbles are visible in advance**: Newsweek was writing about subprime in June 2006, 15 months before Lehman (September 2008). In a bubble nobody wants personal responsibility; it is like being in the middle of a party.
- Student example, **Turkey**: with no trust in the lira, Turkey introduced **exchange-rate-protected deposits** (you get the dollar appreciation if the lira falls, or the interest rate if it does not): zero risk for savers, so everyone parked money in banks, **investment stopped** and capital owners profited. Professor: in finance, if someone wins, someone loses. Turkey pays about 40% today.

### The Asian crisis story as he told it

- **1992-96**: the **OECD** economies (the highest income per capita group) were struggling and not growing. He attributed the "Asian Tigers" label to **Jim O'Neill of Goldman Sachs** (O'Neill is in fact known for coining "BRICs"; this is the professor's attribution). Catchy acronyms sell: PIGS in 2012 (**Portugal, Italy, Ireland, Spain**), BRICS.
- **Billions of dollars were allocated to the Asian Tigers**, and foreign money is parked in the local **banks** (more liquid than bonds). Banks do not keep money; **about 97% of deposits are lent out**, to **consumers, corporates and the state**. Result: plenty of liquidity, a loan for a car becomes a loan for an apartment, governments spend. A bubble is a party; nobody asks when it ends (compare 2001-2007).
- **The trigger.** Students offered the dollar peg, over-liquidity in unused buildings, panic and capital flight, the Angola comparison. His corrections:
  - The peg was **a measure the finance ministries took later**, not the trigger. (He drew the "original sin" link: developing economies borrow in hard currency, so a strong dollar and rising US rates inflate their debt, in 1997 and in 2026.)
  - Capital flight was **the consequence after the crisis exploded**, not the trigger.
  - The trigger is the first line of the case: **Somprasong Land Plc**, a property developer, missed a **USD 3.1 million** payment. That is an **NPL, a non-performing loan**. Then came many NPLs and defaults in Thailand in **February 1997**: huge short-term debt, no liquidity (buildings and companies bought with borrowed money). Plenty of liquidity lets you invest wisely or stupidly; the debt remains either way.
  - **EXAM:** the trigger of the Asian crisis was **a lack of confidence**: a flow of bad news (defaults, NPLs) made foreign investors withdraw their money, and because banks hold only about 3% of deposits, withdrawals create a liquidity collapse.
- **The two measures** the ministries of finance implemented to restore confidence:
  1. **Increase interest rates.** The oldest medicine to attract investment; the US does it today with the 5% 10-year bond. But if it were only about rates, everyone would invest in Turkey at 40%.
  2. **Peg the local currency to the US dollar.** His Basque aside: "bat" means one in Basque, so "1 baht = 1 dollar". A peg looks to investors like investing in the US.
- **Why pegs fail.** A peg promises to convert any amount of riyal, lira or baht into dollars, so you need **huge dollar reserves, and only the Federal Reserve prints dollars**. Saudi Arabia (oil), Panama and Ecuador (dollarised, oil) can sustain it; most cannot. **"All the countries that pegged against the dollar, on average it has been a complete disaster."** If Spain pegged to the yen it would need billions of yen it cannot print.
- **What citizens do under a distrusted peg** (he lived Argentina 2001 from Brazil): convert savings to dollars, then **move the dollars offshore** (US bonds). When the peg is finally dropped and the currency floats with no demand, it collapses, the dollar debt balloons, and the country defaults. "As simple as that."
- **Why high rates fail with high debt.** Raising rates when households and companies are already heavily indebted makes the debt worse: people lose the car and the house, companies lay off workers, the unemployed cannot repay, debt rises further. It is a vicious circle: governments need money, raise rates to attract it, and make debt unpayable. Before raising rates you must know **the real debt of your citizens and corporates**.
- **EXAM:** the one-line lesson of the Asian crisis: **"you don't have to increase interest rates when there is a huge debt."** That lesson explains 2008-2022's zero rates. Today rates rise with huge debt, which is why the case matters.
- His framing of the outcome: **"all the Asian Tigers defaulted"** 29 years ago (the case itself describes bailouts and debt rollovers, not formal sovereign defaults; use his framing for his questions).

### IMF and World Bank

- When a company defaults it refinances with its banks; when a **country** defaults, the two entities that refinance it are the **IMF and the World Bank**.
- **IMF money is a loan, not a donation.** Countries go to the IMF because they have to, not because they want to. If you do not want conditions, do not ask for a loan. Spain did not default in 2012 because it is in the EU.
- The **IMF was founded in 1944**. **EXAM:** in its whole history there has been **only one case of a country rejecting IMF aid: Malaysia in 1997**.
- **EXAM:** the measure the IMF demanded from the Asian Tigers was to **increase interest rates** (plus austerity). "It's in the case, believe me. I'm saying this just for the multiple choice final exam." The ministers' objection: we are in this situation because we raised rates, and you tell us to raise them again.
- **Why Malaysia could say no**: Malaysia **has plenty of oil**; with oil prices rising it had liquidity to repay debt, so it chose to **reduce government expenditure** instead of raising rates (a student added capital controls). Analogy: you can quit smoking or lose weight alone, but most people need help; most defaulting countries need the IMF.
- **His defence of the IMF and World Bank**: their loans carry **much lower interest rates than any private bank** (Deutsche Bank, Santander, Pichincha, ICBC). They are disliked because they demand **cuts in government expenditure** (infrastructure, hospitals, education, security) and **higher taxes**, which hit daily life.

### Asian Tigers versus BRICS, and whether 1997 can repeat

- In 1997 people saw the Asian Tigers (six or seven countries) as **a single country**; the deck's slide 5 point about the region perceived as a unit.
- **BRICS** was Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa; now **BRICS+ has 11 countries, including Saudi Arabia**. (At his company it is forbidden to call Hong Kong a country; it is part of China.)
- **World Bank threshold: above USD 10,000 GNI per capita = medium-high income country.** The Tigers were there in 1993-95; most BRICs were not in 2008-11.
- India's per capita income: about **USD 1,400 in 2011, about USD 4,500 today**, multiplied by 4 (he said "in the last 19 years"). Growing 7 to 10% a year from a USD 1,000 to 3,000 base is normal; a USD 30,000 to 50,000 economy (France, Canada, Germany, Japan) cannot grow 10%. **EXAM-style rule of thumb:** if a rich country like Canada or Saudi Arabia is growing 6 to 7% a year, you are in a bubble (real estate, financial or AI), and all bubbles burst.
- Sector concentration: the Tigers' business was **IT and financial services**. If your main sector is finance and a financial crisis hits, the crisis is **multiplied by 3 or 4**. The BRICS are diversified (minerals, cars, weapons, services, technology; South Africa agro-industry and financial services; Saudi Arabia diversified). The Russia boycott since 2022 has not affected China, India or Brazil (Brazil was holding presidential elections that week).
- His conclusion: **a simultaneous default of a whole group of countries, as in 1997, is much less likely in 2027**; the risk is a liquidity problem in **one specific country**. His firm does not worry about Africa as a continent, only about individual African countries. Investors must still accept that countries can default.

## Traps and look-alikes

- **Trigger versus measure versus consequence.** The dollar peg was a *measure* taken by finance ministries; capital flight was a *consequence*; the *trigger* was loss of confidence after NPLs and defaults (Somprasong Land). The professor corrected students on exactly this.
- **IMF versus World Bank amounts for Korea.** IMF-led package USD 55 billion (3 Dec); the World Bank's USD 3 billion on 23 Dec took the total to USD 58 billion; the USD 10 billion on 24 Dec was a faster disbursement, not new money; Korea initially asked for USD 20 billion.
- **Indonesia USD 23 billion versus USD 40 billion.** USD 23 billion was the IMF announcement; USD 40 billion the eventual total with other countries' pledges.
- **Thailand USD 16 billion versus USD 17 billion.** USD 16 billion from the IMF and Asian countries; USD 17 billion once China added USD 1 billion (endnote).

- **30% versus 45%.** Korea's short-term interbank rate reached 30% (deck); Indonesia raised a key rate as high as 45% (case, 23 Mar 1998).

- **Anwar Ibrahim versus Daim Zainuddin.** Anwar (deputy PM and finance minister) favoured high rates and free-market principles, announced the December 1997 austerity plan, and was fired on 2 Sept 1998; Daim was Mahathir's ally appointed 24 June 1998 to favour easing.
- **Amnuay Viravan versus Thanong Bidaya versus Chuan Leekpai.** Amnuay: Thai finance minister who defended the baht at all costs and resigned 19 June 1997. Thanong: his replacement from the Thai Military Bank. Chuan Leekpai: Thai prime minister who renegotiated IMF conditions in January 1998.
- **Kang Kyong-shik versus Lim Chang-Ryul versus Kim Dae Jung.** Kang: Korean finance minister dismissed 19 Nov 1997. Lim: his successor who said no IMF help was needed. Kim Dae Jung: president-elect (first from the opposition) who said "our warehouse is empty".
- **Moody's versus Standard & Poor's.** Moody's cut Indonesia, Korea and Thailand to junk on 22 Dec 1997 (Malaysia cut but still investment grade); S&P *raised* Korea's rating and outlook to stable on 18 Feb 1998 while still junk.
- **Sanyo versus Yamaichi versus Japan Leasing.** Sanyo Securities: 3 Nov 1997, first securities bankruptcy since WWII. Yamaichi Securities: 24 Nov 1997, another top-10 brokerage. Japan Leasing: 27 Sep 1998, USD 16 billion, largest Japanese bankruptcy since WWII.
- **Hanbo Steel versus Kia Motors.** Hanbo collapsed in January 1997 under USD 6 billion of debt (first of the conglomerates); Kia, third-largest car maker, was the fourth of the top 30 to fall, 15 July 1997.
- **Peregrine versus Hang Seng.** Peregrine was the Hong Kong investment bank that failed (12 Jan 1998) on Indonesian loans; the Hang Seng fell 774 points, 8.7%, that day; the earlier 25% Hong Kong market fall was 23-28 Oct 1997.
- **Currency board versus peg versus float.** Indonesia proposed and then abandoned a currency board (Feb 1998) under IMF pressure; Hong Kong's currency board was defended by Zhu Rongji; the Tigers' pegs were abandoned for floats in July-October 1997; Malaysia re-pegged in September 1998.
- **Devaluation of the yuan.** China did *not* devalue (23 Dec 1997 and 19 Mar 1998 statements); the last major revaluation was 1994. Do not confuse with the Asian floats.
- **Default haircut 10 to 15% versus company value 70%.** The 10 to 15% is the share of liabilities typically forgiven when a company defaults; the 70% is the share of a company's value the professor attributes to its image.
- **Collection order.** Government first, banks second, employees usually third; shareholders are not first (a student's wrong answer) and suppliers come last.
- **Medium-high income threshold (USD 10,000 per capita, World Bank) versus 9% growth (developing Asia's 1992-95 average, case introduction).** Different metrics; the class rule is that a rich country growing 6 to 7% signals a bubble.
- **BRICs (slides) versus BRICS versus BRICS+.** The slides compare four BRICs (Brazil, Russia, India, China); BRICS added South Africa; BRICS+ now has 11 members including Saudi Arabia.
- **Asian Tigers on the slides.** The four are Hong Kong, Singapore, Taiwan and South Korea; Thailand, Malaysia, Indonesia and the Philippines are the crisis countries of the case. The professor used "Asian Tigers" loosely for all of them.
- **Jim O'Neill.** The professor credited him with naming the Asian Tigers; O'Neill of Goldman Sachs is the economist who coined "BRICs". If asked who coined BRICs, answer O'Neill.
- **Capital controls, Thailand versus Malaysia.** Thailand's were selective (14 May 1997: no baht sales to foreigners in the swap market, two-tier market until 30 Jan 1998); Malaysia's (Sept 1998) froze shares traded in Singapore and forced repatriation of offshore ringgit within a month.
