The 25 facts most likely to be tested

Dark-topped cards are Aguirre's case material, where the exam questions come from. Blue-topped cards are Neira's frameworks.
If you only have an hour: read this list twice, then do the practice exam at the bottom.
  1. AguirreInvestment grade is BBB- and above on the S&P scale; below is speculative grade. Use the local-currency, long-term rating. Germany AAA, Spain AA-, India and State Bank of India BBB, Société Générale A. S&P rated Lehman AAA shortly before it failed. S&P is free to use.
  2. AguirreFactoring sells your whole receivables book for liquidity up to 90 percent of invoice value. Discounting is bill by bill and always with recourse. Without recourse: the factor takes default risk and receivables leave the balance sheet. With recourse: unpaid invoices come back, so it is a loan. Cost about 1 to 2 percent. Only works with high-quality debtors. Plamar sold to PDVSA.
  3. AguirrePlamar needed about USD 300,000 for a plant making 300,000 handles and 300,000 seals a month. Balance sheet 31 Dec 2002: cash VEB 97.7m, receivables 211.5m, total assets 621.6m. At the deck's rate that is USD 70,361 cash, 152,313 receivables, 447,526 assets. Receivables are one third of assets. Ask the currency and the year before reading any balance sheet.
  4. AguirreWhy Guatemala: free trade agreement with Mexico and so NAFTA access to the US, low cost, close to Venezuela, stable currency, inflation about 2 percent, GDP per capita USD 3,646. Brazil dismissed: Lula just elected, too big for USD 240,000. Dominican Republic: devalued peso, inflation 8.6 percent. Plamar today operates in Guatemala, the Dominican Republic, Brazil and Costa Rica.
  5. AguirreAsian crisis response: raise interest rates and defend dollar pegs. Both backfired. Korea's interbank rate hit 30 percent; the Thai market fell 75 percent. Do not raise rates when the debt burden is already large.
  6. AguirreMalaysia was the only country in IMF history (since 1944) to reject IMF aid, in 1997. It used capital controls and spending cuts, helped by oil. Mahathir fired Anwar on 2 Sept 1998, a day after imposing the controls.
  7. AguirreOriginal sin: developing countries borrow in USD or EUR, not their own currency. A stronger dollar or higher US rates grow the debt automatically.
  8. AguirreTrigger of the Asian crisis was loss of confidence. First sign: 5 Feb 1997, Somprasong Land missed a USD 3.1m interest payment. Thailand floated the baht on 2 July 1997; it fell 15 percent at once.
  9. AguirreIMF packages: Thailand USD 16bn (Aug 1997, 17bn with China), Indonesia 23bn rising to 40bn (Oct 1997), Korea 55bn rising to 58bn (Dec 1997). The IMF lends, it does not donate, at rates below private banks but with austerity conditions.
  10. AguirreSociété Générale lost EUR 4.9bn on Kerviel's unauthorized trades, announced 24 Jan 2008, plus about 2bn on subprime. About EUR 50bn of positions unwound in three days; EUR 5.5bn capital increase. 74 alerts closed procedurally. He took 4 days of holiday in 2007. He came from the middle office and knew when controls ran.
  11. AguirreOther rogue traders: Nick Leeson, Barings 1995, GBP 827m (USD 1.3bn), a bank founded in 1762. Kweku Adoboli, UBS 2011, USD 2bn.
  12. AguirreThe exporter's lesson from SocGen: never speculate on currency. Close or hedge every open position with a forward. Too many controllers means nobody controls.
  13. AguirreForward formula: swap points = (SONIA minus EURIBOR) x days x spot / (36,000 + EURIBOR x days); forward = spot + swap points. Class example: spot EUR/GBP 0.8479, EURIBOR 3.013 percent, SONIA 3.732 percent, 180 days, forward about 0.8509. Equal rates give forward equal to spot. Banks earn the bid-ask spread.
  14. AguirreConvertible currency (UN, any one of): unlimited purchase of foreign currency by residents and non-residents; no central bank authorization; traded internationally and widely used. Hard: USD, EUR, GBP, JPY, CHF, CAD, AUD, NZD, SEK, DKK, NOK. Not fully convertible: CNY, INR, RUB, TRY. Non-convertible pairs route through USD, doubling fees and risk.
  15. AguirreTurkish lira: 1 USD = 9 TRY in 1966, 1,650,000 in 2001, six zeros dropped in 2005, 18.30 in Sept 2022, 41.82 in Oct 2025, 49.22 on 8 Oct 2026.
  16. AguirreICC contract, 8 points: parties, object, transport, terms of payment, guarantees of payment, courts, legislation, risks. Three transaction risks: transport, currency, credit. Term of payment is not a guarantee of payment. Letters of credit cover about 44 percent of international trade.
  17. AguirreBasel capital ratio 8 percent of risk-weighted assets (Basel I, 1988). Basel II (June 2001) kept 8 percent, tied weights to borrower quality, three pillars: minimum capital, supervisory review, disclosure. Basel III added liquidity buffers; Basel IV is current. Schröder: "not acceptable for Germany" (Oct 2001). July 2002 fix: loans under EUR 1m as retail, EUR 1 to 50m turnover lower weights, no long-term surcharge up to EUR 500m, deadline Jan 2007.
  18. AguirreMittelstand: up to 499 employees and EUR 50m revenue (EU SME definition is 250). 3.6m firms, 68 percent of workers, 80 percent of apprentices. Equity below 20 percent for small firms, the Achilles' heel. Hausbank relationship banking versus Anglo-Saxon capital-market banking.
  19. AguirreGerman banking pillars (case): three. Public (Landesbanken and Sparkassen), cooperatives, private commercial. Aguirre counted four. Loan margins about 1 percent versus 2 to 3.5 in Europe and 4 in the US. State guarantees phased out after 2005.
  20. AguirreNegative working capital is normal for a supermarket and a red flag for a carmaker like Porsche. Main reason firms default. Creditor order in liquidation: government, banks, employees, suppliers.
  21. NeiraCAGE (Ghemawat): Cultural, Administrative, Geographic, Economic. Frankel and Rose: language +200 percent, trading bloc +330, currency +340, colony-colonizer +900, border +80, ocean access +50; 1 percent more distance cuts trade 1.1 percent. Star TV lost about USD 500m. Tricon's Mexico moved from 16th to 2nd.
  22. NeiraMarket selection funnel: PESTEL, then CAGE, then Porter plus detailed market analysis, then the competitive advantage equation (company resources and capabilities matched with country specifics and industry success factors; all three must align). Goals must be SMART.
  23. NeiraReadiness is three home-country analyses: external (five forces), internal (generic strategies, Ansoff), resources (RBV, VRIO). Internationalization is Ansoff's market development. Expect 6 to 18 months of ramp-up.
  24. NeiraEntry ladder, low to high commitment: indirect export, direct export, contractual agreements, FDI. Franchising is the fastest. Alliances fail about 60 percent of the time, mainly through poor partner choice and lack of trust. Sogo shoshas handle 50 percent of Japan's exports and 75 percent of imports.
  25. NeiraZara: 10 to 15 days sketch to shipment, about 12,000 designs a year, stores order twice a week, replenishment 2.8 percent, 17 visits a year versus 4.1, advertising 0.3 percent versus 3.5, prices 15 percent lower, over 60 percent made near Spain, gross margin 57.4 versus 38.3, FY2017 revenue EUR 25.3bn, 7,475 stores, 96 markets, 70.1 percent of Inditex EBIT.
02

Neira's half: the strategy of going abroad

A five-step arc: global trends, benefits and reasons, are we ready, where, how. The decks are numbered S6 "Ready" and S7 "Where", but he taught Where on 28 Sep before Ready on 2 Oct.

Global trends Sessions 1-2, 5-6

  • About half of today's Fortune 500 will be gone in 7 years; almost 90 percent of the 1955 list is gone.
  • Three drivers of disruption: technology (enabler), demographics (silent force), globalization (amplifier). Combinatory technology stacks open tools (Amazon Go). Tesla analyzes about 15 percent of the data it captures.
  • Cities generate 80 to 85 percent of GDP; Asia has over half of the 32 megacities; 41 by 2030.
  • Price drives 60 percent of international purchasing decisions (McKinsey); Chinese car brands took about 15 percent of Spain's market in 5 years.
  • Asset-light platforms expand faster: Alibaba owns no warehouses, Airbnb no hotels, Uber no taxis.
  • Distance still matters online: only 12 percent of internet users buy cross-border; e-commerce cross-border use is 11 to 15 percent versus about 50 percent for Facebook.
  • Growth is shifting to Southeast Asia, Africa, China and India. Incumbents fail through cultural resistance, not missing data (Kodak, Blockbuster, Nokia).

Ready to internationalize? Session 6

Strategy is not a goal ("be number 1"), not a single action ("merge"), and not vision or values. It is how you compete and what advantage you base it on. Readiness is three analyses done at home.

1. External: Porter's five forces

Identify the industry, identify the players, analyze each force in order, and ask of each whether it is strong enough to erase industry profit. Entry barriers: capital (Boeing and Airbus), scale (the A380 needed about 400 units to break even), absolute cost advantage (Aramco, Intel), differentiation (cosmetics high, low-cost airlines low), distribution access (Mercadona shelves), legal barriers (traffic rights), retaliation. Buyer power rises with concentration, information (Booking, Kayak) and vertical integration (Zara makes its own clothes). Class addition: define industry boundaries first; disruption is an extra angle.

2. Internal: generic strategies and Ansoff

Generic strategies: cost leadership (Walmart), differentiation (Apple), cost focus (Lidl, Aldi), differentiation focus (Ferrari, Porsche, Tesla). Ansoff has two levers only, what is sold and to whom.

3. Resources and capabilities: RBV and VRIO

Look inside for advantage. Tangible assets can be bought, so they confer little. Intangible assets (brand, know-how, culture) are heterogeneous and immobile, so they sustain advantage. Test each with VRIO (Barney):

Not valuableCompetitive disadvantage
Valuable, not rareCompetitive parity
Valuable and rare, but easy to imitateTemporary advantage
Valuable, rare, costly to imitate, not organized to capture valueTemporary or unexploited
All fourSustained competitive advantage

Costly to imitate comes from time, causal ambiguity and social complexity. Organization means systems, processes, structure and culture. Class addition on funding realism: plan 6 to 18 months of ramp-up before profit and hold cash for capex and working capital. "Spreadsheets can say anything, reality cannot."

Where? International market selection Session 7

Process: strategic analysis, set main goals (SMART and a little flexible), select potential markets, set country-specific goals, entry strategy, implementation. Ghemawat's warning opens the deck: companies routinely exaggerate the attractiveness of foreign markets. Górecka: selecting foreign markets is one of the most critical decisions in entry strategy.

Preliminary screening · macro view with PESTEL · about 200 countries down to 8 to 12
Second screening · the distance approach, CAGE · can we replicate the business there?
In-depth screening · Porter's five forces in 2 to 3 countries plus detailed market analysis (size, product acceptance, pricing, distribution, promotion)
Final selection · the competitive advantage equation: company resources and capabilities matched with country specifics and industry success factors. All three must align; one strength cannot cover a weakness.

Cost of entry, resource constraints and the main goals are weighed at every stage.

Country portfolio analysis is flawed. It plots per-capita income against penetration with bubbles for market size, so it counts only potential sales and ignores the cost of distance. Tricon (Pizza Hut, Taco Bell, KFC) ranked Mexico 16th of 20 on CPA; adjusting for distance from Dallas, a shared border and a trade agreement moved it to 2nd. Star TV assumed Asian elites wanted English programming and that satellite beat geography; it lost about USD 500m and triggered a Chinese ban after Murdoch called satellite TV a threat to totalitarian regimes. A survey of multinationals in China: 54 percent did worse than planned, 62 percent had overestimated market potential.

03

Aguirre's half: the risks of being abroad

Aguirre works at Coface, a trade credit insurer in over 60 countries, and has taught at IE since 1998. His sessions move from political and macro risk to credit crises, currency, ratings and banking, and trade finance. His exam questions come from the four Harvard and IE cases.

Managing risk and the shifting wealth of nations Sessions 3-4

  • Allies become enemies. Iran and Iraq swapped roles between 1979 (embassy seized, hostages 444 days), the 1980s, 1990 (Kuwait), 2003 and 2025. Political risk is not only emerging markets: Iberdrola's 80-turbine US project cancelled in Dec 2025; Naturgy expropriated in Colombia.
  • Developed versus developing threshold about USD 10,000 GDP per capita. China and Japan are the world's biggest lenders. Japan: debt over 220 percent of GDP held at home, first rate rise in 35 years in 2025.
  • Four risks on the deck: emerging-market capital flows and foreign-currency debt (original sin), commodity prices, debt, freight costs.
  • Strait of Hormuz: about 20m barrels a day transit; bypass pipelines carry only about 3.5m; about 27 percent of maritime trade; a third of global fertilizer and about 35 percent of aluminum made nearby. Malacca is the biggest strait by volume, Hormuz second.
  • Macro toolkit: GDP = C + I + G + NX; balance of payments = current, capital and financial accounts; PESTEL; PMI surveys about 50,000 purchasing managers; 2 percent inflation is healthy, Turkey at 30 to 40 percent is not; about 80 percent of loans are variable rate; exports are untaxed for the exporter everywhere except Argentina.
  • Mankiw's ten principles, summarized on the deck as: trade can make everyone better off, and living standards depend on productivity. Corporates are read by balance sheet and P&L, countries by balance of payments plus GDP.
  • Numbers to recognize: Asia-Pacific about 78 percent of world GDP growth 2016 to 2026; China, Germany and the US are the three biggest exporters; 1.52bn tourist arrivals in 2025, Spain 96.8m; Spain built 700,000 flats in 2007 and 70,000 in 2008 (non-productive investment versus Korea's R&D and education).

The ICC international contract

Eight points: parties (often including distributors), object, transport, terms of payment, guarantees of payment, courts, legislation, risks. The three transaction risks are transport, currency and credit. Use the destination country's courts; London arbitration only for big claims. Pro forma invoices need buyer approval. Cuba's average payment term is 360 days. Letters of credit, governed by the ICC, cover about 44 percent of corporate international trade. Reading: Peter Bernstein, Against the Gods.

High rates as medicine (deck): Korea's 30 percent interbank rate caused bankruptcies, destroyed confidence and accelerated outflows until the IMF relented; Thailand starved firms of cash, the market fell 75 percent and 600,000 foreign workers went home; Indonesia saw defaults, inflation, riots and Suharto's exit; Malaysia rejected the IMF, pegged deposit rates to leading banks and suffered least.

Asian Tigers versus BRICS: the Tigers (Hong Kong, Singapore, Taiwan, Korea) were small, export-led after import substitution, US-dependent, with GNI per capita of about USD 8,000 to 23,000 in 1993 to 1995. The BRICs are large and diversified (Brazil cars and minerals, Russia oil and arms, India IT, China manufacturing). In 2008 Singapore and Taiwan were unscathed, Korea had NPLs and a crash, Hong Kong speculative attacks; BRIC markets fell then recovered from 2009. Class view: a simultaneous BRICS default is unlikely; risk is country by country. Sovereign debt treatments: Argentina-Russia (default, credit crunch), Turkey-Brazil (long fiscal adjustment, late public payments), India-Lebanon (banks hold the debt, crowding out).

Currency risk Session 11

Floating currencies are set by supply and demand, more efficient and more volatile. Fixed or pegged currencies need reserves covering the currency in circulation; pegs to the dollar have on average ended badly because only the Fed prints dollars. Convertibility is the ease of buying, selling or exchanging a currency (UN criteria in Top 25). There are 182 official currencies for 193 UN members. A currency future is a standardized exchange-traded contract, usually against USD in units like EUR 125,000, mostly closed before delivery. A forward is the over-the-counter bank hedge. Rising interest rates attract savings and appreciate the currency. The Big Mac Index is purchasing power parity shorthand. S&P, Moody's and Fitch remain the only credible rating agencies.

Credit ratings and German banking Session 12

Investment grade is BBB- and above. In 2012 CaixaBank, Sabadell and Bankinter fell below it when Spain neared default; Santander and BBVA kept it through global diversification. Public banks carry their sovereign's rating. The German case then asks whether a century-old relationship-banking system had to change.

  • Downturn 2002: every big commercial bank except Deutsche posted losses; Ackermann floated a state bad bank; Schröder promised a Mittelstand bank. Closing line: banks lend you an umbrella when it is sunny and take it back when it rains.

Trade finance and factoring Session 14

Factoring gives liquidity up to 90 percent of invoice value, debtor risk cover up to 100 percent, off-balance-sheet treatment and better ratios, and professional collections. The factor sets a credit limit per debtor, buys the guaranteed invoices, withholds a reserve for credit notes and pays the fundable amount up to the client's funding limit. Three misconceptions on the deck: it is too expensive (cheaper than running collections yourself), only weak companies use it (now a mainstream product), you lose control of clients (the relationship is professionalized). It ties you to one bank. Listed corporates factor at quarter-end to dress ratios; fintechs offer non-recourse lines of USD 100 to 200m with 25 staff; Coface insures the banks that buy receivables. Cash conversion cycle: cut DSO, trim idle inventory, stretch DPO; permanent current assets need long-term funding.

People. Francisco Martínez Sr. (born 1949 in Linares, chemical engineer from Barcelona Polytechnic) first went to Venezuela in 1969 for a PVC maker, consulted from 1976, founded a recycling and seals business in 1978 that reached 70 percent of the market, sold up in 1989, went bankrupt with a bag factory in Granada in 1991 and returned to Venezuela with an investor. Sons: Francisco Jr. runs administration and operations, Carlos sales and marketing (finishing an MBA in Madrid), Javier technology.

Business. Started in cleaning products, switched to plastics with a garage extruder repaired with a scrapyard gearbox. Packaging seals for paint containers (the only local rival closed). Metal handles from 1997 after Vicson refused wire and Galtreca, 600 km away, taught them the process; two Brazilian automatic machines cut staff from 18 to 6 and output rose from 50,000 to 300,000 a month. Industrial bags (35 plus competitors, PDVSA the only LDPE supplier) and shrink-wrap were added to smooth seasonality. Sales are personal relationships.

Money. No dividends for four years; every bank refused credit for lack of guarantees; suppliers and cash flow financed everything. 2002: domestic sales VEB 331m, exports 134m (handles 68m, seals 66m), net earnings 17.8m. Exports began opportunistically through a Venezuelan coatings exporter whose Dominican clients bought handles from the US and seals from Argentina; then Guatemala, then Bolivia by air.

Venezuela 2003. GDP expected to fall 12 percent; 60 percent of manufacturers closing; only USD 100m of foreign exchange authorized since February, three days of normal business; oil 50 percent of government revenue, 80 percent of exports, 25 percent of GDP. 2002: GDP minus 8.9 percent, bolívar down over 150 percent, unemployment 18 percent, inflation 22.4 percent. Today: about 7m emigrants, hyperinflation above 700 percent, Dutch disease, price controls.

The four options. Father: invest at home in bags and shrink-wrap or buy distressed rivals, or wait, worst case divest and return to Spain. Francisco Jr.: export harder; handles and seals earn the best margins; US and Argentine rivals ignore small markets; export know-how is an asset. Carlos: a plant in a neighbor country where over 10 prospects want reliable supply; cuts country risk, gives hard currency and bloc access; costs USD 300,000. Class answer: Guatemala.

Bank. Founded 1864, nationalized 1945, privatized 1987. Daniel Bouton CEO from 1993, chairman 1997, ENA graduate. SGCIB returned 48 percent on equity in 2006 against 26 percent for the group; equity derivatives were about 20 percent of 2007 profit, profitable every quarter since 1993, Equity Derivatives House of the Year in Jan 2008, 18.4 percent of the warrants market. A "soldier-monk" culture of engineers encouraged to take proprietary risk.

Desk and man. Delta One (products that move one for one with the underlying), head Martial Rouyère; Listed Products desk head Eric Cordelle from April 2007, a structurer who had never managed traders. Kerviel joined in 2000 in the middle office on about EUR 32,000, became a trading assistant in 2002 and a trader in 2005 on turbo warrants (knock-out options). No Grandes Écoles degree; felt an outsider. Salary EUR 48,000, bonus EUR 60,000 for 2006, asked EUR 600,000 for 2007. Reported EUR 25m proprietary earnings in 2007 (59 percent of the desk) against a true EUR 1.5bn; called the "cash machine".

Concealment. Fictitious offsetting trades cancelled before monthly checks; pairs offset in quantity but not price (SolarWorld 2,266,500 shares at 63 and 53, a fake EUR 22.7m loss); fraudulent provision flows; technical and internal counterparties (ClickOptions); deferred start dates; seven forged emails; other people's computers; a possibly complicit assistant who booked 15 percent of the fake trades.

Control failures. His supervisor resigned in Jan 2007 and nobody monitored the desk for two and a half months; Kerviel validated his own March earnings. Cordelle managed on trust; Rouyère gave no guidance. 74 alerts were handled by procedure. Eurex emailed in Oct 2007 about 6,000 contracts in two hours and wrote twice in Nov; compliance answered. The two-week vacation rule was waived after his father died. Ernst & Young and Deloitte reported no concerns while Delta One volumes doubled. Gross positions had no limit.

Discovery. A Basel II Cooke ratio anomaly on eight forwards; the claimed counterparty Deutsche Bank had no record. Summoned on Saturday 19 Jan 2008, he told Jean-Pierre Mustier he had found a "martingale". Maxime Kahn closed about EUR 50bn in three days with AMF permission to delay disclosure. On 24 Jan 2008: EUR 4.9bn plus about 2bn subprime; 2007 profit fell from EUR 5.2bn to 600 to 800m. Kerviel claimed tacit approval because his profits were impossible within his mandate. The French public sided with him.

Story. Amancio Ortega started dressmaking in 1963 aged 16, opened the first Zara in La Coruña (246,000 people) in 1975, 1,000 stores in 17 years, IPO 23 May 2001. Sales grew from EUR 5.67bn in 2004 to 23.34bn in 2017; gross margin 57.4 percent versus an industry 38.3; 593 stores in China by 2014; a USD 280m SoHo store; stores revamped every four to five years; 0.62 net openings a day. Clothes cost about 20 percent more to make than rivals' offshore product and are priced about 15 percent lower.

Model. Link design to manufacturing and distribution for rapid response, with decentralized decisions. Three levels of "commercials": design teams of four (two product managers, two designers) with authority over design, fabric, quantities and price; regional commercials linking stores to La Coruña; store managers counting stock by hand and ordering twice a week. Small batches test demand; scarcity makes customers buy now. Everything ships from one center fed by 11 factories over 124 miles of underground monorail and reaches stores in two days. CEO Isla rejects "fast fashion": the point is accuracy, store by store, week by week.

Class VRIO. The automated distribution network (rare, capital-intensive, built since 1975), the three-tier commercial system (culturally embedded, decades of subcontractor relationships), and the vertically integrated supply chain in Spain, Morocco and Portugal all pass valuable, rare and costly to imitate, and the single centralized system organizes them, so the advantage is sustained. The industry's key risk is the fashion miss; Zara reacts instead of predicting. Traditional and Zara-style models can coexist. Competitors: Uniqlo (3,445 stores, "a technology company"), Boohoo (GBP 580m sales, 100 new products a day), Gap (USD 673m advertising, 4.2 percent of sales).

Distance Still Matters

Ghemawat · HBR Sept 2001 · Session 7
Full treatment in Neira's chapter above.

Country portfolio analysis counts potential sales and ignores the cost of distance. Distance has four dimensions and each hits industries differently (electricity is sensitive to administrative and geographic distance but not cultural; TV is sensitive to cultural distance; "TV is hardly cement"). The gravity model explains up to two thirds of trade variation. Company features change distance: cosmopolitan managers shrink cultural distance; Tricon already owned four fifths of its Mexican outlets with a 38 percent share. Focused Maytag outperformed far-flung Electrolux and Whirlpool.
