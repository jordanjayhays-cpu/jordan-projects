# The Banality of Evil

Hannah Arendt went to Jerusalem in 1961 expecting a monster.

Adolf Eichmann had organised the transports. The trains, the schedules, the logistics of millions of deaths. She went to look evil in the face.

What she found was a man worried about his pension.

Not a fanatic. Not a sadist. A mediocre bureaucrat who was proud of being efficient, who spoke in clichés, and who could not tell his story without lapsing into the language of the office.

He had not hated anyone in particular. He had followed procedure. He had wanted a promotion.

Arendt was attacked for writing this, and people still misread it. She was not excusing him. She was saying something far more frightening.

That the worst things in history do not require monsters.

They require ordinary people who stop thinking — who let the form, the rule, the chain of command do the thinking for them. Who never ask what the job actually is.

Monsters are a comfort. If evil needs monsters, you're safe, because you're not one.

But if it only needs someone who doesn't examine what they're part of, then it needs someone exactly like you on an ordinary Tuesday.

Evil doesn't announce itself.

It files paperwork.
