You have sung this song a hundred times. Probably in a car, probably badly, probably before you were old enough to read.

*Row, row, row your boat, gently down the stream.*
*Merrily, merrily, merrily, merrily, life is but a dream.*

Nobody ever stops on that last line. It's a nursery rhyme. You sing it to toddlers. And somewhere in there, between the rowing and the merrily, somebody slipped in one of the oldest ideas people have had about being alive, and we have been handing it to four year olds ever since without comment.

*Life is but a dream.*
*A profound little line.*

Gently down the stream is the other half of it. Not upstream. Not faster. The instruction is to stop fighting the current, and it arrives disguised as a song about a boat.

*Life isn't perfect, it's messy and unclear.*
*But find the joy and the now, dissolve your fear.*

I don't think whoever wrote it in 1852 was doing philosophy. That's what makes it good. The thought was so ordinary it could ride along inside a children's song for nearly two centuries and still be sitting there, fully intact, the next time anyone bothered to listen.

*What's real, what's not, that's the question.*
